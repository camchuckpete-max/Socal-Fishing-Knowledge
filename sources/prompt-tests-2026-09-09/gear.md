## 1. The gear class (what to ask for if you owned nothing)

**Rod — jig-stick / surface-iron class.** 8–10 ft, moderate/fast, rated ~25–60 lb, casts 2–4 oz irons ([`tackle/gear-classes.md`](tackle/gear-classes.md)). The application table in [`tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md`](tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md) says the same: *8–10 ft jig stick, moderate*. The yellowtail router narrows it to **9–10 ft** ([`species/yellowtail.md`](species/yellowtail.md)). The length is not vanity — **the distance IS the technique**, and a longer rod gives a longer pendulum arc between tip and jig, which is what buys the distance (`VpW91AKOFVQ`, Scotty/Brothers Sportfishing — registered, high confidence; [`techniques/surface-iron.md`](techniques/surface-iron.md)).

Alternate builds the corpus records, kept side by side rather than reconciled (all [`techniques/surface-iron.md`](techniques/surface-iron.md)):
- **10 ft rated 30–60 lb** (Phoenix Axis 1009 HJ example), casting weight **~1.8 to 2+ oz** (`A70kK2niu2Q`, `LTCFjqtSI8g`, `42A8Owhc8fw` — unregistered channel, medium confidence).
- **9 ft rated 30–80 lb** (Axis 909 HJ) — shorter/stouter, two stated jobs: fired-up **7–15 lb schoolies** (faster fight-unhook-recast cycle inside a short window) and **Baja fish around structure/kelp** you need to muscle off the reef (`LTCFjqtSI8g`).
- **8 ft, extra-heavy, rated 30–80 lb** — one angler's stated favorite *specifically for casting distance* (`u0scEBby7nA`, Cedros, medium confidence).
- A **light spinning bass rod** with fluoro leader was fished on big-grade Cedros yellows and described as "still quite violent" — a genuine outlier build, not the doctrine (`ILBl12Jm7-0`, medium confidence).
- Counterpoint on length: one registered voice caps casting rods at **9 ft**, saying longer is dangerous to cast from anywhere but the bow of a crowded boat (Cesar, `F7jLrt2j2X8`; [`tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md`](tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md)). That is a crowded-rail argument, not a private-boat one.

**Reel — narrow high-speed conventional, thumb-controlled** (Tranx-500 class, ~**42 in per crank** on an HG spool; that range covers a slow grind up to yellowtail speed just by changing cadence) ([`techniques/surface-iron.md`](techniques/surface-iron.md)). **Star drag is enough** — this is not flylined bait; no lever drag, no two-speed needed, **20–25 size, 30 max**, because a bigger/taller reel is harder to cast (Cesar, `F7jLrt2j2X8`). A high-speed reel must be cranked *deliberately slower* to keep the iron kicking; older 4:1–5:1 ratios do that for you (Dave Hansen, `3dVc-2rsYII` — registered, high confidence).

**Line — the KB does not give one number; three notes give three brackets, kept side by side:**

| Source note | Braid for a jig stick |
| --- | --- |
| [`techniques/surface-iron.md`](techniques/surface-iron.md) gear-class detail | **~80 lb** braid main |
| [`tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md`](tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md) application table | **40–65 lb** braid |
| [`tackle/line-and-leader.md`](tackle/line-and-leader.md) (Academy Ep16, `aXF0bxAFtU0`) | fish the lightest the job allows — mostly **30 or 50 lb**, **up to 65 on a jig stick** |

The tie-breaker in the notes is the iron, not the fish: **heavy line kills a light iron.** You can't throw an AA-light class jig on an 80 lb jig stick; the downsized build is a **medium-action 9 ft graphite-composite stick, smaller narrow reel, ~50 lb braid, short fluoro leader** (`pcwcRdmWmLc`). Same mechanism in the lure note — an **A1-light is capped at ~30 lb test** (heavier line kills distance and planes the jig up), while a **40-light handles 40–50 lb** fine (`EmZO8QiOfik`; [`lures/iron-jigs.md`](lures/iron-jigs.md)).

**Top shot / leader — two complete systems. Pick one; don't hybridize inside casting range:**
- **Short system:** **50 lb mono or fluoro, ~18 in**, to a **100 lb clip** tied straight to the iron (the clip is what lets you swap irons without re-tying) — [`techniques/surface-iron.md`](techniques/surface-iron.md).
- **Long mono top shot:** **80–100 yd of 40–50 lb mono** straight to the jig over 50–80 lb braid backing, sized **by location**: **40 lb** for day-range islands (Coronados, Catalina, Channel Islands), **50 lb** for overnight-range (San Clemente, Santa Barbara Island) **and anywhere in Baja**, because those spots carry a real shot at a trophy fish at any moment (`A70kK2niu2Q`, `LTCFjqtSI8g` — medium confidence).

Whichever you run: **the braid-to-mono knot must never clear the spool on a full cast.** A knot dragged through the guides hangs up, backlashes, and zero-stretch braid then snaps the leader and loses the jig **even at 40 lb** (`aXF0bxAFtU0`). The same failure produced a **thumb slice** on a Cedros trip when a surface-iron cast ran a mono-to-fluoro knot through the caster's hand (`ILBl12Jm7-0`). Stated fix: single material within casting range, or a top shot long enough that the knot never reaches the guides ([`tackle/line-and-leader.md`](tackle/line-and-leader.md)).

Two more line details specific to irons:
- **Run the thinner/lighter end of your top-shot range on irons** — a thick top shot dampens the swim; stickbaits and poppers tolerate heavy top shots, an iron doesn't (Cesar, `F7jLrt2j2X8`).
- **Braid texture near kelp:** a rough, high-tooth (solid/low-carrier) braid saws through kelp stringers when a yellow buries you; smooth braid just slides. [`tackle/line-and-leader.md`](tackle/line-and-leader.md) attributes the rough-braid-near-kelp preference to Landesfeind, who likes it on surface iron specifically.
- **Material, attributed:** one Cedros angler runs **straight mono for surface iron, fluoro for everything else on the trip**, no reason stated in source (`WE643Fue1_A`, medium confidence).

**Drag:** set to roughly **a quarter to a third of your lowest line-class rating**, starting at the light end and bumping one click at a time if you're in a stalemate (`VpW91AKOFVQ`; [`tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md`](tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md)).

**Regional line-class floors if you take this south** ([`tackle/line-and-leader.md`](tackle/line-and-leader.md)): Cedros = **40 lb floor, 50 lb covers most everything** (short bait leaders and surface iron), 80 lb carried just-in-case (`WE643Fue1_A`); a second Cedros operator fielded **80–100 lb as standard** over 28–30 ft rock (`QSmE3mdEL28`). Two operators' independent answers to the same risk — not reconciled in the KB.

**Decision frame** (the notes don't rank these, so here's what the choice actually turns on): if your limiting factor is **reaching fish that won't come to the boat**, take the 10 ft. If your limiting factor is **swing room where you're standing**, take 9 ft. That's a platform question, not a technique question — see §3.

Blank material — two jobs, not one best pick (Billy, Fisherman's Landing, via `sjlL5GidM58`, in `techniques/surface-iron.md`): **all-fiberglass loads more easily and is the stated go-to for surface iron**; a **composite** (graphite/glass) blank is stiffer and is the better tool for working poppers and stickbaits. Dan Wade's material nuance (`yn1suHH6b1Y`): **S-glass does what E-glass does at ~20% less weight** — matters over 300 casts in a day — and has more backbone ("denim versus polyester"). That was said on a rod manufacturer's stage, so the note treats the mechanism as doctrine and the brand framing as promotional.

### Reel — **mid-size star-drag conventional**

- **Star drag is the standard**, and specifically because it casts: *"the star drags definitely cast better than the lever drags"* (Steve Carson, `Y_ElKixrhsc`). Kearn (`3g82igEL8yk`) names a **Torium 20** as his own example of the class, plus "any manufacturer that makes a nice star drag reel of this size"; newer casting lever-drags are "becoming viable," mostly for tuna.
- **Size ceiling**: 20–25 size, **30 max** — a bigger/taller reel and a lever drag's heavier spool are harder to cast (Cesar, `F7jLrt2j2X8`).
- **Gear ratio is the trap.** Design intent for these jigs was the **Penn Jigmaster at 4:1**; *"some of these reels that are six and a half, seven and a half to one — they're too fast"* (Dan Wade, `Ec2GaNpiOHI`). Kept side by side, not reconciled: a **7:1** baitcaster framed as a torque/versatility middle ground for big yellows (`pOFsjDTqaxY`, `tackle/tackle-express-baitcaster-gear-ratio-yellowtail.md`). The reconciling mechanic is cadence, not hardware — **match crank speed to the ratio; a high-speed reel has to be cranked deliberately slower** (Dave Hansen, `3dVc-2rsYII`), and **reeling too fast is called out as the single most common mistake** on this technique.
- **Modern alternate build**, kept side by side: a saltwater **low-profile baitcast level-wind** (his example a Penn Fathom 400) on the same 9–10 ft stick, reached for specifically with **smaller/mid jigs** (Carson, `Y_ElKixrhsc`). Kearn names the **Tranx** as a good casting reel for its **adjustable brakes** — less backlash while still letting the jig get out.

Rules that apply to all of them:

- **On irons, run the lighter/thinner end of your top-shot range** — a thick top shot dampens the iron's swimming action, in a way stickbaits and poppers don't suffer (Cesar, `F7jLrt2j2X8`).
- **Braid: fish the lightest the job allows — up to 65 lb on a jig stick** (Academy Ep16, `aXF0bxAFtU0`, `tackle/line-and-leader.md`).
- **The connection knot must never clear the guides on a full cast.** Either run the top shot long (100 yd) or short enough it never reaches the tip; a knot dragged through the guides hangs up, backlashes, and braid's zero stretch then snaps the leader and loses the jig even at 40 lb (`aXF0bxAFtU0`; restated as a standing rule on the Searcher, `fyJA3o2hVh0`). Real-world cost: a Cedros angler cast a surface iron past his mono-to-fluoro knot and took *"a pretty good slice in the thumb"* (`ILBl12Jm7-0`).
- **Mono vs braid is a casting-latitude call, not a swim call.** Dan Wade fishes 40 lb mono, 125–150 yd: *"On straight spectra you need to make very deliberate casts. You can't just see a spot of fish over in the other corner and make that cast — it's a backlash."* On whether braid kills the action: *"A good swimming lure is going to swim well no matter what."* (`Ec2GaNpiOHI`). One Baja/Cedros angler runs straight mono for surface iron only and fluoro for everything else on the trip, no reason stated (`WE643Fue1_A`).
- The `techniques/surface-iron.md` class bullet gives a different terminal system, kept side by side: **~80 lb braid main → 50 lb mono/fluoro leader, ~18 in → a 100 lb clip** tied straight to the iron so you can swap irons without re-tying.

### Iron + hook

## 1. In gear-class terms

### Rod — **jig-stick class**

- **Lexicon spec:** 8–10 ft, casts **2–4 oz** irons, moderate/fast, **~25–60 lb** (`tackle/gear-classes.md`).
- **The technique note is stricter and gives the reason:** *"An 8-ft jig stick is a yo-yo rod — it's not a jig stick. **9-ft is okay. 10-ft is better**"* (`yn1suHH6b1Y`, `techniques/surface-iron.md`). Independent second statement of the same floor: **no less than 9 ft to be "in the game," 10 ft preferred**, because fish don't always come close enough to reach on a shorter rod (`3g82igEL8yk`, `tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md`).
- **Counter-parameters the KB keeps side by side:** a **9 ft cap for casting safety** when you fish the back or middle of a boat rather than the bow (`F7jLrt2j2X8`); and one source who started on a full 10 ft conventional found it "too much" and dropped to 9 ft (`IhBY5RHFvuk`). ### Reel — **mid-size star-drag conventional**, or a low-profile baitcaster

- **Standard:** a **Torium 20-class star drag**, or any manufacturer's equivalent mid-size star drag (`3g82igEL8yk`). Star drag free-spools more cleanly and **casts better**; lever drag wins only once the priority shifts to fighting (`tYebwLzTyf8`, `tackle/star-drag-vs-lever-drag.md`).
- **Size: 20–25, a 30 max** — don't go bigger or taller, since a larger reel (and a lever drag's heavier spool) is harder to cast (`F7jLrt2j2X8`).
- **Ratio — genuinely contested, both sides held:** irons were designed around the **~4:1 Penn Jigmaster**, so **6.5–7.5:1 is "too fast"** (`Ec2GaNpiOHI`); against that, **7:1** is pitched as the torque-vs-speed middle ground for big yellowtail across both iron and yo-yo (`pOFsjDTqaxY`, `tackle/tackle-express-baitcaster-gear-ratio-yellowtail.md`). The KB's decision frame: **what matters is line speed at the jig**, set by crank discipline and spool diameter as much as ratio — a slower reel does the slow-down for you, a faster reel has to be cranked deliberately slower.
- **Baitcaster route:** **Daiwa Lexa / Penn Fathom / Shimano Tranx** are the named yellowtail casting reels, with the explicit instruction to leave heavy tuna gear home (`QEmxUIGmKbo`). Stated advantages over a conventional like a Torium: the **level wind removes line management**, and the **external brake dial + spool tensioner let you re-dial per cast when you swap lure weights** (`hAryw1v3I68`). Its stated **limit**: the light-baitcaster path is for **sub-2 oz, 5–6 in irons** — *"if you're fishing like a Tady 45 and up, I'd probably go with a different rod"* (`IhBY5RHFvuk`).

### Line — the KB carries four complete systems; pick one, don't blend

1. **Short leader + clip:** ~**80 lb braid** main → **50 lb mono/fluoro, ~18 in** → **100 lb clip** tied straight to the iron so you can swap jigs (`techniques/surface-iron.md`).
2. **Backing + long top shot:** **65 lb spectra** backing → **~100 yd of 40 lb mono** (step to 50 lb for bluefin) (`3g82igEL8yk`); or **50–60 lb (up to 80) braid** backing → **80–100 yd of 40–50 lb mono** straight to the iron, no leader (`A70kK2niu2Q`).
3. **Straight mono:** **40 lb, 125–150 yd** on a classic glass stick (`Ec2GaNpiOHI`); budget version runs **straight 40 lb mono, no spectra backing at all** (`49joKHD7Umc`).
4. **Baitcast version:** **50 lb braid** to the top of the spool + a **4–5 ft leader of 40 lb mono** (`Y_ElKixrhsc`).

**For yellowtail specifically** the species page calls **straight mono, 40–50 lb**, running the heavier end when the water allows because a trophy is possible at any moment (`species/yellowtail-surface-iron.md`).

Sizing rules that cut across all four:

- **Location sizing:** **40 lb** top shot for full-day-accessible islands (**Coronados, Catalina, Channel Islands**); **50 lb** for overnight-range (**San Clemente, Santa Barbara Island**) and **anywhere in Baja**, because those carry a true trophy shot at any moment (`LTCFjqtSI8g`).

Two operators' independent choices for the same risk, not a correction of each other.
- **Braid ceiling on a jig stick: up to 65 lb**, and fish the lightest braid the job allows (`aXF0bxAFtU0`, `tackle/line-and-leader.md`).

## Hook — style

**Circle**, and the mechanism is bluefin-specific: they have a lot of teeth and will **saw through the leader**, so a circle's corner-of-the-mouth hookset keeps the leader out of the teeth (Capt. Dave Hansen, `RbqOKkINSCM`; a slightly **offset** full circle described as seating the corner for the same reason, `RfiC8sfIWTk` — both in [`tackle/hooks.md`](tackle/hooks.md)). A sportboat captain's house rule states it flatly: **circle hooks always for bluefin, J hooks for yellowtail** (`UfuiWFVvz2E`). Physical corroboration: a landed 40 lb bluefin came aboard still carrying another angler's hook after biting through his line (`c3NFkQbdDy0`).

**Conflict, kept side by side** ([`tackle/hooks.md`](tackle/hooks.md)): asked circles vs. Js for a finicky bluefin bite on No. 4/No. 2 hooks, a Ranger 85 crew member stated a personal preference for **J hooks** — faster to pin a bait than a circle, and less time in your hand means a livelier bait and more bites (`LsFMBCa9DOQ`). Cesar makes the same mechanical point: at the smallest sizes a J's gap is noticeably bigger and much easier to pin a bait through than a small circle's tight gap (`48ZFXnCTTQE`). **What the choice turns on** — leader protection against bluefin teeth (circle) versus baiting speed and bait liveliness (J), plus whether you can be trusted to *not* swing: on a crowded rail or if you don't reliably feel the bite, circle. This is a genuine split in the corpus, not a ranking.

## Hook — wire and finish

- **Thin wire** is the live-bait default: it adds little weight to the bait's nose so the sardine swims naturally. Hansen picks **thin wire specifically for sardine** because a sardine, unlike a heartier mackerel, "has a hard time swimming" a standard-gauge hook around convincingly (`ftEvyfwjZFU`).
- **But not paper-thin at this line class.** The 20–30 lb-grade crew preferred a **stouter No. 4** over a light-wire one at 25–30 lb test (`LsFMBCa9DOQ`), and a Newport captain fishing 3–4 in anchovies for 40 lb-class fish on 30 lb leader argued for **thicker-shank hooks (Mustad, VMC named)** because thin wire didn't survive repeated heavy pulls (`_PGm-TlFU2A`, [`tackle/hooks.md`](tackle/hooks.md)).
- **Finish: bronze or black, never nickel-plated or chrome** for bait fishing — chrome reflects light underwater, bronze/black goes effectively invisible; bluefin are named as one of the pressured SoCal species this matters most for. Bronze/black also rusts out of a fish inside about a week after a break-off, where nickel lasts indefinitely (`IMnoZVEYpm4`, [`tackle/hooks.md`](tackle/hooks.md)).

## Leader — material, test, length

**Fluorocarbon.** It's called a must on finicky SoCal pelagics — "you won't get bit without it" on bluefin (`EyB98RuKfeQ`, [`tackle/line-and-leader.md`](tackle/line-and-leader.md)) — and Hansen runs **fluorocarbon throughout** on a bluefin flyline precisely because bluefin "have so many teeth" and will bite through anything lighter (`ftEvyfwjZFU`). One dissenting data point: on the June 2023 El Dorado trip fluoro was called optional — "if you don't have fluoro it's not a detrimental death sentence" (`oB4BpIUTTl4`).

**Test: 25–30 lb is the center of the corpus for this grade.**
- **20 lb fluorocarbon** on the day that actually graded 20–30 lb, ~25 lb average (`LsFMBCa9DOQ`).
- **25 lb** mono or fluoro to a #2 circle (`nQvJnfb5jQ4`); **25–30 lb fluorocarbon** called out on camera as "key, it's a must have these days," with a separate angler on the same trip calling 30 lb "smart" after a 15 lb leader failed on a prior trip (`_PGm-TlFU2A`).
- **25 lb vs 30 lb** framed as the real choice: **25 lb gets bit a little better, 30 lb gives the extra leverage** (`A70kK2niu2Q`), with the leader **matched within 5 lb of the main line** (`LTCFjqtSI8g`).
- A boat-skipper cap cited by Cesar for tough bites: **fly line at 20–25 lb test, no more than 30** (`48ZFXnCTTQE`).

**Length: 4–6 ft.** Functionally only ~3 ft is needed, but on light (25 lb) leader you cut and re-tie the hook after every fish to clear nicks, so the extra is buffer against a full leader replacement mid-trip (`LTCFjqtSI8g`, [`techniques/flyline.md`](techniques/flyline.md)). Alternatives in range: roughly rod length (7–8 ft) of 30 lb on a 30 lb setup, or 4–5 arm lengths of 25–30 lb on light flyline gear (`xPFm_ZV2PZU`, [`tackle/line-and-leader.md`](tackle/line-and-leader.md)). **Don't run it long** — a long leader puts the knot up past the tip guide where it hangs on the cast (`RbqOKkINSCM`).

**Connections:** hook straight to the fluoro with a **San Diego jam** — 6 wraps down the standing line (5 also fished, wrap count is preference), lubricate, slide the coils down tight to the eye before setting (`RbqOKkINSCM`, `ftEvyfwjZFU`, `1BH7nQdIg5Q`, `VpW91AKOFVQ`, [`rigging/essential-knots.md`](rigging/essential-knots.md)). On a hook with no solid ring, use the **loop knot** — overhand 6–8 in from the tag, back through the same way it exited, 4–6 wraps, lubricate with chapstick, not just spit (`48ZFXnCTTQE`). **Straighten the fluoro first:** it comes off the spool twisted — rub it warm between your fingers so the knot seats clean (`RbqOKkINSCM`). For the braid-to-top-shot join, [`tackle/line-and-leader.md`](tackle/line-and-leader.md) names the **FG knot** as the smoothest braid-to-leader connection when it must pass the guides ([`rigging/fg-and-albright.md`](rigging/fg-and-albright.md)), and the **RP knot** for braid-to-fluoro (6 up, 6 down).

**The bite-guard variant** — reach for it when the bite wants light line but there's real fish pressure: **25 lb monofilament main/top shot to 2–3 ft of 50 lb fluorocarbon**, joined with the **Seaguar knot** (`48ZFXnCTTQE`, [`techniques/flyline.md`](techniques/flyline.md), [`rigging/essential-knots.md`](rigging/essential-knots.md)). The mechanism: braid has no stretch and fluoro very little, so the shock of the bite lands entirely on the mono-to-fluoro connection — that's what parts light leaders, not the fluoro being "too light." The 25 lb mono is what pulls through the water (the bait swims like it's on 25 lb) while the short heavy section rides inside the mouth and along the teeth. Applied step-ups against 30+ lb tuna: **30 lb main → 40 lb leader** and **25 lb main → 40 lb leader**, which "dramatically helped" land fish.

## Main line

Braid backing → mono top shot → fluoro leader is the standard SoCal spool ([`tackle/line-and-leader.md`](tackle/line-and-leader.md)).

- **Top shot: 50+ yd of 30 lb mono, more if possible** — that's the light stick in a two-rod bluefin flyline system explicitly sized for **20–50 lb grade** fish (`jQW2HLkMsmY`). The updated version of the same build runs braid to **80–100 yd of 30 lb mono**, then 4–6 ft of 25 or 30 lb fluoro (`LTCFjqtSI8g`).
- **Why mono and not straight braid for flylining:** mono comes off the reel more easily and doesn't stick to itself the way braid does, which helps the bait swim naturally. Straight braid to fluoro maximizes capacity and is "totally fine," just less efficient for this specific job (`jQW2HLkMsmY`).
- **The depth reason to carry the long mono:** braid floats, mono sinks. At **200 ft back**, braid with a 10 ft fluoro top shot holds the bait around **10–12 ft down**; straight mono at the same distance puts it **50–60 ft down** — a large practical difference for bluefin that won't come to the surface (`48ZFXnCTTQE`). If the captain calls fish holding down, that's the rod you want.
- **Braid class:** fish the lightest braid the job allows — mostly **30 or 50 lb**, up to 65 on a jig stick (`aXF0bxAFtU0`). The "workhorse live bait" rung, spec'd for smaller yellowfin (15–25 lb) and **smaller bluefin**, runs **65 lb braid** on a 7 ft 30 lb-rated rod with a 10-size 2-speed, leader ≈ rod length of 30 lb to a 2/0 live-bait circle (`xPFm_ZV2PZU`, [`tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md`](tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md)). The "light flyline / paddy bait" rung below it is ~8 ft parabolic 20–40 lb, small conventional, 30 lb braid + light fluoro.
- **One hard rule:** make the mono top shot long enough that the **braid-to-mono knot never clears the spool on a full cast**. A knot dragged through the guides hangs up, backlashes, and braid's zero stretch then snaps your leader even at 40 lb (`aXF0bxAFtU0`) — and it can slice your thumb on the way out (`ILBl12Jm7-0`).

## Rod and reel

**Flyline / soft-rod class**: ~9 ft, MH, fast, ~30–65 lb — a tip soft enough to cast an unweighted bait without killing its swim ([`tackle/gear-classes.md`](tackle/gear-classes.md)). The build sized to your grade: **8 ft (or 8'6") rated 20–50 lb**, Talica 16/20-size reel (`jQW2HLkMsmY`, `LTCFjqtSI8g`). A lever drag that free-spools well by preference; a star drag (Torium 16-class) works too (`A70kK2niu2Q`).

**Blank material matters here:** a **graphite tip is too heavy/stiff to load on the cast** — the whip-crack release either gives the bait whiplash (it lands stunned and won't swim) or throws it clean off the hook. A slow-taper **glass or composite** rod loads gently so the bait lands lively; glass casts a bait best, composite is the easier all-rounder for fighting (`48ZFXnCTTQE`, [`techniques/flyline.md`](techniques/flyline.md)).

## How to rig the bait

**1. Pick the bait — it's ~90% of the game** ([`bait/fishing-live-bait.md`](bait/fishing-live-bait.md)).
Fish the **hardest bait to catch in the tank**: the lively **lime-green** one with **scales intact and no red/bloody nose** (`SluBXkT3cuw`, `raUPkuaFXpw`). Make that call **while the bait is still up and swimming** — bait piled flat in the net all reads silver from above (`6ueGWJek1gI`). Cull the bloody, scaly, lethargic ones into the chum bucket. Sardine is the stated bait pairing for tuna (`gKrYKvqHUjk`); match the hatch if the fish are keyed on something else (`mscHk0qiXnk`).

**2. Get it from tank to hook without killing it.** Lean the rod on the rail so both hands are free. **Net the bait, don't hand it** — carry it to the rod still in the net; lifting it out and holding it knocks scales off and starts killing it before it's hooked. Once hooked, get it back in the water fast rather than dangling it while you sort out the reel (`6ueGWJek1gI`).

**3. Hook position — pick the swim you want** ([`bait/fishing-live-bait.md`](bait/fishing-live-bait.md), [`techniques/flyline.md`](techniques/flyline.md)).

| Position | Where | What it does | When |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Nose** (default) | **Side to side** through the clear spot at the very **tip of the nose**, in front of the eyes — "the only way" to nose-hook a sardine (`ZggReeO1nyU`) | Swims naturally, stays up | The all-purpose flyline hook |
| **Butt** | Near the vent, under the two rear fins | Swims **down and away**, a bit deeper; **defeats sea lions** picking off nose-hooked baits | Fish holding below the chum line; pinnipeds working the boat. **Zero weight only** |
| **Shoulder / dorsal** | Through the shoulder ahead of the dorsal | Keeps your **line up out of the water**, bait high and skating | Reaction bite, stealthier line presentation |

Butt-hooking is the move when the fish won't come up: on the June 2023 El Dorado trip an angler butt-hooked specifically because the captain called bluefin **80–100 ft down and not coming to the chum line**, and credited that for the hookup (`oB4BpIUTTl4`/`r4J5nP5Bkl4`). Trade-off: **the butt hook draws blood, so that bait doesn't last as long** as a nose-hooked one (`ZggReeO1nyU`). Mechanism, for the finicky/tensioned case: a nose-hooked bait under any tension gets its **head dragged back toward the boat** — the opposite of what you want; a butt hook anchored **over the bone behind the fin, eye facing the tail**, makes it dive and flee harder the more you roll the reel (`48ZFXnCTTQE`).

**4. Pin the circle hook correctly.** Hold the hook **high on the arch and press straight down** into the bait. Holding it from the bottom the way you would a J leaves no gap and squeezes the bait into a "potato chip" (`8YvmROeVL-0`, `raUPkuaFXpw`).

**5. Fish it.** **No weight.** Cast **well away from the boat** — fish are finickier close to the hull — and the goal is to get your bait **beyond everybody else's**, into its own zone. Adding a split shot or slider to help the cast is framed bluntly as a crutch for not knowing how to cast, not a technique upgrade: **no weight, hook tied directly to the line** (`gKrYKvqHUjk`). On a butt- or belly-hooked bait, **keep tension the moment it lands** — a little pull makes it swim away like a propeller; give it slack and it turns and swims back under the boat (`8YvmROeVL-0`).

**6. Swap baits aggressively.** **60-second rule:** if the bait isn't pulling line off the reel shortly after it hits the water, wind it in, flick it off, grab a fresh one — "we're not marrying these baits, we're speed dating" (`SluBXkT3cuw`, `8YvmROeVL-0`). A Ranger 85 crew member reinforced exactly this for a finicky bluefin bite: "the guys that change baits often are the ones that are going to catch more fish" (`LsFMBCa9DOQ`). A stricter cadence — "change your bait every minute" — is logged beside it, not merged (`BdRX4b8Fo5w`).

**7. The bite.** **Set your drag before you fish** — stressed in a pre-dawn captain's briefing as "very, very important," so you're not fighting a fish with it unset (`V_ONnegk95M`, `M7BtON4GZgQ`). On a circle: **do not swing.** Give the fish a **10 count**, engage the reel, point the rod where the fish is going, and **wind tight** — it needs to turn away and eject the bait back into the corner of the mouth for the hook to find purchase (`48ZFXnCTTQE`, `raUPkuaFXpw`). If you've gone to a small J instead, a **3 count** is enough before putting it in gear.

**8. Re-tie.** On 25 lb leader, cut and re-tie the hook **after every fish** to clear nicks (`LTCFjqtSI8g`).

## The one conflict you should decide before you leave the dock

The KB does not settle this, and I won't pretend it does — section order here is not a ranking.

- **Don't scale down.** The bluefin router's gear summary puts bait/flyline at **40–60 lb class, circle hooks size 1–2/0**, and states explicitly that **bluefin gear doesn't get scaled down** on a mixed tuna trip because grade is unpredictable trip to trip — tackle stays sized for the biggest fish that could show up (`PrdPJy26H8c`). A Constitution captain's briefing set a **40 lb daytime flyline floor** for bluefin (`UfuiWFVvz2E`). And Tanner Bank crews describe **10 lb schoolies up to 80, "maybe even 100" lb** moving through together on one size line, calling that the core tackle challenge of the trip (`947solNfiPw`).
- **Downsize to get bit.** Against that, the finicky-bite school caps fly line at **20–25 lb, no more than 30**, on the mechanism that in warm water live bait hasn't had time to toughen up and won't swim convincingly on thick line (`48ZFXnCTTQE`). The supporting case: at Guadalupe a bite that produced on 50–60 lb went dead by mid-morning; downsizing to **30 lb** got a bite within two minutes and landed a **130 lb** fish in 45 minutes. A stated personal best on 25 lb main with a 50 lb bite guard is **~100 lb**.

**What it actually turns on:** whether the grade on your stop is genuinely capped at 20–30 lb or whether bigger fish are mixed into the same chum line, and how picky the bite is. If it's clean schoolie grade and they're finicky, **25–30 lb with a No. 2 circle** is the corpus's answer at your stated grade. If a 60–100 lb fish could eat the same bait, either step to **40 lb** or use one of the two hedges the KB documents:

1. **Two rods** — light stick 30 lb mono top shot for 20–50 lb grade, heavy stick running **40 lb (sometimes 50 lb)** fly line direct for bigger fish (`jQW2HLkMsmY`).
2. **The bite guard** — 25–30 lb mono to a short 40–50 lb fluoro section, so the bait swims like it's on light line while you can still lean on a big one (`48ZFXnCTTQE`).

The corpus also shows crews finding the answer empirically: one Baja crew fished **three rods rigged at 30, 40 and 25 lb at once** and watched which got bit, then leaned the spread that way — their own framing being that it **changes day to day** (`84XPJAeH0Rw`).

## Leader material and test — two competing doctrines, and what the choice turns on

**Option A — straight light fluoro, matched to the top shot.** 4–5 ft of **20 lb** (or 25 lb) fluorocarbon tied to the same-test mono top shot (`ptoIvB2MspE`, `dg1sbr6GuB8`). Deckhand working numbers from a SoCal boat put ~90% of daytime bites on a dedicated **25–30 lb fluorocarbon flyline stick** (Ray Sharifi, `149px8WQ2Ng`, in [`species/bluefin-tuna.md`](species/bluefin-tuna.md)). Fluoro is called a must on bluefin — "you won't get bit without it" ([`tackle/line-and-leader.md`](tackle/line-and-leader.md), `EyB98RuKfeQ`).

**Option B — light mono + a short heavy fluoro bite guard.** **25 lb mono** top shot to **50 lb fluorocarbon, 2–3 ft**, joined with a [Seaguar knot](rigging/seaguar-knot.md). The stated mechanism: braid has no stretch and fluoro very little, so bite/hookset shock lands entirely on the mono-to-fluoro connection — that's what parts light leaders, not the fluoro being "too light." The 25 lb mono is what actually pulls through the water (bait swims like it's on 25 lb) while the heavy short section rides along the teeth and gill plates. Applied trip numbers from the same source: **25 lb main → 40 lb leader** and **30 lb main → 40 lb leader**, with the heavier-leader step "dramatically" improving landing rate (Cesar / Ray Sharifi, `48ZFXnCTTQE`, in [`techniques/flyline.md`](techniques/flyline.md)).

**The decision frame** (section order here is not a ranking): pick A when the bite is the binding constraint and the grade really is capped around 30 lb — a matched-test leader is the least drag on the bait and is what the finesse rig was designed around. Pick B when you expect the same school to hold occasional 40–60 lb fish, or you've already been sawed off — the bite guard buys abrasion protection without changing what the bait feels. Note the two doctrines disagree about *why* light leaders break, and the KB does not resolve that.

**Leader length has its own unreconciled split.** Carson's rule is that the connecting knot **must stay outside the rod tip on the cast** — roughly 3 ft on a 7 ft rod, 4 ft on an 8 ft rod, up to 5 ft on a 9 ft rod, varying by casting style (`QHY5kmU7OTU`; same rule from Capt. Art in [`tackle/line-and-leader.md`](tackle/line-and-leader.md), `fyJA3o2hVh0`). Capt. Matt's 25 lb all-purpose flyline setup instead runs **15–25 ft of fluorocarbon** on 30 lb braid — his "happy medium," long enough the fish doesn't see the Spectra, short enough a flylined sardine isn't dragging a lot of thick line (`G1YIlakiRqg`). If you cast your baits, the knot-clears-the-tip rule is the one that governs; if you soak or lob them, the long-leader build is on the table.

Practical: **straighten the fluoro before tying** — it comes off the spool twisted; rub it between your fingers to take the kink out so the knot seats cleanly (Capt. Dave Hansen, `RbqOKkINSCM`).

## Hook — style, then size

### Style

**Circle is the bluefin default.** Reason stated repeatedly: bluefin have a lot of teeth (unlike yellowfin) and will **saw through the leader** — a big one chews through a leader "in 5 minutes, less than it takes to land one." The circle's corner-of-the-mouth hookset keeps the leader out of the teeth (Capt. Dave Hansen, `RbqOKkINSCM`, `high` confidence; Ray Sharifi, `RfiC8sfIWTk`, in [`tackle/hooks.md`](tackle/hooks.md)). A crew angler on a strictly-flyline bluefin day credited a No. 2 (or No. 4) circle with a **~90% corner-of-the-mouth hookup rate** that trip (`oB4BpIUTTl4` / `r4J5nP5Bkl4`, [`techniques/flyline.md`](techniques/flyline.md)).

**The J counter-case, kept side by side.** Carson's finesse rig actually runs a **J** — an **Owner Gorilla Light, size 2** — "a nice light hook that lets your bait swim" (`9JnIS8HkZlA`). And for light-line bluefin specifically, a small J goes on a lively bait *fast*, with no need to scale the bait first; a circle's smaller point-to-shank gap makes it trickier to thread — a baiting-speed argument, not a hookset one (Vince, `icpm7gADxvU`). What it turns on: teeth/leader protection versus baits-per-hour and how well the bait swims off a very light wire.

**Either way, don't swing.** On a circle, engage the reel, point the rod where the fish is going, and wind tight — it pins the corner on its own; a **solid three count**, then come tight. Even on a J, the beginner-safe version is raise the rod to about **45°** and wind, not an exaggerated hookset (`sWRSYCmt4Tw`, `raUPkuaFXpw`, `R48YdVFfEOI`, [`tackle/hooks.md`](tackle/hooks.md)). Because there's no manual set to fall back on, the drag has to be doing its job.

### Size — set it by the bait in your hand, not by the fish

Bait sets the hook size; the target fish is secondary (`HIXTFWlwnM0`, `m2q22sPPkEM`, [`tackle/hooks.md`](tackle/hooks.md)). Below 1/0 the numbers count *up* as they shrink.

| Bait | Hook | Cite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sardine, small / picky bite** | **Size 2** circle — the most-cited working answer at your grade (size 1 up to 1/0 also used). Owner Mutu **size 4** on the lighter 20–50 lb-grade outfit; Owner Mutu Hybrid **size 2**; Gamakatsu Nautilus (lighter wire than Owner) fished down to **4, 2, and 1** on small/weak bait | `149px8WQ2Ng`, `jQW2HLkMsmY`, `icpm7gADxvU`, `G1YIlakiRqg` |
| **Sardine, ~5–7 in "sportboat" bait** | **2/0** for smaller-grade/picky, up to **4/0–5/0** mid-grade; **4/0 thin-wire circle** straight-tied is the classic sardine hook. San Clemente sportboat standard has been ~**2/0–3/0 on 30 lb** | `HIXTFWlwnM0`, `ftEvyfwjZFU`, `V_ONnegk95M`, `nsUdT-zXI8s`, `M7BtON4GZgQ` |
| **Anchovy** | **No. 4 thin wire** (No. 4 to No. 2 fished all day on a SoCal tuna trip) | `HIXTFWlwnM0`, `_PGm-TlFU2A` |
| **Mackerel** | Step up hard — thick-wire Mustad circles in **4/0, 6/0, 8/0** | `RbqOKkINSCM` |

**Wire gauge:** thin wire for live bait — it adds little weight to the bait's nose so the sardine swims naturally. Stated flat for a sportboat sardine flyline on bluefin: reach for a **4/0 thin-wire circle**, because a sardine (unlike a heartier mackerel) "has a hard time swimming" a standard-gauge hook around convincingly (`ftEvyfwjZFU`, `sWRSYCmt4Tw`). Counterpoint on record: on ~3–4 in anchovies against fish to 40 lb, a thin-wire hook didn't have enough shock absorption under sustained drag and parted — thicker-shank Mustad/VMC held up (`_PGm-TlFU2A`).

**A rod caution that belongs with the small hook:** a rod tip too heavy for this size hook **rips the hook out** of the bait or the fish (`QHY5kmU7OTU`).

## How to rig the bait

### Hook placement — pick the swim you want

From [`bait/fishing-live-bait.md`](bait/fishing-live-bait.md) and [`techniques/flyline.md`](techniques/flyline.md):

- **Nose** — through the **clear spot right in front of the eyes**, and on a sardine specifically **side to side through the tip of the nose**, not chin-to-top; that's "the only way" to nose-hook one (`SluBXkT3cuw`, `ZggReeO1nyU`). All-purpose, most reactive, swims naturally and stays up. **Mandatory the instant any weight goes on the line** (`aD0Iim9C15o`, `w5_x6kkN-xE`). One caution for a tensioned finicky flyline: any tension on a nose-hooked bait drags its head back toward the boat instead of letting it swim away (`48ZFXnCTTQE`).
- **Belly** — just above the anal fin. Sends the bait **down and away**; the panel's stated reason to default here for tuna is that a belly-hooked bait "shoots out" with an urgency that triggers a **reaction strike**, it only catches a little cartilage (a butt hook set too far forward catches organs and makes the bait bleed), and it tends to wind off on the retrieve, forcing you onto a fresh bait. Deckhands belly-hook the bait they hand out **nine times out of ten** (`UP_3fBu7VPA`).
- **Butt** — near the vent, under the two rear fins. Also down and away, and it **defeats sea lions** picking off nose-hooked baits at the islands. Zero weight only. On a butt-hooked bluefin flyline day the fish were **80–100 ft down and not coming up the chum line**, and getting the bait down is what the angler credited (`T3p1mrqNjIo`, `8YvmROeVL-0`, `oB4BpIUTTl4`). Trade-off: it draws blood, so the bait doesn't last as long (`ZggReeO1nyU`).
- **Shoulder / nape** — for **bluefin boiling 50–200 yd off and not coming closer**. Entered behind the head in the nape area, deep enough to hold but not into the spine. As the bait swims away the hook **folds down**, cutting resistance so the bait carries more of its own distance, and riding folded it's less visible to a tuna looking up. Let it out 100–150 yd, then **wind it back slowly** — bites often come as it comes back toward the boat (`aD0Iim9C15o`, `QHY5kmU7OTU`).
- **Sardine "wishbone"** — the bony structure near the two small fins in the middle of the chest, hooked crosswise. For fish sitting **30–40 ft under the boat**; the tell is other anglers hooking up within **10–15 seconds** of their bait hitting the water. No cast needed — drop or flip it ~10 ft out and it dives straight down. Trade-off: that bait lives about **3 minutes** versus 10–15 hooked further back, and the hook tends to tear out when you start reeling (`aD0Iim9C15o`, `QHY5kmU7OTU`).

**Shoulder and belly are soft spots — cast them underhand, not overhand**; a hard overhand swing tears the bait off the hook (`SAltQjih0ms`, [`techniques/underhand-casting.md`](techniques/underhand-casting.md)). Carson prefers underhand generally on this rig: landing gently and swimming away on its own matters more than distance (`QHY5kmU7OTU`).

### Putting the hook in

Hold a circle hook **high on the arch and press straight down** into the bait. Holding it from the bottom the way you would a J leaves no gap and squeezes the bait into a "potato chip" (`8YvmROeVL-0`, [`bait/fishing-live-bait.md`](bait/fishing-live-bait.md)).

### Bait selection is the other half of the rig

Cull the bloody, scaly, lethargic ones into the chum bucket and fish the lively lime-green baits that are hardest to catch in the tank. **If it isn't pulling line off the reel within about 60 seconds, wind it in, flick it off, grab a fresh one.** Pick for scales intact, good color, and slime. On a finicky bluefin bite specifically: "the guys that change baits often are the ones that are going to catch more fish" (`V_ONnegk95M`, `LsFMBCa9DOQ`, [`techniques/flyline.md`](techniques/flyline.md)).

### Fishing it

**No weight, cast well away from the boat** — fish are finickier close to the hull, and the goal is to get your bait out beyond everybody else's, into its own zone. Adding a split shot to help the cast is framed bluntly as a crutch for not knowing how to cast, not an upgrade (`gKrYKvqHUjk`, `raUPkuaFXpw`). On a butt- or belly-hooked bait, **keep tension the moment it lands** — a little pull makes it swim away like a propeller; give it slack and it turns and comes right back under the boat (`8YvmROeVL-0`).

## Drag

Every source on this rig says the same thing about it: **have the drag set before you fish**, and pair it with a reel whose drag is genuinely good — "everything has to be perfect if you're going to catch a 30 or 40 lb tuna on one of these finesse rigs" (`ptoIvB2MspE`, `V_ONnegk95M`).

[`tackle/drag-setting.md`](tackle/drag-setting.md) gives the rule as **a quarter to a third of your lowest line-class rating**, starting at the light end (Scotty, `VpW91AKOFVQ`, high confidence; Carson targets the 25% end, `9JnIS8HkZlA`; a scale-based alternative cites ~30% of rated breaking strength). Its worked example is 40 lb → 10–13 lb. **Applying that rule arithmetically to your build**: a 20 lb top shot lands at roughly **5–6.5 lb** of strike drag, a 25 lb top shot at roughly **6–8 lb**. That arithmetic is mine — the KB does not state a drag number for a 20–25 lb flyline outfit. Confirm by hand-feel or a spring scale ($20–30, `-hY20bxz3oM`), and if you're in a stalemate mid-fight, bump it **one click at a time** rather than clamping down.

## If they won't bite

The KB's first lever is unambiguous and repeated three times on one registered channel (`high` confidence): **go down in hook size, not in line class.** The worked example is a 1/0 circle stepped to a No. 1; a Tanner Bank season with 40–60 lb bluefin had a smaller hook getting bit better **regardless of bait size**. Stated reason for not dropping line instead: fishing too light "just to get a bite" wastes the trip, because you can't land the fish — "you're gonna flag a fish for two hours" (`FXWOIB0TPfE`, `YntRJAN88fs`, `shZCjX2-fkI`, [`tackle/hooks.md`](tackle/hooks.md)).

Held side by side with that, from the light-line side: when bluefin won't bite, sometimes the bait simply isn't "swimming right on heavier lines" — drop to **20–25 lb** and the fish want to eat it (Vince, `icpm7gADxvU`). The two are opposite first moves; the KB keeps both.

# Flyline rig for 20–30 lb bluefin

You gave me no conditions, so this is step 4 of [`planning/day-plan-protocol.md`](planning/day-plan-protocol.md) only — gear resolution. No zone, timing, or conditions call below.

**The short version:** at 20–30 lb the KB puts you in the *finesse fly-line class* — 8–8.5 ft rod, 12–30 lb ([`tackle/gear-classes.md`](tackle/gear-classes.md)) — braid backing, a **20–25 lb mono top shot**, **~4 ft of 20–25 lb fluorocarbon**, and a **small, fine-wire hook (size 4 to 2/0 depending on the bait and whose doctrine you follow)** tied straight to the fluoro with nothing else in line.

## 2. Two heavier-shouldered variants, same grade

Both are in-corpus alternatives, not corrections — pick by how likely a bigger fish is to eat your bait:

- **25 lb all-purpose flyline setup** ([`tackle/bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md`](tackle/bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md), `G1YIlakiRqg`): 8–8.5 ft rod rated **15–30 or 15–40 lb** (Calstar 850L named), **two-speed** Fathom 15, **30 lb braid straight to 15–25 ft of fluorocarbon** — long enough the fish doesn't see the Spectra, short enough that a flylined sardine isn't dragging a lot of thick line through the water. - **Ray Sharifi's "light stick"** of a two-rod bluefin flyline system, explicitly built for **20–50 lb grade** ([`techniques/flyline.md`](techniques/flyline.md), `jQW2HLkMsmY`): 8 ft rod rated **20–50 lb**, Shimano Torium 16, **30 lb mono top shot, 50+ yards**, short fluoro leader matched to the top shot. ## 3. Hook — size and style

**The bait sets the size, the fish is secondary** ([`tackle/hooks.md`](tackle/hooks.md), `HIXTFWlwnM0`, `m2q22sPPkEM`):

| Bait | Hook |
| --- | --- |
| Sardine (~5–7 in) | **2/0** for smaller-grade / picky tuna, up to **4/0–5/0** for mid-grade bluefin; 4/0 thin wire is the classic sardine hook |
| Anchovy | **No. 4 thin wire** — San Clemente Island bluefin historically came on No. 4, even No. 8 |
| Mackerel | Step up — thick-wire Mustad circles run **4/0, 6/0, 8/0** (`RbqOKkINSCM`) |

**Line-class-driven sizing for live sardines on Owner Gorilla live-bait J hooks: 25 or 30 lb test → a No. 1 or No. 2 hook** (`y0LVT59inEA`).

**Circle or J at 20–30 lb — the KB does not settle this, and the split is real:**

- **The weight threshold says J is fine.** "Circle hooks for fish 40 lb and up; a standard J hook is fine under 40 lb" (`YntRJAN88fs`, `y0LVT59inEA`). The mechanism: on a bigger fish a J sets deep in the mouth or throat where the leader rubs teeth on every headshake; a circle drags out and pins the corner of the mouth, keeping the leader off the teeth. At 20–30 lb that teeth-exposure problem is what the threshold says you don't yet have.
- **At small sizes a J baits better.** A small J's gap is noticeably bigger and easier to pin a bait through than a small circle's tighter gap; faster baiting = less handling = a livelier bait = more bites (`48ZFXnCTTQE`, `icpm7gADxvU`). The finesse rig above uses a J for exactly this reason.
- **Against that: "circle hooks always for bluefin."** A Cortez Bank charter's trip-wide briefing (`UfuiWFVvz2E`); a first bluefin trip is told **1/0 or 2/0 circle, not J** (`EyB98RuKfeQ`); and the [`species/bluefin-tuna.md`](species/bluefin-tuna.md) router's own picky-on-bait row routes to a circle.
- **Decision frame the KB actually gives:** the choice turns on **grade and leader exposure vs. baiting speed**. If you genuinely expect 20–30 lb and nothing bigger, the small-J case holds. If a 40–60 lb fish can plausibly eat your bait (the 25 lb-class reality above), the circle's corner-of-mouth hookset is what keeps 20–25 lb fluoro off bluefin teeth. Section order here is not a ranking.

**If you go circle at this grade, the corpus sizes cluster small:** Owner **Hybrid Mutu size 2** is a named favorite for light-line bluefin bait fishing at **20–25 lb test** when the bite is finicky — thinner gauge than a standard Mutu, for a better-swimming bait (`icpm7gADxvU`, `0AtmEH6aQt0`). Owner **Mutu size 4** on the lighter 20–50 lb-grade flyline outfit (`jQW2HLkMsmY`). One deckhand's boat put ~90% of its daytime bites on flyline worked on a dedicated **25–30 lb fluorocarbon stick with a size 2 circle** (sizes 1 to 1/0 also used) (`149px8WQ2Ng`). One observed daytime bluefin trip ran **25–40 lb test to a straight No. 2 hook** on sardine, and an angler credited a **No. 2 (or No. 4) circle with a ~90% corner-of-the-mouth hookup rate**, reasoning a smaller hook lets the bait swim better (`oB4BpIUTTl4`/`r4J5nP5Bkl4`).

**Contrasting heavier build, same technique, different premise:** Capt. Dave Hansen's sportboat sardine flyline for bluefin is a **4/0 thin-wire circle**, straight-tied with a San Diego jam, **no swivel, no bead**, fluorocarbon throughout because bluefin "have so many teeth" (`ftEvyfwjZFU`); his mackerel version runs **3–4 ft of straight 80 lb fluoro** (`RbqOKkINSCM`). That's a heavier-grade program, not a 20–30 lb one — it's here so you know the range exists, not as your build.

**Wire and finish:** **thin wire for live bait** — it adds little weight to the bait's nose so a sardine or anchovy swims naturally; straightening a thin-wire hook is rare on a normal drag ([`tackle/hooks.md`](tackle/hooks.md), `sWRSYCmt4Tw`, `m2q22sPPkEM`). Run **bronze or black, never nickel or chrome** — nickel/chrome reflects light underwater, bronze/black goes effectively invisible wet, and pressured SoCal bluefin are named specifically (`IMnoZVEYpm4`). A **ringed** hook ties straight on and is called out as helping **specifically on light line with a finicky bite**.

**When the bite goes slow, the KB gives you two opposite levers — know which you're pulling:**
- *Downsize the hook, never the line* — a 1/0 circle stepped to a No. 1; "fishing line too light just to get a bite wastes the trip, because a hooked fish that size can't be landed on under-gunned line." Stated at **Tanner Bank, 2020 season, on 40–60 lb bluefin** (`FXWOIB0TPfE`, `YntRJAN88fs`, `shZCjX2-fkI`).
- *Downsize the line* — in warm water a bait doesn't "cure" and toughen, so on too-thick line it won't swim convincingly; skipper guidance caps fly line at **20–25 lb, no more than 30** for a tough bite (`48ZFXnCTTQE`, `icpm7gADxvU`, [`techniques/flyline.md`](techniques/flyline.md)).
- The contexts differ — the hook-not-line rule is stated over a **40–60 lb** grade where landing risk dominates. At your 20–30 lb grade the landing risk is what the finesse rigs above are built to accept.

## 4. Leader — material, test, length

- **Material: fluorocarbon, and it is not optional.** Called a must on finicky SoCal pelagics — "you won't get bit without it" on bluefin ([`tackle/line-and-leader.md`](tackle/line-and-leader.md), `EyB98RuKfeQ`). One observed daytime trip did call fluoro optional ("if you don't have fluoro it's not a detrimental death sentence," `oB4BpIUTTl4`) — an observation, not doctrine. A shop rule of thumb prefers **Japan-made** fluoro; named examples Aftco Psycho and Sunline FC Sniper, both made by Sunline — a brand preference with no mechanism given on camera (`gn2yquuU6eM`).
- **Test: 20–25 lb, matched within about 5 lb of the top shot.** 20 lb fluoro on the 20 lb rig (`ptoIvB2MspE`); the finicky-tuna cap is 20–25 lb, no more than 30 (`48ZFXnCTTQE`); a general live-bait/daytime call is **4–5 arm lengths of 25–30 lb on light flyline gear** (`xPFm_ZV2PZU`).
- **Length — genuinely contested, and it turns on whether a knot has to clear the tip.** **~4 ft on an 8 ft rod** when a mono top shot means the connection knot is right there and would hang up in the tip guide on the cast (`QHY5kmU7OTU`); **15–25 ft** on the braid-straight-to-fluoro 25 lb setup, where the connection sits down on the spool and the reasoning is Spectra visibility instead (`G1YIlakiRqg`). Which number applies is decided by your line build, not by preference.
- **Leader-length floor:** cut-and-retie after fish can whittle a leader to 1–2 ft, at which length bites drop off — rebuild to **5–6 ft** rather than fishing it out (`UP_3fBu7VPA`).
- **Optional bite guard, if you want to lean on a fish that outgrades you:** **25 lb mono top shot → 2–3 ft of 50 lb fluoro**, joined with a Seaguar knot. The 25 lb mono is what's pulling through the water (so the bait swims like it's on 25 lb) while the short heavy section rides inside the mouth along gill plates and teeth. Applied at 20–25 lb main against **30+ lb** yellowfin and bluefin, stepping to 30 lb main → 40 lb leader and 25 lb main → 40 lb leader, which "dramatically helped" land fish (`48ZFXnCTTQE`, [`techniques/flyline.md`](techniques/flyline.md)). Note this is aimed one grade above yours.
- **Knots.** Hook end: **single-pass San Diego jam** is the live-bait default — the doubled version's bulk works against presentation ([`rigging/san-diego-jam-knot.md`](rigging/san-diego-jam-knot.md), `5J7a6UwTA90`). One caveat in that same note: a stated line-class breakpoint puts **improved clinch at 40 lb and under, San Diego jam above 40 lb** (`tpmOYXYQwhU`) — which sits against Carson tying a San Diego jam on 20 lb fluoro. Both are in the KB; neither is reconciled. Mono→fluoro: **4 turns** on a surgeon's for 20–100 lb test (5 turns under 20 lb) is the only version stated as an explicit formula and it repeats across four demonstrations, but **3 turns** is what the rest of the source set actually ties at 25–80 lb ([`rigging/surgeons-knot-mono-to-fluoro.md`](rigging/surgeons-knot-mono-to-fluoro.md)).
- **Nothing else in line** — no weight, no swivel, no bead (`ftEvyfwjZFU`, `RbqOKkINSCM`).

## 5. Rigging the bait

**Pick the bait first — it's called the deciding factor.** Cull bloody, scaly, lethargic baits into the chum bucket and fish the lively lime-green ones that are hardest to catch in the tank; scales intact, good color, slime. **If a bait isn't pulling line off the reel within about 60 seconds, wind it in, flick it off, grab a fresh one** ([`techniques/flyline.md`](techniques/flyline.md), `V_ONnegk95M`).

**Hook position by what you want the bait to do** ([`bait/fishing-live-bait.md`](bait/fishing-live-bait.md), `T3p1mrqNjIo`, `mscHk0qiXnk`, `raUPkuaFXpw`, `8YvmROeVL-0`, `ZggReeO1nyU`):

- **Nose — the all-purpose flyline hook.** Through the clear spot at the tip of the nose, **side to side, one nostril to the other** — not chin-to-top or top-to-chin; that's the only entry point that works on a sardine. Bait swims naturally and stays up. Bonus: winding a nose-hooked bait in to swap it after two or three minutes still draws bites, because it looks like a natural swimming bait coming in (`MuC0uGKDzxg`).
- **Butt — down, away, and sea-lion-proof.** Side to side through the body just above the anal vent, holding the two rear fins together. The bait swims opposite the pull, so it goes down and away from the boat, and it defeats the sea lions and harbor seals that pick off nose-hooked baits at the islands. **Zero weight only** — the instant any weight goes on, switch to the nose hook, because a butt-hooked bait dragged by a sinker swims backward (`w5_x6kkN-xE`). Trade-off: it draws blood, so that bait doesn't last as long. Observed on a Catalina west-end bluefin trip as the "hot ticket" bait, on roughly a **2/0–3/0 hook and 25–30 lb test** (`M7BtON4GZgQ`).
- **Shoulder / nape — for fish boiling well off the boat.** Behind the head, deep enough to hold but not into the spine (that kills the bait). For bluefin boiling **50–150, up to 200-plus yards off** and not coming closer: as the bait swims away the hook folds down, cutting resistance so it carries more distance under its own power and hides the hook from a fish looking up. Let it out **100–150 yards, then wind slowly back** — bites often come as it works back toward the boat (`aD0Iim9C15o`, `QHY5kmU7OTU`).
- **Sardine "wishbone" — for fish sitting under the boat.** The bony chest structure ahead of the small fins, for tuna holding **30–40 ft down**; the tell is anglers around you hooking up within seconds of a bait hitting the water. Don't cast it — drop or flip it about **10 ft** from the boat and it dives straight down. Trade-off: roughly **3 minutes** of bait life versus 10–15 minutes hooked further back, and the hook tears out as soon as you start reeling (`aD0Iim9C15o`, `QHY5kmU7OTU`).
- **If you're fishing under tension** (rolling the reel to keep the bait swimming), a nose hook drags the bait's head back toward the boat — the wrong direction. Use the shoulder hook, or the butt hook anchored **over the bone behind the fin** (in from the tail side, through the body cavity, back out the front side of that fin), with the **hook eye oriented toward the tail** so rolling pressure makes it dive and flee (`48ZFXnCTTQE`).
- **Baiting a circle hook:** hold the hook **high on the arch and press straight down** into the bait. Holding it from the bottom the way you'd hold a J leaves no gap and squeezes the bait into a "potato chip" (`8YvmROeVL-0`).
- **Casting:** shoulder and belly are **soft spots** — cast them **underhand**, not overhand; a hard overhand swing tears the bait off the hook (`SAltQjih0ms`). A graphite tip is too stiff to load on a live-bait cast — it whip-cracks and either stuns the bait or throws it off; **glass or composite** loads gently so the bait lands lively (`48ZFXnCTTQE`).
- **No weight, and get it out past everyone else's bait.** Fish are finickier close to the hull. Adding a split shot or slider to help the cast is framed bluntly as a crutch, not a technique upgrade — learn to cast the bait itself (`gKrYKvqHUjk`).

## 6. Setting up and coming tight

- **Drag: a quarter to a third of the outfit's lowest-rated class** ([`tackle/drag-setting.md`](tackle/drag-setting.md), `VpW91AKOFVQ`, `9JnIS8HkZlA`) — on a 20 lb top shot that's **~5–6.5 lb**; on 25 lb, **~6–8 lb** (arithmetic from that rule, not a stated figure). Set it from free-spool by feel, then confirm on a scale. On this rig specifically: pair it with a reel that has a very good drag, have a crewman set it if you're unsure, and **every knot has to be perfect** — "everything has to be perfect if you're going to catch a 30 or 40 lb tuna on one of these finesse rigs" (`ptoIvB2MspE`).
- **The bite:** give a flylined bait a solid **three count** before coming tight, without swinging. On a circle that alone seats the corner of the mouth; on a J, still don't swing — raise the rod to about 45° and wind tight (`R48YdVFfEOI`). At the small hook sizes above the counts split: **10 count on a circle** (it needs to turn away and swallow-then-eject the bait into the corner), **3 count on a J** (`48ZFXnCTTQE`).

# Daytime bluefin knife jig, 200–350 ft

Your depth window is 33–58 fathoms, which sits right on the KB's core sounded-school scenario for bluefin: `species/bluefin-tuna.md` calls the "fish sounded deep (30–50 fm), wind up, no surface sign" row a **knife-jigging** situation — meter-and-jig, not troll. ## 1. Jig weight

**Start from the rule, then adjust for current.**

- **Speed/knife jig: ~1 g per foot of depth** (`techniques/knife-jigging.md`, `lures/knife-jigs.md`). For your window that is **200 g at 200 ft, 350 g at 350 ft**. (Slow-pitch/wing-fall profiles run heavier — ~1.5 g/ft — because the extra weight offsets the flutter; that would be 300–525 g for the same depths.)
- **The router's daytime band:** `species/bluefin-tuna.md` gives **day 150–300 g**, stepping to **300–400 g** with more current or deeper fish, "to punch down and still stay horizontal in the strike zone."
- **Captain-called range overall: 150–400 g**, heavier (200–300 g+) for deeper fish or harder current; a lighter ~220 g fluttery jig is for fish only 150–200 ft down (`techniques/knife-jigging.md`).
- **Tightest working band in the corpus** (Highliner deckhand POV, `149px8WQ2Ng`, 2023-04-24; unregistered channel — medium confidence): **200 g minimum, up to 300 g, occasionally 350 g; stay under 400 g unless the current is really ripping**, the point being to keep a smaller bait-profile presentation.
- **Practical ceiling** (Cesar via Dirty Hookers, `M8hOYQ_6rSg`, 2025-05-20 — medium confidence): above ~350 g the jig becomes a lot of work for the angler for little added benefit.

**Carry 200 / 250 / 300 / 350 g** and put ~1 g/ft on the captain's called number. Two step-outs are documented but both are conditions-specific, not your default: a hard-drifting windy night at Cortez/Tanner where 250–350 g would not hold the zone and the boat went to **500 g** (`LsFMBCa9DOQ`, mid-August 2024; medium confidence), and a night-only floor of **300 g** (250 g for anglers savvy enough to fish it) from one Cortez Bank briefing (`UfuiWFVvz2E`; medium confidence). Neither is a daytime number.

**Profile, not just weight** (`lures/knife-jigs.md`): in fast drift or wind go **longer and more slender** so the jig cuts water and falls straight; in slack/light wind go **fatter and more fluttery** for hang time. Counter check at the tackle shop — balance the jig on a fingertip at its midpoint: tail-heavy = speed jig, balances flat = slow-pitch/wing-fall.

**One real daytime-depth data point for your exact window:** Capt. Dave Hansen (`scmPq63lLWM`, 2021-05-18; registered voice, product-adjacent — medium confidence) says the low-visibility **flat-fall bite on bluefin comes "at night or 250–350 ft down"** — dark enough that he reasons the fish react to profile and flash rather than detail. So on the deep half of your window a flat-fall / center-weighted profile is documented as a daytime option, not just a night lure.

**Color at that depth** (`lures/knife-jigs.md`): glow paint reflects **UV** more than regular paint, which is why an all-glow jig can be a strong **daytime** pick at real depth (e.g. 300 ft) — there's still light down there, just dim (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`; the same seminar rates color itself as mostly angler confidence — low confidence on specific colors). Baseline daytime rule of thumb: off-color/darker water → whites and yellows; bright clear sun → more chrome/flash. Hansen runs **reds, silvers and blues in daylight**, favorite pattern imitating **pelagic red crabs** with orange for the egg-bearing belly (`RbqOKkINSCM`, `scmPq63lLWM`) — and he explicitly calls the "glow works best" claim angler-confidence marketing. The KB keeps those side by side and does not reconcile them; his own tiebreak is to switch to whatever is actually getting bit on the boat.

## 2. Hook rig

**Doctrine for bluefin knife jigs: a single bottom assist pair, no top hook** (`techniques/knife-jigging.md`, `lures/knife-jigs.md`). Reasons given: the fish suck the jig in, so a top hook adds little, and a top hook fouls fish in the cheek or pec on the swinging jig — which moves the fight off the mouth and puts a loose hook near your hands at the rail. `lures/knife-jigs.md` also frames **US waters as a two-hook cap**.

Be aware this is a **live, unreconciled conflict** in the KB — several logged rigs run top *and* bottom hooks:

**What every source agrees on** (`lures/knife-jigs.md`, `rigging/assist-hooks.md`, source `U4zifdssSes` — medium confidence):

- **Light-wire assist hooks**, short cord loops, matched to jig length so the hook rides at the head and doesn't tangle the body.
- **Speed-jig assists run noticeably shorter** than the long assists sold on the rack, which are built for slow-pitch — do not grab a slow-pitch assist for a speed jig.
- **If you run any double, the two hooks must never cross or lock together.** Tie each hook from the back of the shank forward toward the point, one hook per leg, so the points ride outward.
- **Don't over-hook a jig** — too many assists kills its action.
- **Sizing = pass-through test:** hold the hook against the jig body; it should be just wide enough to pass all the way through. Oversized interferes with the swim.
- **Position:** a single top assist's loop should rest roughly **a third of the way down** the jig's total length.
- Cord: shrink tubing over the cord itself stiffens it for a **speed jig** so the loop doesn't flop into your main line; softer cord for slow jigs. Gel (not thin) cyanoacrylate on the knot, 30–45 s to set.

**The hookset is part of the hook rig.** Do not wind on the bite like bait fishing — a jig-hooked fish typically gets stuck just above the barb on a hard point of the jaw, and if you don't drive the point past the barb the hook can bend open under load regardless of wire (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`, via `techniques/knife-jigging.md`). On the bite: **claw/palm the spool and give three or four hard sideways swings**, but wait until **drag is actually coming off the reel** before you swing. **Strike drag 19–20 lb** — the note flags the 35 lb figure circulating online as dangerous (a witnessed loss of five jigging outfits ripped out of hands on one trip).

## 3. Terminal — jig connection

Two schools, kept side by side, both fished in the fleet:

- **Crimp school** (`rigging/bite-leaders.md`, `kO_BqzUYayc`; `nRFFM8DT-og`): a **~2 ft bite leader, no more than 2–2.5 ft**, of heavy fluoro — the example builds on **130 lb**, and `species/bluefin-tuna.md` calls a **200 lb bite-leader class** the most critical part of a bluefin setup. Crimp **directly through the jig's solid wire ring, not a split ring**. On 130 lb use a **size 1.6 crimp + 1.6 chafe sleeve**, two presses per crimp (one top, one bottom), snug not crushed, with a little tag through. Length is capped because the large swivel at the top **will not pass the guides** — too long and you're stranded at the top-shot connection with the fish still at deep color.
- **Direct-tie school** (West Coast Jiggerz, `j37zxs33gws`): no crimp — 80–100 lb braid, FG knot, **15–20 ft mono/fluoro top shot tied straight to the jig**. A variant (`gevNj2Y1Ep8`, 2023-09-02; medium confidence): 100 lb braid straight to a **10 ft leader of 130 lb fluoro**, no crimp, no swivel — the bulky knot is deliberate, so when it stops running smoothly through the guides you know to slow down before you crack a tip insert. The same angler's **stated preference** is actually ~25 ft of **80–100 lb mono** to a crimped bite leader, with straight fluoro (6–8 ft of 130 lb) as the fallback when no mono is aboard (`-MP2RqJC7B0`).

## 4. Rod and reel class

**Class: knife / speed-jig class** (`tackle/gear-classes.md`) — a rod that vertically rips a **150–400 g** jig, thin braid, assist hooks.

- **Rod:** short, **parabolic**, high-power; often **spiral / acid-wrapped guides** to kill rod twist and stop the line wrapping the tip during the jigging stroke. `tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md` puts the application at a **PE 4×8, 250–400 g parabolic** blank. Power is deceptive for the size — a demoed speed-jig rod is rated **55 kg (≈110 lb) of pulling power at a 60° rod angle** despite a slow action, but that power only exists because the blank loads through to the butt: **fish it in hand, not racked on the rail**, which throws away roughly half the rod (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`). A budget broomstick (130–150 class) covers schoolie grade; a stiffer fiberglass-tipped composite for cow fish.
- **Reel:** any **narrow two-speed**, high-speed retrieve. Premium (Talica, Daiwa class) if budget allows; a **Fathom** is called out as strong value — big, high capacity, two-speed — with the caveat that the stock handle is thin enough to snap, so budget for a thicker/longer **aftermarket handle** (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`; medium confidence).
- **Documented combo** (`jQW2HLkMsmY`, 2022-05-02; medium confidence): **Shimano Talica 25** on a **Phoenix Black Diamond Hybrid, 7 ft, 60–130 lb**, 100 lb braid, jigs 250–400 g. Caveat from the same angler on the same rod (`-MP2RqJC7B0`): that blank runs **too stiff on 40–50 lb-class bluefin** — hard to keep a consistent bend — while on **80 lb-and-up fish the stiffness worked to their benefit**. Size to the grade you expect on your zone.
- **Line:** **80–100 lb braid** to an FG connection (`techniques/knife-jigging.md`); the application table lists **100 lb braid**, and `species/bluefin-tuna.md`'s gear summary says **100–130 lb-braid jig class / PE6–8**. Two things point you toward the lighter end for 200–350 ft: **thinner braid means less line angle and a jig that hangs straighter under the boat**, and **80 lb sinks faster than 100**; the note explicitly warns against over-gunning the braid chasing a big-fish story — 65–100 lb covers it, and heavier than 100 lb is only worth it on the biggest jigs. `lures/knife-jigs.md` makes the same point: even a jump from 20 lb to 30/40/50 lb braid adds scope and blows the jig sideways.
  - **My read:** 80 lb braid for the 200–250 g / lighter-current end, 100 lb once you're at 300–350 g, the current is up, or there are genuine cows in the zone. `species/bluefin-tuna.md` is explicit that **bluefin gear does not get scaled down** on a mixed-tuna trip (`PrdPJy26H8c`) — grade is unpredictable and tackle stays sized for the biggest fish that could show.
- **PE note:** jig rods and braid are rated in PE (a **diameter** window, not a strength spec); rough conversion **65 lb ≈ PE5** (`tackle/gear-classes.md`, `Ul5FLB2dFgQ`).

## 5. Fishing it at 200–350 ft

- **Mark your line.** Sharpie or a contrasting braid wrap **every 100 ft down to ~400 ft**, or run color-metered braid (changes every 100 ft, some every 25). Interval is angler's choice — one source marks every **50 ft**. The captain calls fish by depth ("fish at 180," "fish at 230") and you must know where your jig is (`techniques/knife-jigging.md`, `nRFFM8DT-og`, `Ul5FLB2dFgQ`). Called depth changes fast — track it continuously rather than dropping once and assuming.
- **Stay vertical — this is the whole game at your depth.** Don't run to the stern and drop with the crowd: your line scopes out and **on the 300 ft mark your jig is only ~80 ft down**. Throw **up-current off the bow, let it sink straight, and walk back toward the stern**. Attributed alternative for stern anglers: **cast down-swell** so that by the time the boat drifts over it the jig sits at roughly **200–250 ft** and hangs straight up and down (`UfuiWFVvz2E`; medium confidence). Ask the deckhand which way the boat drifts.
- **Work the column in 100 ft increments** (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`): at the called depth, in gear, **pop the tip high, drop it, let slack line the jig flutter all the way down** — repeat 2–3 times. Then **wind 10 full turns (≈30–40 ft)** and repeat for another 3–4 sweeps. Ten turns at a time works you through roughly 100 ft while keeping the jig in the zone longer than a straight yo-yo wind.
- **Stroke:** on the down-stroke drop the jig; on the wind-up lift the rod tip **while simultaneously winding a full handle turn**. **Slack the drop, don't tightline it** — resistance on the nose kills the camber/flutter/dart. Daytime is the fast, darting program; the slow "blinking" pace and the center-weighted flip-slider profile are the *night* prescription, not yours.
- **Numbers of jigs matter:** several worked jigs going up and down at once read to a stopped school as a moving school of bait — five idle rods on the rail don't do that. Called out as mattering most at night but explicitly for daytime too (`UfuiWFVvz2E`; medium confidence).

**Two pressures pulling opposite ways — the decision frame.** Daytime bites call for scaling the whole system *down* (thinner line, smaller jigs, more refined presentation) because the fish can actually see your gear, whereas night is forgiving of heavy gear (`Et2TUskzCSs`, [techniques/knife-jigging.md](techniques/knife-jigging.md)). ### 2. Hook rig

**Hard constraints first.** US waters cap you at **two hooks** on the jig ([lures/knife-jigs.md](lures/knife-jigs.md)). If a jig is already rigged with a treble, **never add hooks to it** — Carson calls that "a real safety issue for you and for the crew" (`vVOkxHx58Eg`). And a **captain's stated hook policy overrides everything below**: a lot of sportboats require twins on the bottom only, purely because a top hook has caught a deckhand's hand swinging a jig aboard (`gimFbgN5Jjk`, [lures/knife-jigs.md](lures/knife-jigs.md)).

**The tier-matched answer for a 280–300 g jig** (Carson, `vVOkxHx58Eg`, [tackle/searcher-bluefin-jig-ladder-by-daypart-and-depth.md](tackle/searcher-bluefin-jig-ladder-by-daypart-and-depth.md)):

- **Heavy single hooks, one off the nose and one off the tail** (his own hook is captioned "70 5x" — read as a 7/0, 5X-strong; the number is asr-uncertain in the KB).
- **Leader: 200 lb fluorocarbon, ~2.5–3 ft, to a ~400 lb test swivel.**
- Stated reason for nose+tail: bluefin frequently bite the jig **as it's sinking**, so they "might bite it at either end" — two ends covered. Stated trade-off: a hooked fish can snag the *other* hook and come in sideways, which takes more pulling to land.
- **170 g is the largest jig he'll pair with a treble** — above that he's on singles. At **400 g he flips again to two hooks on the tail only**, because that deep the fish just suck it all the way in and a nose+tail rig risks pinning a big fish in two places.
- Separate rule if you're working a **mechanical cranking / fast-pitch retrieve** (~220 g): **two hooks off the nose end**, "usually the best" for that retrieve style.

**Hook count/placement is a live, unreconciled conflict in this KB** — it's flagged as such in [lures/knife-jigs.md](lures/knife-jigs.md), which carries eight attributed configurations side by side. The main ones:

- **Single bottom assist pair, no top hook** — the night-bluefin doctrine in [techniques/knife-jigging.md](techniques/knife-jigging.md): fish suck the jig in so a top hook adds little, and a top hook fouls fish in the cheek or pec, moving the fight off the mouth and putting a hook at the rail.
- **Single top + short double on the bottom, both light-wire** (Eric's Tackle, `Ul5FLB2dFgQ`) — reported landing fish consistently on the bottom hook; framed as increasingly common in San Diego, standard in Japan.
- **At least one assist on each end** (Billy, Fisherman's Landing Tackle, `6_E5JCRvazc`) — reasoning that these jigs are long enough that a single-end assist costs hookup ratio. His shop's universal size: **Mustad assist hooks, 6/0**, on a heavy-duty split ring.
- **Keyed to jig profile** (Kikkawa, `gimFbgN5Jjk`): small/short 100–150 g → single nose assist; **long-profile jigs → one top and one bottom**; short heavy profile → twins on the bottom only.

**What the choice actually turns on** (the KB doesn't rank these, and section order isn't a ranking): your jig's **profile and weight tier**, your **retrieve** (cranking → nose hooks; drop-and-work → covering both ends), and the **boat's safety rule**. For a 280–300 g slender knife jig fished daytime on the rise, nose+tail singles and long-profile-one-each-end agree — that's the least-contested build for your case.

**Build details that aren't in dispute:**

- **Upgrade the stock hooks.** Stock hooks ship undersized for this grade — "ready to catch local island fish and smaller grade bluefin. - **Speed-jig assists run short** — much shorter than the long slow-pitch assists sold off the rack; don't grab a slow-pitch assist for a speed jig ([lures/knife-jigs.md](lures/knife-jigs.md)). - **Stiffen the cord** with shrink tube over the cord itself on a speed-jig build, so the hook rides **stationary** where the fish will eat it on the pump/upswing — slow-pitch wants the opposite, a loose flopping hook for the fall bite (`unARAuTgF_A`, [rigging/assist-hooks.md](rigging/assist-hooks.md)).

**Leader — day is lighter than night.** The KB carries four connection schools side by side ([techniques/knife-jigging.md](techniques/knife-jigging.md)): crimped ~2 ft of 200 lb; direct tie to a 15–20 ft top shot; a pre-made 200 lb fluoro split-ringed on; and a 200 lb leader → 400 lb swivel → 300 lb split ring. **Counter-pressure to keep in mind:** as bluefin cross roughly **50–60 lb their teeth get bigger**, and a light leader risks abrasion if a big fish engulfs the lure deep — which is why the heavy-grade programs run 200–400 lb mono (`shZCjX2-fkI`, [techniques/flat-fall-jigging.md](techniques/flat-fall-jigging.md)). **Class term: the knife/speed-jig class** — a rod that vertically rips a 150–400 g jig, thin braid, assist hooks ([tackle/gear-classes.md](tackle/gear-classes.md)). The bluefin router's own gear summary for the day-and-night jig program: **100–130 lb-braid jig class / PE6–8, short parabolic jigging rods** ([species/bluefin-tuna.md](species/bluefin-tuna.md)).

**Rod — rate it in grams, not just line class** ([tackle/bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md](tackle/bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md), `Et2TUskzCSs` / `gimFbgN5Jjk`):

| Class | Activates | Fighting drag | Read |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **PE 4–8** ("80 lb stick"), 5.5–6 ft | **150–300 g**, sometimes to 350 g | ~20–30 lb (30 lb the realistic ceiling) | The single rod to bring if you're bringing one |
| **PE 6–10** ("100 lb stick"), 5.5–6 ft | 200 g at the light end, typically **250–500 g** | 25–40 lb | The bigger-fish and night pick |
| PE 3–6 | 100–250 g | ~15 lb, max ~25 | Too light for the deep end of your band |

For a **280–300 g** jig you're on the PE 4–8 / PE 6–10 boundary. The panel's own tiebreaker: **pick the lightest rod that still activates the jig you're fishing, not the heaviest one that can** — their example is a 250 g jig, which both handle, but which "does a little bit better" on the lighter PE 4–8. So **PE 4–8 for 200–300 g, PE 6–10 once you're pushing 350–400 g** or expecting triple-digit fish.

Two buying checks from [tackle/jig-rod-rating-selection.md](tackle/jig-rod-rating-selection.md) (`DTvXJrtytwg` / `PbiZMk4Ft6w` / `ZrcwcugaEe4`, all one source appearance):

- **Buy toward the top of the printed gram rating, not the middle.** A rod printed 100–300 g does *not* fish a 200 g jig well — it fishes best with 250–300 g. The worked example is literally your case: a 200 g jig in 3–400 ft calls for a rod rated close to 200 g **at its top end**.
- **Power has to be soft enough to load.** A blank too stiff won't bend enough to load and unload with the jig, so it can't impart the action — his example is refusing "the 609" for a 150 g jig and sending customers to a seven or six power instead.

**Build details:** short and parabolic, often **spiral / acid-wrapped guides** to kill rod twist and stop line grabbing the tip mid-stroke ([techniques/knife-jigging.md](techniques/knife-jigging.md), [tackle/bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md](tackle/bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md)). These rods carry far more power than they look — one demoed blank is rated **55 kg (~110 lb) of pulling power at a 60° rod angle** — but only because it loads all the way to the butt, so **fish it in hand, not flat on the rail** (that throws away roughly half the rod's power), and keep it at **60° or lower**, no bouncing, no high-sticking (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`, `ZrcwcugaEe4`).

**Reel: a narrow two-speed.**

- **"Narrow" means under 2 in of spool width.** At the **15–30 lb of drag** jigging commonly runs, line on a wide spool digs into itself under load and then won't pay out cleanly (`gimFbgN5Jjk`, [techniques/knife-jigging.md](techniques/knife-jigging.md)).
- Named benchmarks across the class: **Shimano Talica 20** on the light end — valued specifically for free-spool getting a jig **down fast** — up to a **Penn International 50** with 130 lb on the heavy end (`6_E5JCRvazc`). Capt. Matt's daytime knife-jig pick is a **Jigging Master PE7**, or a **Penn Fathom 40** as the budget answer (`G1YIlakiRqg`). The trophy/triple-digit program's spec is a reel putting out **30 lb+ of drag with 400–800 yd of capacity** ([tackle/tackle-express-trophy-bluefin-jig-outfit.md](tackle/tackle-express-trophy-bluefin-jig-outfit.md)).
- A **Fathom** is called out as strong value, with one caveat — the stock handle is thin enough to snap, so budget for an aftermarket handle ([techniques/knife-jigging.md](techniques/knife-jigging.md)).

**Line, for daytime at this depth.** Thin braid is part of the jig system, not a separate choice — less scope, the jig hangs straighter, gets down faster ([lures/knife-jigs.md](lures/knife-jigs.md)). Cesar Chang's split by jig weight (`ZrcwcugaEe4`): **80 lb braid on 350 g-and-heavier jigs with ~15 ft of 130 lb mono top shot**; **65 lb braid on 200 g-and-lighter with a 100 lb mono top shot** — and he's blunt that a rod and reel can't generate enough drag to break 80 lb braid by pulling alone. He landed a **140 lb daytime bluefin on 65 lb braid, single-speed, in 15 minutes** (2023), and a 180 lb fish hooked at ~450 ft on 80 lb braid, two-speed, in 6 minutes (2024), and contrasts both against anglers running 100–130 lb braid on 450–500 g jigs at the same depth who got **less far down** for the extra weight. Capt. Matt's daytime jig setup runs **80 or 100 lb braid**. Ray Sharifi flags the day/night confusion explicitly: daytime jigging with lighter jigs fishes **40–50 lb test**, the night knife-jig bite steps to **80 lb minimum, 80–100 recommended** (`RfiC8sfIWTk`, [techniques/knife-jigging.md](techniques/knife-jigging.md)).

**Drag.** Set to roughly **a quarter to a third of your lowest line-class rating** ([tackle/drag-setting.md](tackle/drag-setting.md)) — on an 80 lb system that's ~20–27 lb, which matches the PE 4–8 fighting-drag window above. **19–20 lb strike is the documented safer setting**; 35 lb+ at strike can rip the outfit out of your hands on the first hookup (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`). And a trap that bites specifically at your depth: **dropping a jig 400 ft already leaves the spool a quarter to a third empty**, and a smaller-diameter spool turns the same lever setting into materially higher real drag — re-check or back off after a long run (`9JnIS8HkZlA`, [tackle/drag-setting.md](tackle/drag-setting.md)).

### 4. Working it, at that depth specifically

- **Mark your line, or you're guessing.** Every 100 ft is the common interval (25/30/50/60 ft also documented); make each mark **12–18 in long**, not a dot — line runs off fast enough on a drop that a short mark gets missed (`fgTmUq78ofQ`). For tuna specifically there's **no bottom to hit**, so the argument for finer multi-colour marking is relocating the *zone*: note the colour you were on when the jig hesitated, work it, then drop back to that same colour (`t0kkwlrNwHk`). Failing to mark line is called the single most common beginner mistake — a fish at 350 ft will not eat a jig worked at 250 or 550 (`UP_3fBu7VPA`).
- **Overshoot the call, then work up.** Fish called at 240 ft → drop to **about 300 ft**, then retrieve up through 180–240 (`gimFbgN5Jjk`); the same 240 → 300 figures come independently from Captain Hart (`AyN9MBWg-XY`). Then work the column *at* the called depth, not 100 ft above or below it.
- **Stay vertical.** Thumb light tension on the spool through the fall — it keeps the jig vertical instead of scoping, and it gets down faster and straighter than a free fall (`Et2TUskzCSs`). Don't fully thumb off, or a bite on the drop backlashes you (`6_E5JCRvazc`). Positionally: fish the very bow or very stern, never midship; cast **up-current off the bow and walk back**, or cast **downwind/down-swell** so the boat drifts onto it and it hangs straight under you. Cast distance is conditions-set: long on a windy fast drift, as little as **20–30 ft** on a flat calm one, because a long cast on a slow drift over-scopes it ([techniques/knife-jigging.md](techniques/knife-jigging.md), `G1YIlakiRqg`).
- **Daytime bite timing is the opposite of night.** At night roughly **70% of bluefin bites come on the fall**; during the day that ratio **flips to most bites on the rise** — the reaction bite (`Et2TUskzCSs`). So the daytime program is pump up / wind down at a brisk pace, and if fish are bumping without hooking up, **speed it up** rather than slowing down (`M8hOYQ_6rSg`) — with the one caveat that as daytime water warms and fish go dormant and sit lower, slowing down is worth a try before assuming they're gone (`Et2TUskzCSs`).
- **Hookset: do not wind on it like bait fishing.** A jig-hooked fish gets stuck just above the barb on a hard point of the jaw; if you don't drive the point past the barb the hook can bend open under load. Wait for drag to run, then drive it with hard swings (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`).
- Deckhand heuristic worth knowing at this depth: you're over water too deep for the jig to ever hit bottom, so **"when you hit bottom, you're bit"** — a sudden stop in the free-spooling line is a fish (`6_E5JCRvazc`).

## 1. Jig weight

### The rule of thumb
**Roughly 1 gram per foot of depth for a speed/knife jig** — "fish 200 ft down, go around 200 grams, you're safe" (`lures/knife-jigs.md`, `gimFbgN5Jjk`). A slow-pitch/wing-fall jig runs heavier at **~1.5 g per ft**, because the extra weight offsets the flutter and keeps it vertical. That puts your band at **200–350 g knife**, or **300–500 g if you're fishing a wing-fall/flutter profile**.

The three-factor priority in the same note: **depth decides weight, current decides shape, color is least important** (`gimFbgN5Jjk`).

### The daytime-specific ladder (the closest direct answer)
From `tackle/searcher-bluefin-jig-ladder-by-daypart-and-depth.md` (`vVOkxHx58Eg`), which orders by how well fish can see the lure *first*, then depth:

| Jig | When / depth | Hook style at that tier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ~100 g | midday, full daylight — good to about **200 ft** (the floor of your band) | heavy-duty **20-size treble**, upgraded from stock |
| ~170 g | dawn/dusk, **or** any time fish sit deeper than the 100 g jig's ~200 ft ceiling — as deep as **~250 ft** | still a treble; this is the heaviest jig paired with a treble |
| ~280 g | good to about **300 ft**, depending on current and wind; beyond that, or in wind, may not reach | heavy **single** hooks, one off the nose, one off the tail (captioned "70 5x" — read as 7/0, 5X-strong; exact number is `asr-uncertain`) |
| ~300 g | same depth range as 280 g | same nose-and-tail single pairing |
| ~220 g | mid-depth **speed/fast-pitch** (cranking retrieve) | **two hooks off the nose end** |
| ~400 g | reaches ~500 ft | **two hooks on the tail only** |

That source's own caveat: treat it as a starting point, not a lookup — which jig and depth works can change every hour, so check with the crew (`vVOkxHx58Eg`). Its stated reasoning for keeping daytime presentations smaller: bluefin eat comparatively little and can see the lure well in daylight, so daytime calls for smaller, more careful gear than the night tiers.

### What the rest of the corpus says at this weight range
- **Router row:** day **150–300 g**; more current or deeper fish → **300–400 g** to punch down and still stay horizontal in the strike zone (`species/bluefin-tuna.md`). ⚠ The KB itself flags this against the Searcher ladder's 100 g daytime jig — the two are not reconciled.
- **Working range from a highliner deckhand, 2023:** **200 g minimum, up to 300 g, occasionally 350 g; stay below 400 g unless the current is really ripping** (`lures/knife-jigs.md`, `149px8WQ2Ng`). This is the tightest match to your question.
- **Captain-called range:** **150–400 g**, heavier (200–300 g+) for deeper fish or harder current; a lighter ~220 g fluttery jig for fish 150–200 ft up in the column (`techniques/knife-jigging.md`).
- **Windy day:** a short, stubby "hot dog style" jig at **170–250 g** sinks faster and flutters on the drop, so it keeps producing when chop makes a slim jig hard to keep vertical (`lures/knife-jigs.md`, `G1YIlakiRqg`). At ~20 mph the same seminar goes to a **400–500 g knife jig** instead of a light flat-fall (`techniques/flat-fall-jigging.md`, `qLKLlZdiflA`).
- **Triple-digit fish specifically:** carry a spread — ~200 g flat-fall up through **320, 400, 500 g** knife jigs, picked by how fast the jig needs to reach the fish (`tackle/tackle-express-trophy-bluefin-jig-outfit.md`, `DuNkl8F45NE`); the same shop's 2025 recap widens the box to **160 g flat-fall up to a 560 g knife jig** (`Q4sW6JRQzAY`). The KB keeps these ranges side by side rather than reconciling them.

### My read for you
**Carry 170 g, ~200–250 g, and ~300 g, and fish the lightest one that still hangs straight under the boat.** Specifically:
- **200–250 ft, light current:** 170–250 g.
- **250–300 ft, or a real drift:** 280–300 g.
- **300–350 ft, or wind/hard current:** 300–350 g, stepping to 400 g only if the jig is scoping out.

The diagnostic is in `techniques/knife-jigging.md` (`Et2TUskzCSs`): the **first drop of a drift calibrates the rest of it** — if a jig that should sink straight starts to scope out, go narrower/longer (cuts water, sinks faster) or go heavier. Keep light thumb tension on the drop; it keeps the jig vertical and gets it down faster and straighter than a free fall.

### Shape and color, daytime
- **Longer, skinnier lures draw more bites during the day**; the short/stubby fast-sinking profile is the *night* answer, where an estimated 90% of bites come as the lure sinks (`lures/knife-jigs.md`, `G1YIlakiRqg`).
- Current cuts the same way: longer/skinnier in a fast drift, fatter and more fluttery in slack water (`gimFbgN5Jjk`).
- Color is the least important of the three factors. One real mechanism worth knowing: glow paint reflects UV, so an all-glow jig can be a strong **daytime** choice at real depth (e.g. 300 ft) where there is still light, just dim — do not assume glow means night-only. ⚠ The KB carries a fact-check flag on that passage (`-nIhadZwxAA` never says "glow paint"; its UV discussion is about chartreuse/black/silver finishes). Baseline daytime rule of thumb: off-color/darker water favors whites and yellows, bright clear water favors chrome and flash (`lures/knife-jigs.md`).

### The flat-fall alternative in the same band
If the captain is calling a specific number and you want the fall-triggered cadence instead, `techniques/flat-fall-jigging.md` documents **250 g and 300 g** Shimano flat-falls (`an_uw-5pjfw`), a **350 / 320 / 250 / 120 g** ladder defaulting to the **250–320 g** tier (`fgTmUq78ofQ`), and a **320 g** lure for fish sounding 240–360 ft with a stated **250 g floor** at that depth (`eehDVb6_GoI`). A 150 g flat-fall does not sink fast enough to beat a wind-driven drift to the bite zone (`shZCjX2-fkI`). Doctrine there is **go heavier, not lighter**, especially in wind.

## 2. Hook rig

Two fixed constraints from `lures/knife-jigs.md` before anything else:
- **US waters cap a jig at two hooks.**
- **Stock hooks are rarely worth fishing as-is** — Searcher-grade fish run bigger than these lure lines are built for anywhere else in the world (`vVOkxHx58Eg`); the trophy program pulls every jig off the shelf and re-rigs it before it's fished (`DuNkl8F45NE`).

**A captain's stated boat policy overrides everything below.** Many sportboats require **twin bottom hooks only** — a safety rule, not an action rule: a top hook has caught a deckhand's hand swinging a jig aboard a crowded boat. Some boats allow both top and bottom hooks by day. Ask before you rig (`gimFbgN5Jjk`).

**The KB is explicit that hook count and placement are unresolved across the fleet** — no configuration is called correct over another (`lures/knife-jigs.md`, and `shZCjX2-fkI` in the flat-fall note). Here is what it does give you:

### By jig weight (the daytime ladder, `vVOkxHx58Eg`)
- **Through 170 g:** an upgraded heavy-duty treble — a **20-size treble** on the 100 g jig specifically. **Safety rule: never add extra hooks to a lure already rigged with a treble.**
- **280–300 g (your working tier):** heavy **single** hooks, **one off the nose and one off the tail**. Reason: bluefin frequently bite the jig as it sinks rather than striking cleanly at one end, so the fish can bite at either end and the pairing covers that ambiguity. Trade-off: a hook on each end lets a hooked fish snag the other hook and come in sideways, which takes more pulling force to land.
- **If you're fishing a cranking speed/fast-pitch retrieve at ~220 g:** **two hooks off the nose end** — placement here is driven by retrieve style, not depth.

### By jig profile (the other documented axis, `gimFbgN5Jjk`)
- Small, short-profile jigs (100–150 g) → **single assist on the nose only**.
- Long-profile jigs → **one assist top and one bottom**.
- Short, heavy-profile jigs → **twin assists on the bottom only**, no top hook.

### The competing baselines
- `techniques/knife-jigging.md` baseline is a **single bottom assist pair, no top hook**: the fish suck the jig in, so a top hook adds little and risks fouling the fish in the cheek or pec, moving the fight off the mouth. (Stated for the night program specifically.)
- An attributed variant fished alongside it: **a single top assist plus a short double on the bottom, both deliberately light-wire** — reported landing fish consistently on the bottom hook, an increasingly common San Diego rig and the standard in Japan (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`).
- **Mustad 6/0 is called "a good universal size"** across the Rip Roller, Streaker and Jigpara, on a heavy-duty split ring, with a stated minimum of **at least one assist on each end** — a single-end-only assist costs hookup ratio on a jig this long (`6_E5JCRvazc`).
- **Don't over-hook:** too many assists kills the jig's action (`jQW2HLkMsmY`).
- **The two hooks of any double must never cross or lock together** (`lures/knife-jigs.md`; tying method in `rigging/assist-hooks.md`).

### Assist-hook build parameters (`rigging/assist-hooks.md`)
- **Hook size — pass-through test:** hold the hook against the jig body; it should be just wide enough to pass all the way through the body. Oversized interferes with the jig's swimming action (`U4zifdssSes`).
- **Position:** a single top assist rests roughly **one third of the way down the jig's total length**.
- **Cord stiffness by style — this one matters for a speed jig:** slide shrink tubing over the *cord itself* to stiffen it, so the hook stays **stationary**, riding exactly where the fish will eat it. A speed jig is bit **on the pump/upswing** (roughly 85% pump / 15% fall, per `ZrcwcugaEe4`), so a stiff cord holds the hook there for a solid hookset on the swing. A slow-pitch jig gets bit on the fall and wants a **looser, flopping** hook instead (`unARAuTgF_A`).
- **Cord:** ~215 lb mono-core for a single top assist on a speed jig, ~300 lb for a tail double (both `asr-uncertain`); a hollow-core PE alternative runs a clean **180 lb**. The heavy rating is for **abrasion resistance against teeth and scales**, not breaking strength — an assist cord is never loaded near 180 lb.
- **Ring vs swivel at the jig** follows how much flutter the jig already has: a welded/solid ring split-ringed to the jig is the default for longer bluefin jigs, because a swivel accentuates flutter and on an already-fluttery jig that makes it *less* attractive to bluefin; a jig that flutters little benefits from the swivel (`Et2TUskzCSs`).

### Leader — several builds, not reconciled
- **Base knife-jig bite leader:** **130 lb fluoro, no more than about 2–2.5 ft**, over 80–100 lb braid, crimped with a **size 1.6 crimp and matching 1.6 chafe sleeve**, snug not maximal, two presses per crimp. The length cap exists because the large swivel crimped to the top end will not wind through the guides — a longer leader strands you at the top-shot connection with the fish still at deep color (`rigging/bite-leaders.md`, `kO_BqzUYayc`).
- **At the 280 g tier specifically:** **200 lb fluorocarbon, ~2.5–3 ft, to a ~400 lb test swivel**; the 300 g tier runs 200 lb fluoro at ~3 ft (`vVOkxHx58Eg`).
- **At 170 g the leader is explicitly situational** — sometimes 130 lb fluoro, sometimes straight to the 50 or 60 lb mainline, sometimes 80 lb fluoro; check with the crew day-of rather than picking in advance (`vVOkxHx58Eg`).
- **Trophy-fish program:** **200–300 lb** standard, up to 400 lb for heavy night work — the reasoning is that a swallowed jig sits against the fish's teeth for the whole fight, and bluefin do have teeth (`DuNkl8F45NE`, `Q4sW6JRQzAY`).
- **No-crimp builds** also exist: 80–100 lb braid through an FG connection to a 15–20 ft mono/fluoro top shot tied direct to the jig (`techniques/knife-jigging.md`).

## 3. Rod, reel and line class

### Class term
**Knife / speed-jig class** — a rod that vertically rips a 150–400 g jig, thin braid, assist hooks (`tackle/gear-classes.md`).

### Rod — pick by PE rating, matched to jig weight
From `tackle/bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md`, PE ratings are keyed to the jig weight the blank **activates** and the fighting drag run at that class (`Et2TUskzCSs`, `gimFbgN5Jjk`):

| Class | Activates | Fighting drag | Fit to your band |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **PE 3–6** | 100–250 g | ~15 lb, max ~25 lb | too light above ~250 g |
| **PE 4–8** ("80 lb stick", 5.5–6 ft) | 150–300 g, sometimes 350 g | 20–30 lb (30 lb the realistic ceiling) | **the pick for 200–350 ft** |
| **PE 6–10** ("100 lb stick", 5.5–6 ft) | 200 g, typically 250–500 g | 25–40 lb, a few claim 45 lb | if you're pushing 350–400 g in wind, or expecting cow grade |

Two selection rules that matter more than the printed range:
- **Pick the lightest rod that still activates the jig, not the heaviest one that can.** A 250 g jig that both a PE 4–8 and a PE 6–10 can technically move does noticeably better on the **PE 4–8** (`gimFbgN5Jjk`).
- **Buy toward the top of a rod's printed gram rating, not the middle.** A rod rated 100–300 g does not fish a 200 g jig well — it fishes best with 250–300 g. Stated example in `tackle/jig-rod-rating-selection.md` is almost exactly your case: a **200 g jig in 300–400 ft calls for a rod rated close to 200 g at the top of its range** (`DTvXJrtytwg`). Separately, blank **power** has to be soft enough to load and unload with that jig weight — a gram range can span the jig while the blank is too stiff to work it (`PbiZMk4Ft6w`).

Also: **spiral / acid-wrapped guides** are standard on these blanks — less side-to-side twist under load, and geometry that stops line grabbing the tip during the jigging stroke (`bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md`, `gimFbgN5Jjk`). A demoed speed-jig rod showed 55 kg (≈110 lb) of pulling power at a 60° rod angle despite a slow action — **fish it in hand, not flat on the rail; racking it throws away roughly half the rod's power** (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`; ⚠ the KB flags that capacity figure as single-source).

### Reel
**Any narrow two-speed — "narrow" meaning under 2 inches of spool width.** Wider spools let line dig into itself under the 15–30 lb of drag jigging commonly runs, and once it digs in it can't pay out cleanly (`techniques/knife-jigging.md`, `gimFbgN5Jjk`). Named benchmarks spanning the class: a **Shimano Talica 20** on the light end, valued for a free-spool that gets a jig down fast, and a **Penn International 50** with 130 lb line on the heavy end (`6_E5JCRvazc`). A **Penn Fathom** is called strong value — big, high-capacity, two-speed — with the caveat that the stock handle is thin enough to snap, so budget for an aftermarket one. The daytime seminar's budget one-rod answer is a **PE 4–8 rod with a Fathom 40, 80 or 100 lb Power Pro Max Quattro, 100 lb mono or fluoro leader at 30–40 ft, working 150–250 g jigs** (`G1YIlakiRqg`).

### Line
`techniques/knife-jigging.md` splits it by jig weight and daypart (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`, `ZrcwcugaEe4`):
- **200 g and lighter (your shallow end):** **65 lb braid** to a **100 lb mono top shot**; the light/day outfit is described as 65 lb braid to a 25 ft, 80 lb top shot.
- **350 g and up (your deep end):** **80 lb braid** to a **~15 ft, 130 lb mono** top shot.
- Thinner braid means less line angle in the water and a jig that hangs straighter; 65–100 lb covers the class, and much heavier braid is only worth it on the biggest jigs. A rod and reel can't generate enough drag to break 80 lb braid by pulling alone, so tangles aren't a reason to size up.

⚠ **Unreconciled conflict in the KB:** `species/bluefin-tuna.md` publishes the class as **"100–130 lb braid / PE6–8"** in both its gear summary and its sounded-deep row, above the 65–100 lb ceiling in the technique note — and the technique note's own fight examples name 100–130 lb braid as the *heavier comparison group* that put more scope and belly in the water. The KB flags this contradiction rather than resolving it. Other documented floors sit in between: 80–150 lb reasoned as sizing for the biggest fish that could show even though most landed fish graded 30–60 lb (`149px8WQ2Ng`), and 80 lb braid / 130 lb leader on one boat (`UfuiWFVvz2E`, `oB4BpIUTTl4`).

### Drag
- **Strike drag 19–20 lb.** The note is emphatic that online advice to run 35 lb strike drag is dangerous — a hookup at that setting can rip a jigging outfit clean out of your hands; one trip lost five outfits to it (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`).
- **Fighting drag 20–30 lb** at PE 4–8; 25–40 lb at PE 6–10 (`Et2TUskzCSs`).

## 4. Two execution details that decide whether the weight matters

- **Mark your line, or none of the above works.** Failing to mark line is called the single most common beginner mistake — a fish at 350 ft will not eat a jig worked at 250 or 550 ft (`UP_3fBu7VPA`). Use metered braid or Sharpie marks, and make each mark **12–18 inches long, not a dot** — line runs off fast enough on a drop that a short mark gets missed entirely (`fgTmUq78ofQ`). With no marks at all: a slow "Mississippi" count at roughly **1 second per 10 ft of fall** (`gimFbgN5Jjk`).

# Jig stick vs. rail rod

Both terms are **gear classes** in this KB, not brands or single models. Neither has its own note — both are defined as rows in the class lexicon at `tackle/gear-classes.md`, with the working detail spread across the technique notes that use them. Here is what the KB actually says.

---

## The jig stick (a.k.a. the surface-iron class)

**Spec** — `tackle/gear-classes.md` defines the **"Surface-iron / jig-stick class"** as **8–10 ft, casts 2–4 oz irons, moderate/fast action, ~25–60 lb**, application: [surface iron](techniques/surface-iron.md). The worked example is Cameron's Torium setup — a **Phenix Abyss PSX1009J, 10'0", 25–60 lb, moderate/fast**, deckhand cord-wrapped handle and clamp-on reel, Shimano Torium 20HG, **50 lb braid** (`profiles/cameron/rods.md`, cameron).

`tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md` gives the same row as an application map: **surface iron → 8–10 ft jig stick, moderate action, narrow high-speed conventional reel, 40–65 lb braid**.

`techniques/surface-iron.md` adds the build detail: **9–10 ft, medium-heavy, a tip soft enough to cast a heavy iron but with backbone**; reel a **narrow high-speed conventional (Tranx-500 class), thumb-controlled**; line **~80 lb braid main**, a short **50 lb mono/fluoro leader, ~18 in**, to a **100 lb clip** tied straight to the iron so you can swap irons without re-tying.

**What it is for — distance and swim.** The whole point of the length is the cast. "The distance IS the technique … the long rod is what buys that distance, and **the rod does all the work**" (`techniques/surface-iron.md`). Concretely:

- Hang the jig about a leader length below the tip (**~18 in**), take the rod back to **2 o'clock**, let the blank load, sweep through to **9 o'clock**.
- Build the swing with an **up-and-down pendulum, not a side-arm sling** — a longer rod gives a longer pendulum arc between tip and jig, hence more distance (Scotty, Brothers Sportfishing, via Roman Castro, `VpW91AKOFVQ`, 2020-01-28).
- Release on the **outside part of the swing** (`3dVc-2rsYII`), and **lay the reel on its side** through the cast so the spool rests on one bearing — stated payoff is a noticeably farther cast (`KLoEJInlmZo`).
- Retrieve with the **rod tip pointed down at the water** so the iron kicks side to side; tip-up pulls it onto the surface and it stops swimming (`VpW91AKOFVQ`). A high-gear jig-stick reel covers ~**42 in per crank** (Tranx-500-class HG), so you change cadence rather than reels.

**What it throws besides surface iron.** The same class is the yo-yo rod in heavier trim — `techniques/yo-yo-iron.md` calls for a **heavy jig-stick class with real backbone** (Cameron's Trinidad 40, 8'0" XH 40–80 lb, 80 lb braid). The weight difference is the tell: a surface iron runs **2–3 oz**, a yo-yo iron **5–6 oz** — "pick the two up and you can feel the difference" (`VpW91AKOFVQ`). It is also the popper/foamer rod for tuna in beefed-up form (`techniques/foamer-casting.md`, `species/bluefin-tuna.md`).

**Species it routes to** (from the router gear summaries): yellowtail (**9–10 ft jig stick**, `species/yellowtail.md`), calico bass (`species/calico-bass.md`), barracuda and bonito — including a downsized "light casting" end of the spectrum (`species/barracuda.md`, `species/bonito.md`), white seabass (**7–9 ft, 40–50 lb**, `species/white-seabass.md`), and yellowfin/bluefin foamers on a beefed version.

**Length caveats the KB carries side by side:**
- Cesar (Eric's Tackle, `F7jLrt2j2X8`) caps casting rods at **9 ft for safety** — 8 ft is fine, longer than 9 ft is dangerous to cast, especially for anglers not on the bow.
- For a **Baja panga**, Cesar prefers **8 ft, 20–50 lb** over the "purist" 9 ft standard, because a 9–10 ft rod makes it hard to work a fish close enough for the **4–5 ft gaffs** typically carried aboard (`M8hOYQ_6rSg`, `tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md`).
- One Cedros angler's favorite was an **8 ft extra-heavy, 30–80 lb** stick chosen specifically *for* casting distance (`u0scEBby7nA`) — kept beside, not replacing, the 9–10 ft class.

**Line-class note.** `tackle/line-and-leader.md` (Academy Ep16, `aXF0bxAFtU0`) says fish the lightest braid the job allows — mostly **30 or 50 lb, up to 65 on a jig stick**, and **65–80 for casting at 100 lb-class bluefin**. And when the bait shrinks, the tackle shrinks with it: you can't throw a Tady AA-light on the 80 lb jig stick — that downsized program is a **medium-action 9 ft graphite-composite stick, smaller narrow reel, ~50 lb braid** to a short fluoro leader (`pcwcRdmWmLc`, `techniques/surface-iron.md`).

## The rail rod (a.k.a. the heavy tuna class)

**Spec** — `tackle/gear-classes.md` defines the **"Rail-rod / heavy tuna class"** as **~8 ft, 3X-H, 60–130 lb, 2-speed reel**, application: **casting/fighting jumbo [bluefin](species/bluefin-tuna.md)**. Worked example: Cameron's **Phenix Axis HAX-C 800X3H — 8'0", 3X-H, 60–130 lb**, with a **cross-cut 4-slot aluminum gimbal butt**, Shimano **Talica 25II 2-speed**, **100 lb braid**. His role note: main heavy setup, commonly trolling, and **the rod he'd cast at a jumbo 80–140 lb bluefin foamer school** (`profiles/cameron/rods.md`, cameron).

**The full build** — `tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md`, from the top-5 offshore framework (`xPFm_ZV2PZU`): "Big bluefin cast/fight → **rail rod (2x4) / 50–100 lb; HD30-size 2-speed; 130 lb braid**", with a **Bimini or splice to a [wind-on leader](rigging/wind-on-leader.md), 150 lb blue label (~20–25 ft), crimped to a snap swivel.** Kite flyer rigs off the same rung step to **300 lb line with a 10/0–11/0 J hook and an extra-heavy treble**.

**Terminal connection** — `rigging/essential-knots.md` names the **Bimini twist** as the standing double-line ahead of a topshot **on rail-rod big-tuna setups**, and the **worm knot** as the specific knot that ties that mono topshot onto the already-tied Bimini loop — its other selling point being fast field repair, since the Bimini's twisted section survives when the knot is cut away.

**What it is for — leverage on 100–300 lb fish.** `techniques/fighting-big-bluefin.md` describes the rail-braced style this class serves, and it is emphatically **not** a belt-and-harness style:

- The rod's butt goes **under the angler's left armpit**, reel hand on the handle, and the angler **squats down and stands up, using the boat as leverage** — wind down while standing up on the back of a swell, don't wind while the swell is lifting the boat. **No fighting belt or harness is used** — coached live as "you're off the belt." That operation's rail hardware is called **"wrap system pipes"**; the reel on those outfits is an **Okuma Makaira 50 Wide** (`usHl-4SfqDA`, 2024-11-13, NE of Santa Barbara Island).
- The same source names the whole style **"railroading"** — "lock the rod to the rail and let your body in the boat do the work" (`mDmbGdQAy-4`). ⚠ The KB keeps a **second, narrower sense of the same word** side by side, unreconciled: on `xzIaUEDklrE` "railroad it" means only the final hand-fought stretch once the fish comes straight up and down under the boat, after ~80% of the fight was won by bumping the boat in and out of gear.
- Third data point, same style: **kneel, rod butt under the left armpit, left hand on the rod** (`vCskOx6N-XM`, Tanner Bank).
- Competing doctrine in the same note: on one program the rod **stays in the gunnel holder** and the boat does the work entirely — hand-fighting a big bluefin in a harness (**30–40 lb of drag**) is called a personal-endurance choice, not a functional advantage. Either way that program runs **130 lb braid minimum**.

`techniques/knife-jigging.md` uses the rail rod as the explicit heavy reference point: a short parabolic jigging rod "gives leverage to fight a big fish off a light rod **with less fatigue than a rail rod**."

**Where over-gunning bites you.** A real caution on record: Ray Sharifi found a **60–130 lb-rated** jig stick (Black Diamond Hybrid) ran **too stiff on 40–50 lb-class bluefin** — it wants to stay straight and is hard to keep a consistent bend in through the fight — while on **80 lb-and-up fish** the stiffness "worked to our benefit" (`-MP2RqJC7B0`, `tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md`). Read that as: the rail class is the wrong tool for schoolie and mid-grade fish even though it's the right one when the grade goes big.

**One 2-speed caveat.** Dave Hansen's point in `tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md` (`o8vLdz7OmaE`): a two-speed lever drag is "almost impossible" to fine-tune mid-battle, unlike a star drag you can click one notch at a time — the workaround on lever-drag gear is the hand-pull drag re-test through the fight.

## Which to reach for

- **Anything where the cast is the technique** — yellowtail boiling or on a paddy, calico along the kelp line, barracuda/bonito on top, tuna foamers — is the jig stick. Longer buys distance; go heavier iron (Salas 7X class) when it's windy, downsize the whole outfit when the bait is small anchovy.
- **Fish holding deep or in current too strong for the surface** — same class, heavy trim, as a yo-yo rod with a 5–6 oz iron.
- **A jumbo bluefin** — the rail rod, 100–130 lb braid, 2-speed, gimbal butt, either braced on the rail beltless or left in the gunnel holder while the boat gear-taps the fish in. Do not bring it to a schoolie bite.

## Short answer

They are two different **tools solving two different problems**, and the KB defines each by the physical feature that makes it work:

- A **jig stick** is a long casting rod — its job is **distance and lure presentation**. The length is the point.
- A **rail rod** is a short, heavy **fighting** rod with an extra-long padded foregrip — its job is **leverage against the boat's rail** on a fish too big to hold up unsupported. The foregrip is the point.

You cast with one and you grind with the other. A jig stick will not brace on a rail (and laying its blank on one breaks rods); a rail rod is not what you throw a Tady 45 with.

---

## The jig stick

**Class definition** (`tackle/gear-classes.md`): "Surface-iron / jig-stick class — **8–10 ft, casts 2–4 oz irons, moderate/fast, ~25–60 lb**." The application map in `tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md` gives it as **8–10 ft, moderate, narrow high-speed conventional, 40–65 lb braid**.

**Length — where the KB's sources actually land:**
- Dan Wade (registered voice, `yn1suHH6b1Y`, via `techniques/surface-iron.md`): *"An **8-ft jig stick is a yo-yo rod** — it's not a jig stick. **9-ft is okay. 10-ft is better.**"*
- Doug Kearn, Fisherman's Landing counter (`3g82igEL8yk`, `tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md`): **no less than 9 ft to be "in the game," 10 ft preferred** — stated reason is casting leverage, because fish don't always come close enough to reach on a shorter rod. The same voice in a calico-specific clip (`r9bF3VtzDUg`) allows **8, 9, or 10 ft** for the lighter calico-grade irons; the KB keeps those two side by side rather than merging them.

**Blank and action** (`techniques/surface-iron.md`):
- **All-fiberglass** (example: Calstar 90J) loads more easily and "can really swing into pretty far" — the go-to build for surface iron. A **composite** (example: Shimano Terramar 90) is stiffer and is called the better tool for working poppers/stick baits (`sjlL5GidM58`, medium confidence).
- **S-glass does what E-glass does at ~20% less weight** with more backbone — relevant when you're "on a boat 12–14 hours making 300 casts" (`yn1suHH6b1Y`; the note flags the brand framing as promotional and keeps only the mechanism).
- Many jig sticks are **deckhand-style with no fixed reel seat** — the reel is held by a **clamp** so you can position it where you want (`BNp-gSOpWBw` / `_C7TJoGT0nw`, `tackle/tackle-express-jig-stick-trolling-outfit.md`; also the 10 ft Seeker build in `tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md`, `49joKHD7Umc`).

**Reel and line — three documented builds:**

| Build | Rod | Reel | Line |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Counter standard (`3g82igEL8yk`) | 9–10 ft | mid-size **star-drag** conventional (Torium 20 class) | **65 lb spectra + ~100 yd of 40 lb mono** top shot; step to **50 lb** for bluefin |
| Traditional (Steve Carson, `Y_ElKixrhsc`) | **9 ft, 25–50 lb** | Penn Fathom 25 narrow star drag — *"star drags definitely cast better than the lever drags"* | **100 yd of 40 lb mono** |
| "Younger set" baitcast (same source) | 9–10 ft | low-profile saltwater baitcast (Fathom 400 class) | **50 lb braid** to a **4–5 ft, 40 lb mono** leader (leader short enough that the knot never passes the tip on the cast) |

Also in `tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md` (`49joKHD7Umc`): a 10 ft stick spooled **30–60 lb, most often 40 lb** — the stated reason for 40 is that it lets you run a **snugger drag**, which helps the hookset. Budget version: **9 ft, single-speed star drag, straight 40 lb mono, no spectra backing**.

**Reel ratio is a live disagreement in the KB, not settled doctrine** (`techniques/surface-iron.md`): the irons were designed around a **Penn Jigmaster at 4:1**, and **6.5–7.5:1 reels are called "too fast."** A Tackle Express counter clip (`pOFsjDTqaxY`) argues a **7:1** baitcaster is a torque/versatility middle ground. Both are kept side by side. Spool diameter is the other half of it — a taller spool picks up more line per turn, so a nominally slower ratio can still be right.

**What it's for** (`techniques/surface-iron.md` "Reach for this when"): yellowtail boiling or on a kelp paddy, surface-feeding calico and sand bass, barracuda and bonito on top, and bluefin foamers when you want a bigger/faster presentation than a plug. Jig weight: a surface iron runs **2–3 oz** against a yo-yo iron's **5–6 oz** (`VpW91AKOFVQ`, high confidence); Kearn gives **1¾–2¾ oz, 5–7 in** for the jigs themselves.

**The rod follows the jig down, too:** on small anchovy ("rice") bait you drop to a Tady AA-light/A1-light class iron — and *"you can't throw an AA-light on the 80 lb jig stick."* The downsized outfit is a **medium-action 9 ft graphite-composite stick, a smaller narrow reel, ~50 lb braid** to a short fluoro leader (`pcwcRdmWmLc`).

**Secondary job:** a jig stick doubles as a light trolling outfit — an 8 ft Calstar with a 20-size reel, **65 lb braid, close to 400 yd**, run **clicker on with the drag backed off a couple of clicks**, then on the bite **advance the drag two to three clicks and kill the clicker** (`BNp-gSOpWBw`, Cedros yellowtail, medium confidence — `tackle/tackle-express-jig-stick-trolling-outfit.md`).

## The rail rod

**Class definition** (`tackle/gear-classes.md`): "Rail-rod / heavy tuna class — **~8 ft, 3X-H, 60–130 lb, 2-speed reel**," for **casting/fighting jumbo bluefin**.

**What physically makes it a rail rod** (`tackle/gear-classes.md`, "Rail-rod class — what makes a rod a rail rod"): a **longer foregrip section wrapped in hypalon**, long enough that the rod can be **laid on and braced against the boat's rail** instead of being carried entirely by the angler's arms and body — *"the rail is your friend."* On the Seeker SSR 7650 that's quantified as **6–8 in of extra foregrip** whose stated purpose is "to lay this rod on the rail" once you're hooked up (`aAqKHeyBwEo`). It is **paired with a two-speed reel, not a single-speed** (`Y2XZ34-Tpa0`).

**The hard rule that goes with it** (`tackle/searcher-rail-rod-ladder.md`, `sjOJiR6_HJ4`): **put the rubber on the rail, never the blank** — putting the blank on the rail breaks the rod.

**Length is the reverse of a jig stick's:** **don't go shorter than 7 ft**, and the 1980s/90s-era **5 ft 2 in – 6 ft stand-up trolling rods** are called out by name — "put them away, sell them on eBay." A bigger/stronger angler goes **longer** (7 ft 3 in or 7 ft 6 in), not shorter. Note the class shorthand in `tackle/gear-classes.md` says "~8 ft" while every named Searcher model sits at **7 ft to 7 ft 8 in** — I'm carrying both rather than reconciling them.

**The four-tier ladder by line rating** (Steve Carson, `sjOJiR6_HJ4`; named models are low confidence per the sponsored-claim rule, the parameters medium):

| Tier | Rod | Line to fish | Reel | Grade |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 — lightest | **7 ft 8 in, 40–100 lb**, a little more tip action | **60 lb** (50 or 80 fine) | — | live sardine/mackerel: flyline, dropshot, sinker rig |
| 2 | "Carnage 3," **7 ft, 60–100 lb**, extra foregrip reinforcement | **80 lb** | — | tuna to **~225 lb** |
| 3 — "most popular on the Searcher" | **"8130," 7 ft, 80–130 lb** | **100 lb** | Penn International 16VISX class | tuna to **~300 lb** |
| 4 | **80–150 lb model** | **100 or 130 lb** top shots | Penn International 20, "or even a 30" | **350–400 lb** tuna |

**General rod rule stated at every tier (high confidence, repeated doctrine):** throw out the top and bottom of a rod's printed **line** rating and fish as close to the **middle** as you can. (Careful — `tackle/jig-rod-rating-selection.md` documents the *opposite* rule for a dedicated jig rod's **gram** rating: buy toward the **top** of the gram range, e.g. a 100–300 g rod actually fishes 250–300 g jigs, not 200 g. Different printed parameter, different rod class; the KB keeps them side by side precisely because generalizing one to the other gets you backwards.)

**Other rail rods in the corpus:** the **Seeker OSP 1x3**, 7 ft composite, given as **60–100 lb** and nicknamed "the Guadalupe special" (`aAqKHeyBwEo`), and as **80–100 lb** on a 6-to-8-day trip (`MPC_OQjvO-o`); paired with a **Penn Fathom 60 two-speed** for flat-fall bluefin (`an_uw-5pjfw`). One size up, the **Seeker OSP 2x4**: **80–130 lb, 7 ft 3 in composite**, heavy-duty guides, named for the sinker rig, the knife jig at night, and flylining big fish on **10-, 16-, and 18-day** trips (`scScYJJF95Y`).

**How it's actually fought** (`techniques/fighting-fish-from-the-rail.md`): tuck the butt under the **left armpit** (right-handed angler), lay the rod on the rail, keep a hand on top of or against the reel to stop it twisting, **stay in low gear**, and **lift with body weight / wind down**, repeat — not cranking with the arms. Watch the **rod tip**, not the fish: wind while the tip is up, stop when it loads back down (`scScYJJF95Y`). The failure mode it exists to correct is **high-sticking** — holding the rod up unsupported. It's framed as necessary for everyone except "NFL linemen"; a rail rod exists because the rail does the work your back otherwise would.

# Jig stick vs. rail rod

Two SoCal-specific rod classes that sit at opposite ends of the same job list. A **jig stick is a casting tool** — length buys distance so you can reach fish that won't come to the boat. A **rail rod is a lifting tool** — it is short, stiff, and built with a long padded foregrip so the boat's rail carries the load of a big fish instead of your arms. Neither substitutes for the other.

Both are defined in the KB's class lexicon, [`tackle/gear-classes.md`](tackle/gear-classes.md), which is where a plan resolves "reach for the jig-stick class" against what you actually own.

---

## The jig stick (surface-iron class)

**What it is.** An 8–10 ft casting rod, moderate/fast, rated roughly **25–60 lb**, with a tip soft enough to load and throw a 2–4 oz iron but enough backbone to fight the fish that eat it ([`tackle/gear-classes.md`](tackle/gear-classes.md)). The length is not style — it's the mechanism. The cast is a **pendulum**: you build the swing by moving the rod tip up and down, and a longer rod gives a longer pendulum arc between tip and jig, which is where the distance comes from (`VpW91AKOFVQ`, [`techniques/surface-iron-casting.md`](techniques/surface-iron-casting.md)).

**Length doctrine, stated bluntly in the corpus:** *"An 8-ft jig stick is a yo-yo rod — it's not a jig stick. 9-ft is okay. 10-ft is better"* (`yn1suHH6b1Y`, [`techniques/surface-iron.md`](techniques/surface-iron.md)). A second source sets the floor the same way — **no less than 9 ft to be "in the game," 10 ft preferred**, because the longer rod throws the jig farther from the boat and fish don't always come close enough to reach on a shorter stick (`3g82igEL8yk`, [`tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md`](tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md)).

**What it's for.**
- [Surface iron](techniques/surface-iron.md) — long-cast a **2–3 oz** iron and swim it back on top, rod tip **down**, so the iron kicks side to side; the kick-out is the trigger, not a side effect (`VpW91AKOFVQ`, `yn1suHH6b1Y`, `Ec2GaNpiOHI`).
- Yellowtail boiling or on a paddy, surface-feeding calico and sand bass, barracuda, bonito, and bluefin foamers ([`techniques/surface-iron.md`](techniques/surface-iron.md)).
- It also doubles as a **trolling outfit** — 8 ft Calstar blank + 20-size reel, 65 lb braid for close to **400 yd**; troll with the clicker on and drag backed off a couple of clicks, then on the bite **advance the drag 2–3 clicks and kill the clicker** to convert to fighting mode (`BNp-gSOpWBw`, [`tackle/tackle-express-jig-stick-trolling-outfit.md`](tackle/tackle-express-jig-stick-trolling-outfit.md)).

**Reel and line — this is where it differs most from a rail rod.** The standard is a **mid-sized star-drag conventional** (Torium 20 class); *"the star drags definitely cast better than the lever drags"* (`Y_ElKixrhsc`, `3g82igEL8yk`). Gear ratio is deliberately **slow**: the irons were designed around the **4:1 Penn Jigmaster**, and 6.5–7.5:1 reels are called too fast for them (`Ec2GaNpiOHI`). Working line builds from the corpus, kept side by side rather than merged:

**Blank material matters here.** All-fiberglass (Calstar 90J class) loads easily and "can really swing into pretty far" — the classic surface-iron build; a graphite/glass composite (Teramar 90 class) is stiffer and is the better popper/stickbait tool, giving "a lot more response out of your sweep" (`sjlL5GidM58`). S-glass does what E-glass does at **~20% less weight**, which matters over 300 casts in a 12–14 hour day (`yn1suHH6b1Y`).

Set the reel position by measuring from the **crook of your elbow to the tip of your middle finger**, within about an inch (`49joKHD7Umc`) — too high and your off hand can't get in front of the reel to pull on a fish; too low and you lose casting leverage ([`techniques/surface-iron-casting.md`](techniques/surface-iron-casting.md)). Flagged in the KB as single-source.

## The rail rod (heavy-tuna class)

**What it is — the actual mechanism.** A rail rod's defining physical difference from a conventional casting/fighting rod is a **longer foregrip section wrapped in hypalon** — long enough that the rod lays on and braces against the boat's rail instead of being held entirely by your arms and body. It is paired with a **two-speed reel**, not a single-speed (`Y2XZ34-Tpa0`, [`tackle/gear-classes.md`](tackle/gear-classes.md)). Some models add double reinforcement at the foregrip, because you rub it against the rail hard (`sjOJiR6_HJ4`).

**Put the foregrip on the rail, never the blank — blank-on-rail breaks the rod** (`sjOJiR6_HJ4`, [`tackle/searcher-rail-rod-ladder.md`](tackle/searcher-rail-rod-ladder.md)).

**Length runs the other direction from a jig stick: 7 ft is the floor**, explicitly to retire the 5 ft 2 in–6 ft stand-up trolling rods of the 1980s–90s ("put them away, sell them on eBay") — the stated reason for the floor is moving around the boat and around other anglers. A bigger or stronger angler sizes **up**, to 7 ft 3 in or 7 ft 6 in (`sjOJiR6_HJ4`). The class lexicon lists the class as **~8 ft, 3X-H, 60–130 lb, 2-speed reel** ([`tackle/gear-classes.md`](tackle/gear-classes.md)).

**The four-tier ladder by line rating** ([`tackle/searcher-rail-rod-ladder.md`](tackle/searcher-rail-rod-ladder.md), all `sjOJiR6_HJ4`):

| Tier | Rod | Line | Reel / bait | Grade ceiling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 — livebait | 7 ft 8 in, 40–100 lb | 60 lb (50 or 80 also work) | live sardine/mackerel; flyline, dropper, sinker rig; more tip action than the tiers below | — |
| 2 | "Carnage 3," 7 ft, 60–100 lb | 80 lb | large live baits or medium jigs | ~**225 lb** tuna |
| 3 — the one to own | "8130," 7 ft, 80–130 lb | 100 lb | Penn International 16 class | ~**300 lb** tuna — "if you don't have anything else, have this one" |
| 4 | 80–150 lb model | 100 or 130 lb top shots | Penn International 20, "or even a 30" | framed at **350–400 lb** |

**Fish the middle of the printed line rating**, not the ends — 60 lb on a 40–100 rod, 80 lb on a 60–100, 100 lb on an 80–130 — "that's where you're going to get the best performance" (`sjOJiR6_HJ4`).

**What it's for — the fight, and how you actually use it.** Casting to and fighting jumbo bluefin ([`tackle/gear-classes.md`](tackle/gear-classes.md)). The technique it exists to serve is in [`techniques/fighting-fish-from-the-rail.md`](techniques/fighting-fish-from-the-rail.md):

- Tuck the rod butt under the **left** armpit (for a right-handed angler) — there's a natural pull to tuck right, and the coaching is explicitly against it (`6wVlDDno2TE`).
- If the fish is down below, **lay the rod directly on the rail** — this is the part that requires the long foregrip.
- Put a hand on top of or on the side of the reel to counter the **twisting** load.
- **Stay in low gear** on the two-speed and lift with whole-body weight, wind down, repeat — not arm/wrist cranking (`6wVlDDno2TE`, [`techniques/two-speed-low-gear-fight.md`](techniques/two-speed-low-gear-fight.md) is the reel-side counterpart).
- Cue for when to wind: **watch the rod tip.** As the boat rocks and the fish loads the rod, the tip goes down; when it comes back up, wind. When it loads and drops again, stop (`scScYJJF95Y`, demoed on a Seeker OSP 2x4, 7 ft 3 in composite, 80–130 lb).
- The rail is stated as necessary for anyone fighting **100–300 lb+** tuna except anglers strong enough to fight unsupported — "the rest of us normal humans have to use the rail" (`sjOJiR6_HJ4`). Baseline phrasing across the corpus, attributed to Captain Art of the Searcher: *"the rail is your friend"* (`0xJqOtkmHqY`, `Y2XZ34-Tpa0`).
- Historical footnote: around **2001**, IGFA world-record rules disqualified a catch if the angler touched the rail, which pushed anglers off the rail for a stretch; by 2021 "everybody's using the rail again." Fighting belts are still common; shoulder harnesses are now rare — cumbersome, and getting in and out of one costs time at exactly the wrong moment (`fgTmUq78ofQ`).

## Side by side

| | Jig stick | Rail rod |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary job** | Deliver a lure a long way, then swim it | Lift and beat a big fish, braced on the boat |
| **Length** | 8–10 ft (lexicon); 9 ft floor, 10 ft better in practice | 7 ft floor, up to ~7 ft 8 in; lexicon lists ~8 ft |
| **Line class** | ~25–60 lb | 40–150 lb across the four tiers; ~60–130 lb typical |
| **Action** | Moderate/fast; tip soft enough to load a 2–4 oz iron | 3X-H and up; stiff, built to lift |
| **Defining feature** | Length (pendulum arc = casting distance); often no fixed reel seat | Long hypalon foregrip that lays on the rail; sometimes double-reinforced |
| **Reel** | Mid-size star-drag conventional, deliberately slow ratio (4:1 design intent) | Two-speed lever drag, fished in **low gear** |
| **Typical line** | 65–80 lb braid + 100 yd of 40–50 lb mono top shot | 60–130 lb, fished at the middle of the rod's rating |
| **Target** | Yellowtail, calico, sand bass, barracuda, bonito, bluefin foamers | Bluefin from ~200 lb to 350–400 lb |
| **Notes** | [`techniques/surface-iron.md`](techniques/surface-iron.md), [`techniques/surface-iron-casting.md`](techniques/surface-iron-casting.md) | [`techniques/fighting-fish-from-the-rail.md`](techniques/fighting-fish-from-the-rail.md), [`tackle/searcher-rail-rod-ladder.md`](tackle/searcher-rail-rod-ladder.md) |

The short version of the trade-off: **the jig stick's length is casting leverage you hold in your hands; the rail rod's shortness plus long foregrip is fighting leverage you borrow from the boat.** A 10 ft jig stick has nothing to brace on a rail and no blank stiffness to lift a 250 lb fish; a 7 ft rail rod has no pendulum arc and no tip to load a 2 oz iron.

## The boundary case in between

The rod between the two is the **yo-yo / heavy-iron class** — ~8 ft, XH, 40–80 lb, fast crank ([`tackle/gear-classes.md`](tackle/gear-classes.md)). That is exactly why the corpus insists "an 8-ft jig stick is a yo-yo rod, it's not a jig stick" (`yn1suHH6b1Y`): 8 ft with heavy power is the [yo-yo iron](techniques/yo-yo-iron.md) tool, not the surface-iron tool. Weight tells you which iron goes on which: a surface iron runs **2–3 oz**, a yo-yo iron **5–6 oz** — you can feel the difference picking them up (`VpW91AKOFVQ`).

**Your own box makes the boundary concrete** ([`profiles/cameron/rods.md`](profiles/cameron/rods.md)):
- **Setup 5, the Torium** — Phenix Abyss 1009J, **10'0", 25–60 lb, moderate/fast**, deckhand cord-wrapped handle and clamp-on reel, Torium 20HG, 50 lb braid. That is a textbook jig stick, listed as your full surface-iron setup.
- **Setup 6, the Talica 25** — Phenix Axis 800X3H, **8'0", 3X-H, 60–130 lb**, gimbal butt, Talica 25II two-speed, 100 lb braid. Listed as rail-rod class, and the rod you'd cast at an **80–140 lb** bluefin foamer.
- **Setup 1, the Trinidad 40** — 8'0" XH 40–80 lb — is the yo-yo/heavy stick in between, and your own note on it says it "can cast large surface irons but not well." That's the length/power boundary above, in one line.

## Where the KB is thin or disagrees with itself

- The class lexicon says the jig-stick class is **8–10 ft**; [`techniques/surface-iron.md`](techniques/surface-iron.md) says 9–10 ft and that 8 ft is a yo-yo rod; and the white seabass router calls the same class **7–9 ft, 40–50 lb**. The KB carries this as a flagged internal contradiction rather than resolving it — treat 9–10 ft as the working number for throwing iron.
- Similarly, rail-rod length: the lexicon says ~8 ft, the Searcher ladder says 7 ft floor to 7 ft 8 in. Both are in the KB; neither is retracted.
- Confidence: the four-tier rail-rod ladder is **medium** confidence — it rests almost entirely on one video (`sjOJiR6_HJ4`), and the named models (Penn Carnage 3, the "8130," the Penn Internationals) carry a sponsored-claim caveat because the presenter is a Penn rep. The presenter also hedges on camera about the printed 60–100 lb figure itself ([`tackle/evidence/searcher-rail-rod-ladder.md`](tackle/evidence/searcher-rail-rod-ladder.md)).
