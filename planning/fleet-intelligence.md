# Fleet Intelligence

How to treat what the rest of the fleet is doing — VHF chatter and AIS/vessel
tracking — as a planning input, and why the two are not equally trustworthy.
This sits alongside your own search, not in place of it: see
[search and glassing](search-and-glassing.md) for the on-the-water method and
[electronics and sounder](electronics-and-sounder.md) for finding fish
yourself once you're there.

## VHF channel discipline

- Monitor **channel 72 or channel 65** while fishing offshore SoCal — that's
  where the fleet's actual chatter happens (`8XrMkWPRxgs`, `9hEa3sGTh40`).
- A direct call for help ("out of bait") gets answered straight, on the
  working channel. But what a boat is actually doing — where they're on
  fish — never goes out over VHF at all, even on that same channel: it runs
  over single sideband (SSB), through a private dispatch, off the public
  channels anglers monitor entirely (`8XrMkWPRxgs`).
- Channel 16 is for hailing and distress, not a duty to monitor all day
  hoping to be the one who runs to a rescue — that's the Coast Guard's or a
  vessel-assist operator's job, not a private boat's. The exception is an
  immediate, visible emergency right in front of you (a boat on fire, people
  waving for help): help directly rather than waiting on radio protocol
  (`9hEa3sGTh40`).
- Anglers who are actually on fish don't get on the radio to call the rest
  of the fleet over to their own bite. Read an unprompted "wide open" call
  against that baseline, not at face value (`9hEa3sGTh40`).

## Radio misinformation

**Doctrine: turn the VHF radio off, or at minimum weight your own pre-trip
research and your own sign over an unsolicited "hot bite" call from a
stranger.** A radio report costs the sender nothing to fake and directly
benefits them if it works — pulling competing boats off a real spot while the
source of the call keeps fishing it alone (`9hEa3sGTh40`).

The pressure pattern to recognize: an increasingly urgent "wide open" report
at a different spot, escalating over several exchanges (running low on bait,
"the boat's sinking" from too many fish) to force a course change away from
water you already researched (`9hEa3sGTh40`).

One captain's version of the tactic, described from the transmitting end: to
shake a private boat that keeps closing on him, he repeats a fabricated
hot-bite report — "at the dump at Doheny" — five times on **channel 72**,
then repeats the same fake report on **channel 65** (the other channel
recreational boats default to) until the shadowing boat takes off after it
(`8XrMkWPRxgs`). The trick works because he already answers every call that
comes over the channel, real information or not, when bored — so a
fabricated report from him reads exactly like any other exchange
(`8XrMkWPRxgs`).

## Visual boat-chasing costs you your own search

**Doctrine: go fishing for fish, not for boats.** This is the single most
common mistake anglers make offshore — making finding the fleet the first
goal of the day instead of fishing (`8XrMkWPRxgs`). Climbing up top once
it's light enough to see and scanning for **sport boats** instead of fish,
then running at whichever one looks stopped, costs the same way the
radio-chatter trap does — attention spent reading the fleet instead of your
own water. A cluster of sport boats parked on one spot is usually nothing
more than one boat stopping and the others following it over, not
independent confirmation that the spot beats the sign you drove past to get
there. Even a boat that's visibly hooked up doesn't help you: the fish are
keyed on bait falling off *that* boat's transom, not on your boat sitting 25
yards off its side (`8XrMkWPRxgs`, `ZFqe49jRgA0`). The habit runs deep enough
that anglers ask to borrow a captain's gyro-stabilized binoculars — $5,000 a
pair — just to watch someone else catch fish instead of fishing their own
water (`8XrMkWPRxgs`).

One documented run lost the whole leg watching a single stopped boat and
drove past an unworked [kelp paddy](../conditions/kelp-paddies.md) and
breezing tuna along the way (`8XrMkWPRxgs`) — full account in the
[evidence file](evidence/fleet-intelligence.md).

The same doctrine restated at chart-reading altitude: a cluster of boats
sitting on water is a reason to look elsewhere, not a reason to run there —
the goal is clean water with no boats on it, not just avoiding a crowded
channel (`JiJYamo0QHk`). Pairs with the same talk's chlorophyll trend-reading
method in [water color](../conditions/water-color.md).

## Fleet position as a data layer

Reading **where boats are**, rather than what they say over the radio, is
harder to fake than a voice call — the practical reason to lean on it over
radio chatter when both are available.

- **AIS sportboat tracking at the Coronado Islands.** Stop/start patterns on
  sportboat AIS (e.g. the Grande, MMSI 366746210) are one of the most
  valuable data sources for learning the islands; correlating stops against
  tide, wind, SST, chlorophyll, and current is a research direction, not yet
  built out (cameron).
- **Bait-boat tracking.** Watching Everingham's bait boats on MarineTraffic
  shows when the San Diego Bay / Mission Bay bait receivers get refilled
  during bait shortages (cameron); see [making bait](../bait/making-bait.md)
  for the buy side this feeds.
- **VHF ch 72 offshore monitoring on busy weekends** also picks up Navy
  ship-to-ship traffic on the same channel, with the same ships/operators
  recurring week to week — situational awareness, not a fishing signal
  (cameron).
- **Radar** doubles as a fleet-scouting tool while running a search pattern:
  running the boat's radar while searching the 14 Mile Bank / 209 / 267
  complex off San Diego showed a boat parked on a kelp paddy on the radar
  screen before getting close enough for that boat to notice the approach —
  a paddy-holding boat read as a radar return rather than picked out by eye
  first (`-bw1KDfDjv4`). No range or radar-mode detail is given beyond the
  set being on.
