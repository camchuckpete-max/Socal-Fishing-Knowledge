# Fleet Intelligence

How to treat what the rest of the fleet is doing — VHF chatter and AIS/vessel
tracking — as a planning input, and why the two are not equally trustworthy.
This sits alongside your own search, not in place of it: see
[search and glassing](search-and-glassing.md) for the on-the-water method and
[electronics and sounder](electronics-and-sounder.md) for finding fish
yourself once you're there.

## VHF channel discipline (dave-hansen; registered voice, repeated doctrine — high confidence)

- **Working channels:** monitor **channel 72 or channel 65** while fishing
  offshore SoCal — that's where the fleet's actual chatter happens. Stated
  twice by the same voice: a live seminar roughly five months earlier than
  the tutorial below gives the identical pair of channels ("you guys only
  know two channels, 72 and 65, that's it") — "**channels 72/65**" is
  therefore **repeated doctrine** (Capt. Dave Hansen, live seminar, Fred Hall
  Show 2019, filmed by Utah Spahghettzi Björker, `8XrMkWPRxgs`, 2019-03-12;
  restated Your Saltwater Guide, `9hEa3sGTh40`, 2019-08-20).
- **Direct help vs. operational info are handled differently.** Hansen says
  he'll personally answer straightforward calls for help — "you call me on
  seven if you're out of bait" — on his own channel. But *what the boat is
  actually doing* (i.e. where they're on fish) is never shared on VHF at
  all, even on that same channel: "you don't talk on seven about what we're
  doing" — that runs over **single sideband (SSB)**, through a private
  dispatch, "like a telephone," so it stays off the public VHF channels
  anglers monitor entirely (`8XrMkWPRxgs`, 2019-03-12).
- **Channel 16 isn't a duty to take on.** Ch 16 is for hailing/distress, but
  Hansen's doctrine is not to spend a fishing day monitoring it hoping to be
  the one who runs to save somebody — that's the Coast Guard's or vessel
  assist's job, not a private boat's. The exception is an immediate, visible
  emergency right in front of you (a boat on fire, people waving for help) —
  help directly rather than waiting on radio protocol. (Single mention,
  9hEa3sGTh40 — medium confidence on this specific point.)
- **Anglers who are actually on fish don't broadcast it.** The baseline to
  judge radio chatter against: getting on the radio to call the rest of the
  fleet over to your own bite isn't something anglers do. An unprompted "wide
  open" call over the radio should be read with that baseline in mind, not
  taken at face value (see misinformation below). (Single mention,
  9hEa3sGTh40 — medium confidence on this specific point.)

## Radio misinformation — treat unsolicited fleet reports as unverified (dave-hansen, 9hEa3sGTh40, 2019-08-20; registered voice, single mention — medium confidence)

Hansen states directly that he has used the radio to mislead competing boats
off his own program, and walks through the mechanic: while running to a spot
identified from his own prior research (e.g. a fish-report site), another
voice comes on the radio describing an increasingly urgent "wide open" bite
at a different spot — the urgency escalating over several exchanges (running
low on bait, "the boat's sinking" from too many fish) to pressure a course
change. The report can be entirely fabricated, sent specifically to pull
competing boats off the real spot while the source of the call keeps running
there themselves.

**Doctrine: turn the VHF radio off, or at minimum weight your own pre-trip
research and your own sign over an unsolicited "hot bite" call from a
stranger.** A radio report costs the sender nothing to fake and directly
benefits them if it works.

**A worked, self-admitted example of the same tactic, told at an earlier live
seminar** (Capt. Dave Hansen, Fred Hall Show 2019, filmed by Utah Spahghettzi
Björker, `8XrMkWPRxgs`, 2019-03-12): Hansen describes doing this himself when
bored on his own boat, monitoring ch 72 for private boats fixated on
following his sportboat. If a boat keeps closing on him, he gets on **channel
72** and repeats fabricated numbers for a hot bite "at the dump at Doheny"
**five times**; if the boat keeps coming, he repeats the same fake report on
**channel 65** (the other channel recreational boats default to) until it
works — "you take off like the boat's on fire." Stated explicitly as a joke he
plays on private boats that chase his sportboat rather than fishing their own
water, and the mechanism behind the doctrine above: he "answers every call"
on 72 when bored, but not with real information.

## Visual boat-chasing costs you your own search (dave-hansen; registered voice, repeated doctrine — high confidence)

Restated across two dated sources by the same voice, roughly two and a half
months apart: a live seminar, the earlier of the two, opens with "stop
fishing for boats" as the number-one mistake he sees anglers make — spending
the whole day trying to locate the fleet instead of fishing their own
water — then names the specific cost: private boats come up to his
wheelhouse asking to borrow his $5,000 gyro-stabilized binoculars just to
watch *him* catch fish instead of fishing themselves (Capt. Dave Hansen, live seminar, Fred
Hall Show 2019, filmed by Utah Spahghettzi Björker, `8XrMkWPRxgs`,
2019-03-12). The tutorial below gives the same doctrine with a worked example
(Your Saltwater Guide, "Top 5 Offshore Fishing Tips", ZFqe49jRgA0,
2019-05-26; registered voice, single mention on this specific facet — medium
confidence on the worked-example detail).

The visual analog of the radio-chatter trap above: once it's grey light and
you can start to see, the instinct is to climb up top and scan for **sport
boats** rather than fish, then run at whichever one looks stopped. Hansen's
worked example: both anglers on a private boat fixate on a named sport boat
(the Ocean Odyssey), staring at it the whole run to judge whether it has
stopped — so neither is looking left or right, and the boat drives right past
an unnoticed kelp paddy and a spot of breezing tuna on the way, only to reach
the sport boat just as it starts moving off the spot.

**Doctrine: go fishing for fish, not for boats.** A cluster of sport boats
parked on one spot is usually nothing more than one boat stopping and the
others following it over — not independent confirmation that the spot beats
the sign you drove past to get there. Even a boat that's visibly hooked up
doesn't help you: the fish are keyed on bait falling straight off *that*
boat's transom, not on your boat sitting 25 yards off its side.

**A Part 2 restatement, tied to chart-reading (same seminar, `JiJYamo0QHk`,
2019-03-12):** later in the same talk, Hansen repeats the doctrine in
blunter form — when he sees a cluster of boats, he goes the other way —
framed explicitly as hunting **clean water with no boats on it**, not just
avoiding a crowded radio channel. If a crowd is sitting on water he'd
otherwise want to run to, the crowd itself is treated as a reason to look
elsewhere. Pairs with the same talk's chlorophyll-chart trend-reading method
in [water color](../conditions/water-color.md).

## Cameron's fleet-tracking practices (cameron; high confidence — merge)

A different, verification-oriented use of fleet activity: reading **where
boats are**, not what they say over the radio, as a data layer. AIS/vessel
tracking is third-party-observed and harder to fake than a voice call, which
is the practical reason to lean on it over radio chatter when both are
available:

- **AIS sportboat tracking at the Coronado Islands** — watches stop/start
  patterns on sportboat AIS (e.g. the Grande, MMSI 366746210) as one of the
  most valuable data sources he has for learning the islands; wants stops
  correlated against tide, wind, SST, chlorophyll, and current (a research
  direction, not yet built out).
- **Bait-boat tracking** — watches Everingham's bait boats on MarineTraffic
  to see when the San Diego Bay / Mission Bay bait receivers get refilled
  during bait shortages (see [making bait](../bait/making-bait.md) for the
  buy side this feeds).
- **VHF ch 72 offshore monitoring on busy weekends** — also picks up Navy
  ship-to-ship traffic on the same channel, with the same ships/operators
  recurring week to week; situational awareness, not a fishing signal.

## Radar as a fleet-scouting tool, not just navigation (StokedOnFishing, `-bw1KDfDjv4`, 2015-09-27; unregistered channel, sponsor-heavy Simrad episode — medium confidence)

A radar-based counterpart to the AIS tracking above, offered without a
specific range or set-up: running the boat's radar while searching offshore
(14 Mile Bank / 209 / 267 complex, San Diego) doubles as a way to see **where
the rest of the fleet is sitting** — the stated payoff was spotting a boat
parked on a kelp paddy on the radar screen before getting close enough for
that boat to notice the approach, i.e. reading paddy-holding boats as radar
returns rather than relying on eyes/binoculars to pick them out first. No
range or radar-mode detail was given beyond "radar set" being on; kept here
as a stated benefit, not a worked setting. Consistent with using boat
position as a data layer (Cameron's AIS practice above) rather than the
VHF-chatter risk this note otherwise warns against — a radar contact is
directly observed, not a broadcast claim that can be faked.
