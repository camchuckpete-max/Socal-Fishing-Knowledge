# Search and Glassing

How to actually look for offshore fish, and how to anchor once you find the water.
Search is the day's main labor — most offshore hours are spent glassing and
running between glass stops, not fighting fish. This note owns the *method*; what
the birds mean is in [bird reading](../conditions/bird-reading.md), how to
read the meter is in [electronics and sounder](electronics-and-sounder.md), and
how to weigh what the rest of the fleet is doing (VHF, AIS) is in
[fleet intelligence](fleet-intelligence.md).

## The glassing protocol

Glass from a full stop, or bow into the swell at ~1 kt — a rolling, drifting
boat makes glassing useless (`DnSqw4r7A1s`, `E4vKwRaRueA`). Use image-stabilized
binoculars.

- **Scan slow and complete.** Work 360° in small increments, 3–4 minutes per
  full scan. When a bird catches your eye, watch it 30–40 seconds before
  judging it — a single glimpse doesn't show whether the dive-line is moving
  or the raft is tracking.
- **Know your ranges.** From deck level, expect a few terns at ~0.5–1 mi and a
  big bird spot at ~2 mi. From a hardtop, birds carry to ~2 mi and dolphins to
  ~4 mi — height extends range, so glass from the highest stable spot
  available.
- **Space stations 3–4 mi apart** to tile the water at those detection ranges.
- **Along a bank, run the contour at 16–18 mph** with a lookout ~30° off each
  bow. On a current break (a hard edge), run fast between glass stops and stop
  only to look — the target is a line, not a point.
- **On gray-calm mornings, scan down-swell** a quarter-mile out and watch for
  kelp paddies rolling over the swell crests — the motion gives them away when
  nothing is flying.
- **Glass every trip or two, at minimum.** Under-glassing is the most common
  way to blank on a day that had fish on it: "if you're not burning batteries
  every trip or two, you're not looking enough" (`DnSqw4r7A1s`, `E4vKwRaRueA`).

Terns are the most productive bird to follow: run to working terns even when
nothing else is working, and stop to cast on them (cameron). The same
run-stop-cast loop, worked at speed on a boil rather than a bird, is
[foamer casting](../techniques/foamer-casting.md); the tern-follow read and
further bird doctrine live in [bird reading](../conditions/bird-reading.md).

## Surface sign vocabulary

Three visual surface reads to glass for, independent of species — named on
camera as the "three main things" this boat's captains glass for: birds, fish
boiling, and breezers/flat spots (`91ZJbhAnzMg`):

- **Boiler / foamer** — fish visible right on top, breaking or boiling the
  surface; the two terms are used interchangeably. A boiler/foamer can carry a
  bird school working over it — the birds are the associated sign, not a
  separate read (see [bird reading](../conditions/bird-reading.md) for what
  specific bird behavior means once you're glassing one).
- **Breezer** — fish holding very close to the surface in calm water, giving
  themselves away by pushing a ripple that reads like wind on the water. The
  same sign is described independently as "raindrops on the water" rather than
  fish boiling (`lf3S28nh-kk`) — treated here as the same near-surface-fish
  sign, possibly connected by an auto-caption mishearing of "breezer" as
  "freezer" (unconfirmed). A third, independent source describes the same
  breezer sign in
  [foamer casting](../techniques/foamer-casting.md#the-approach)
  (`JiJYamo0QHk`).
- **Flat spot** — the choppy-water version of a breezer: with wind and
  whitecaps around, the same near-surface fish flatten the chop directly over
  them instead of rippling it, so the tell is a smooth, whitecap-free patch
  sitting in otherwise broken water rather than a ripple.

Below the surface, side-scan/up-down sonar finds the same near-surface fish
without a boil or bird pile — see
[electronics and sounder](electronics-and-sounder.md) for method
(`lf3S28nh-kk`). Even a single tern or a couple of terns picking at the water
— diving and looking rather than working a school hard — is worth running to
and sweeping with sonar for exactly this reason (`lf3S28nh-kk`); see
[bird reading](../conditions/bird-reading.md) for this KB's other tern
doctrine.

## Sunglass lens color for glassing

Lens tint changes what surface sign stands out, on top of the eye protection
every pair gives:

- **All-around / bright-light lens — blue mirror or standard gray**, matching
  **neutral gray** (`nnrEjc-Gq2o`, `YJX-hYEIcNM`) — the classic offshore
  default: it lets in the least light of any tint here, so it protects best on
  sunny days and is the pair your eyes relax into over a long day. No stated
  edge for a specific sign type.
- **Contrast lens for spotting sign — green or copper**, matching **amber,
  including an amber-mirror tint** (`nnrEjc-Gq2o`, `YJX-hYEIcNM`) — the most
  contrast of the lenses here. One source calls this out for kelp, birds, and
  dolphins; the other narrows the example to picking a kelp paddy out of the
  water (see the surface-sign vocabulary above and
  [kelp paddies](../conditions/kelp-paddies.md)). Kept as given rather than
  merged into one label — "green," "copper," and "amber" are not stated as
  interchangeable.
- **Overcast / low-light lens — yellow**, matching **sunrise yellow**
  (`nnrEjc-Gq2o`, `YJX-hYEIcNM`) — lets in the most light of the lenses here;
  called out for overcast conditions and early/late in the day, and
  specifically for picking out a breezer on gray, flat-light days when the sun
  isn't lighting up the water (see the breezer entry above).
- **Fit beats tint**: a frame that fits your face — minimizing light leaking
  in from the sides and top — matters more than which tint you choose; the
  frame that blocks the most light usually also looks best on you
  (`nnrEjc-Gq2o`).

## Anchoring geometry

Once you've found the water, where you drop the hook depends on what you're
after (`6TBxHnkYXI0`):

- **Bottom fish → anchor on the spot.** You want to sit directly over the
  structure.
- **Grade the mark with repeated passes, then drop in the adjacent sand, not
  on the rock.** Idle over a hard-bottom high spot, mark the peak on the GPS,
  turn around and re-drive the same track to confirm bait/fish sign before
  committing (see the bottom-hardness read in
  [electronics and sounder](electronics-and-sounder.md)). Once it grades out,
  come back on that track and release the anchor into the sand patch beside
  the rock rather than onto the rock itself, so your presented lines drift
  back onto the structure instead of sitting directly over it: "you're not
  setting right on top of the rock, you're actually in front of it, so your
  lines go back to the rock" (`6DzbsElGE7E`, `xI9tPJFXbUM`).
- **Midwater / gamefish → anchor up-current of it,** with the standoff scaled
  to current strength — more current, more standoff, so your chum and baits
  sweep down onto the fish.
- **Break-off risk (yellowtail) → anchor further still,** so a hooked fish has
  room before it reaches the structure.
- **The chum bucket is an artificial "thumb over the hose"** — and its drift
  line is your current sensor. Watch which way and how fast the chum leaves
  the boat to read the true current before you commit. The mechanism is in
  [current structure](../conditions/current-structure.md); the drift-angle
  read in [current diagnostics](../conditions/current-diagnostics.md).
- **Re-anchor on the opposite side of the same spot when current reverses,
  because fish hold facing into the current** — the same way a stream trout
  always faces upstream; facing away would leave them with no control
  (`BskCsKaTiZw`). On a spot fished two days running with the current reversed
  between them, the fish feeding on one side of the structure the first day
  feed from the opposite side the second, because that side is now the
  up-current face the food washes from. Don't just re-anchor to hold the boat
  at the same GPS position — reset so your baits again reach the up-current
  face the fish are now oriented toward, which after a reversal is the other
  side of the spot from the day before. This is the day-to-day, same-spot
  companion to the up-current-face bank-scale doctrine in
  [current structure](../conditions/current-structure.md#where-on-the-bank).
- **Too deep to anchor → drift the spot instead.** On an 180–200+ ft
  [rockfish](../species/rockfish-lingcod.md) spot too deep to hook (35
  fathoms/210 ft on the trip logged here), don't anchor: read wind vs.
  current direction and position the boat up-wind/up-current of the target
  so the drift carries you onto it — e.g. a south wind pushes the boat
  north, so start south of the spot (`_ZThckj2TIM`).
  - Read the drift before committing bait: stop the boat on arrival, note the
    stopping point, then watch which way and how fast wind/current move it
    before dropping lines.
  - Steer/slow the drift with the motor as a rudder: with the outboard off the
    keel line (angled instead of dead straight), the motor acts as a rudder
    and swings/slows that side of the boat in the drift; dead straight with
    the keel, the hull's V cuts through and the boat tracks the drift
    normally.
  - Speed up a slow drift by shutting the engine off and pulling the motor
    completely out of the water — less drag lets the boat slide with the
    wind/current faster.
  - Slow down a fast drift by dropping the motor back in as a rudder and/or
    deploying a drift sock/chute.

## Ground tackle and the pinpoint drop

Below the anchoring geometry above sits the physical rode and the drop itself
(`OpA0OqRgj00`, `HGyL7pXy3Ts`):

- **Run chain-and-rope, never a straight/all-chain rode.** An all-chain rode
  is a tell for an inexperienced boat: "if you go out on a sport boat and the
  guy's got straight chain... he probably is new." Every boat surveyed here
  runs chain and rope together — "I'll guarantee you all the boats have chain
  and rope... because it matters and it's important" — with the chain segment
  doing the key work and wanting to lay flat on the bottom (`OpA0OqRgj00`).
  ⚠ Flagged gap — no corpus source: mechanism for why an all-chain rode is
  worse, beyond it being a tell for an inexperienced boat. On a 65 ft Hatteras
  running 120 ft of chain ahead of the rope, the anchor is payed out to the
  chain/rope splice and stopped there before any rope goes over — confirming
  the same chain-then-rope structure on a second, earlier boat (`HGyL7pXy3Ts`).
- **Size the anchor to the boat, not to a fixed number** (`OpA0OqRgj00`):
  - 21 ft Wellcraft → 12 lb anchor
  - 65 ft Viking → 80 lb anchor
  - Typical SoCal sport boat → 75–100 lb, "usually 100 lb"
  - The Royal Polaris (rendered "Real Polaris" by auto-captions; unverified
    against a named-boat roster, inferred from context as the well-known San
    Diego long-range boat) → roughly 150 lb

  The anchor weight ladder scales with boat size/class; the chain does the
  actual holding work at every size on it.
- **Free-spool the drop to pinpoint the spot, rather than lowering on the
  powered winch.** Every anchor winch has a free-spool release (a star-shaped
  wrench/handle); backing it off lets the anchor free-fall instead of paying
  out under power. Powered payout runs roughly a foot per second — by the time
  ~100 ft of rope-and-chain has paid out that way in wind or current, the boat
  has already drifted past the target. Free-spooling lets the anchor fall
  essentially straight down: pull up to the spot, release the free-spool, let
  it fall, then cinch the star back down to set once it's on bottom
  (`OpA0OqRgj00`).
- **On a close-range rock, drop first and read the lay before committing to a
  re-set.** When the structure is small and close — a rock cresting a few
  feet below the surface, spotted directly on the sounder rather than graded
  from a distance — you may not know which way the boat will lay until the
  anchor is down. Post a spotter at the bow to confirm the drop, pay the chain
  out to the chain/rope splice and stop there, then let the boat settle and
  check the lay against the structure. If it isn't sitting where you want,
  don't re-lift — feed out a bit more chain, then a bit more rope, to walk the
  boat into position instead (`HGyL7pXy3Ts`).

## Confirming the lay reaches the spot

Getting the anchor down (see *Ground tackle and the pinpoint drop*, above) is
only half the job — the boat then has to lie the way planned, because the
spot has to end up off the stern, where the bait lands, not off the bow
(`JK2-cpaXkI8`):

- **Read the compass/GPS heading the moment the anchor sets and compare it to
  what you expected.** Older boats carried a physical compass that showed
  this directly; many boats today run GPS-only with no compass at all.
- **If the boat lies backward from plan, the spot ends up off the bow — reset,
  don't power around into position.** You cannot motor the boat around to
  face the "right" way with the anchor down; wind/current swings it straight
  back to the same lay. The fix: pull the anchor all the way up, re-drive
  around and back over the spot on the heading you actually want, and re-drop
  beyond the spot in that direction of travel so that once it sets, the stern
  — and the line — fall back onto the spot.
- **Re-set as many times as it takes.** Repeated re-anchoring — resetting "10
  times" in one example — is normal, not a failure; being off the spot is the
  actual failure.
- **Current, not the GPS numbers or yesterday's result, decides the bite:**
  "it doesn't matter if you caught 10,000 fish there yesterday if there's no
  current there today." Same point as the no-current tell in
  [current diagnostics](../conditions/current-diagnostics.md#the-treadmill-is-off--no-current-at-anchor).

## Inshore/kayak zone-and-spot search (bay / back-bay)

A distinct method from the offshore glassing protocol above, for working a
bay or back-bay from a kayak (or any fixed-position small-boat/shore
approach) — worked out on
[spotted bay bass](../species/spotted-bay-bass.md) but generic to any
structure-oriented inshore species (`6L8nIFeqvkw`):

- **Zones and spots.** A zone is a stretch of connected structure — bounded by
  a gap in usable structure or a traverse (a bridge, a featureless stretch of
  dock). A spot is smaller: your casting range from one fixed position (a
  kayak holding station).
- **Working a spot.** Take 10–15 casts testing deep water first, then
  shallow, varying the angle, before ruling a spot empty. Once you connect,
  cast the same spot at least 2 more times before moving on — structure fish
  loosely cluster, so one bite often means several more sitting right there.
- **Chaining spots.** Place each new spot so it overlaps the edge of the last
  rather than leaving gaps, and lean the next spot toward whichever side
  (shallower/deeper, up-zone/down-zone) actually produced bites, abandoning
  the side that didn't.
- **Changing zones.** If the next zone's topography is completely different
  from the last (e.g. a dock replacing an eelgrass shoreline), restart the
  pattern from spot one instead of carrying forward what worked in the
  previous zone.

## Reading bait behavior — happy vs. spooked bait (coastal bass)

A visual bait-reading method for coastal bass water, alongside the
current/water-color reads already covered elsewhere (`XyKF34C5iB4`):

- **"Happy" bait — keep moving.** Bait that's meandering, spread out, and not
  doing anything in particular reads as unpressured/uninterested prey — not a
  sign predators are working it. Look down at the bait and keep running if
  this is what you see.
- **"Unhappy" / scared bait — stop and fish it.** Bait that's balled up tight
  and moving around quickly reads as bait under active pressure — stop and
  work that spot.
- **10 to 20 casts, no bite, move on.** A fished spot that doesn't produce in
  roughly that many casts gets abandoned for the next one — a slightly higher
  cast count than the 10–15-cast bay/back-bay figure above, for open coastal
  bass water rather than a fixed dock/eelgrass spot.
- **Visible feeding activity doesn't guarantee a bite** if the fish are keyed
  on a specific natural forage your lures can't imitate (`XyKF34C5iB4`) — a
  reminder to read what the bait is doing, not just whether fish are showing.
- **"Fishing pattern":** once a color/technique/zone type produces a bite in
  one spot, repeat that same combination in a similar-looking area rather than
  treating the bite as a one-off — described on camera as the core definition
  of "pattern" fishing.
- **Fish the color you're confident in.** Anglers on the same panel reported
  fishing different colors, patterns, and techniques from each other through
  the same day on the same water — the panel's framing is to fish whichever
  color you personally feel confident in rather than chase one "correct"
  answer, consistent with the no-systematic-color-doctrine framing already
  logged in [surface iron — colour](../techniques/surface-iron-color.md).

## Sportboat bow play

At anchor at San Clemente Island, chum gets pushed up-current toward the bow
by the fish working it, not down-current with the flow — watch for birds
locking on up there and put the surface iron under them; the fish betray
their position at the bow even though the current "should" carry everything
astern (`DnSqw4r7A1s`).
