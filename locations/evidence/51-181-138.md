# Evidence — 51 / 181 / 138

Provenance for [51 / 181 / 138](../51-181-138.md). **Three of the four
observations below name the 181 and the fourth names the 138.** No source in the
corpus names the 51.

## Structure & bathymetry

- `Rf1HKJG-SDg` (Your Saltwater Guide, 2020-12-15, SoCal outer banks): the 181
  listed as a bank alongside "209, 181, 182, the 43" — banks framed as isolated
  mountains of rock in an otherwise featureless desert that congregate bait for
  safety and then draw the big fish, with current, tide and water temperature
  acting on that structure rather than replacing it. The same segment sets the
  naming convention on the 182 (water 2,000–3,000 ft deep, coming up to 182
  fathoms at the highest part and levelling off again, that shallowest point
  earning the name), notes that places all over the ocean read 182 fathoms
  without being banks, defines a fathom as the unit ocean depth is measured in,
  and gives the 43 as 43 fathoms — a rockfish destination through the 1940s to
  1990s, now closed to rockfish and fished for giant bluefin on the same high
  spot.

## What's there

- `Blh2BA-7Ono` (BDOutdoors, 2022-07-21, SoCal Bight report): bluefin below San
  Clemente were not getting coverage, so the area was framed as one search — "the
  43, the 181, 182, 289, the entire Clemente Ridge, all the way up to Mackerel
  Bank" treated as one section of water, roughly 50–60 square miles to look at,
  explicitly contrasted with running to the 302, the 371 or the stuff off the
  West End of Catalina.
- `OYOda6T3f-8` (BDOutdoors, 2022-10-20, SoCal Bight report): from the West End
  of San Clemente down through Mackerel Bank to the 181/182 Ridge "down there
  towards the corner" was called the warmest water on the coast and where the
  yellowfin were; the stated method for finding them is the warmest water crossed
  against a chlorophyll chart for the cleanest. The same sentence is carried
  without the ridge clause in the yellowfin router and the San Clemente
  front-side zone page, and with the "corner" clause in
  [The Corner / 140 / 182](../corner-140-182.md).

## How it fishes

- `yMiBtZ7k8-w` (BDOutdoors, 2021-02-04, deep-drop swordfishing seminar): asked
  on camera where he wanted to fish on the SST/chlorophyll plot being displayed,
  the presenter named the 138 first, and the 182 next for its mixing water and
  heavy current flow; the 138 was then pointed to again for the "hard edge" on
  it. ⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-by-source): the 182 is raised by the host, not
  the presenter — "and even down at the 182 there's that mixing water and you got
  some heavy current flow there that looks like a good place to check out
  **wouldn't you say**" — and the "hard edge" line is likewise the host's
  question ("that's what you guys are looking for that hard edge huh"), which the
  presenter answers "yeah yeah yep"; the structure/current-edge "banger days" and
  East-coast-contrast lines later in this entry are also the host's. The stated reasoning is that converging waters form at any edge, whether
  temperature or chlorophyll, that big powerful long breaks act as highways for
  both bait and fish, and that SoCal fish orient to structure and then to current
  edges — with the banger days coming when the two line up. The same segment
  contrasts the East-coast pattern of fish hanging on the continental-shelf wall
  with the SoCal case of far more water to cover, where current edges get fished
  as much as structure. This is the source behind the "138/182 area on break
  days" entry in the swordfish router.
