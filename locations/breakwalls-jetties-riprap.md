# Break Walls, Jetties and Riprap

Break walls, jetties, and riprap channel edges are man-made rock armor that
fishes like reef structure: current forced past deviations in an otherwise
uniform rock line concentrates bait and holds fish. This is universal
structure knowledge — the species behavior on riprap lives in the species
notes ([calico bass](../species/calico-bass.md),
[sand bass](../species/sand-bass.md),
[spotted bay bass](../species/spotted-bay-bass.md)).

## Structure & bathymetry

A break wall or jetty looks like a uniform line of rock, but it does not
fish uniformly. What makes riprap produce is the **deviations** in the rock
line — points, pockets, corners, notches, and where the wall changes angle.
Current forced past a deviation accelerates and creates eddies that trap
plankton and bait — the "thumb over the hose" mechanism, the same
current-over-structure chain that works offshore banks (see
[current structure](../conditions/current-structure.md)) (`bydQzE3F9yM`).
Fish hold on and behind the deviation, on the down-current side, waiting for
the accelerated water to bring food past.

The read is: skip the featureless straightaways; fish the corners, points,
pockets, and angle changes. A wall's ends and its inside/outside corners are
prime because the deviation is largest there. An uneven pile of "out-jutting"
concrete slabs on one jetty is called out on camera as a locally known
big-bass zone for exactly this reason — the irregular pile changes the
current enough to aggregate fish there, though the spot itself is not named
(`bydQzE3F9yM`).

A break wall is built in layers, not a uniform pyramid: a sand core, then
progressively larger stone up to the large "class A" armor visible above the
waterline. The face toward open ocean slopes out gently to dissipate wave
energy; the face(s) protecting a harbor or channel from erosion, not swell,
sit much steeper (`bydQzE3F9yM`). Riprap channel edges inside a harbor follow
that steep, erosion-only profile — see
[bays and harbors](bays-and-harbors.md). Depth off a wall varies the same
way: riprap can bottom out in as little as 10-15 ft of water at the toe, and
a boat sitting 10 ft off the rock can already be sitting in 20 ft of water
(`jTXIr9O6zYk`).

A large share of every wall on the SoCal coast can hold fish, because a wall
is a self-contained rock/kelp ecosystem independent of its reputation —
"they're not all created equal," but a wall doesn't need a famous name to
fish (`XyKF34C5iB4`). Walls named on camera across the sources here: the
**Long Beach breakwater** (its federal wall, Long Beach wall, and middle wall
sections) (`bydQzE3F9yM`, `ZoIzpyyS9xw`), the **Seal Beach wall**, the
**Surfside wall**, **Marina del Rey**, the **Zuniga Jetty** (San Diego), and
**Oceanside** (`XyKF34C5iB4`).

## Birds mark the deviation

Birds sitting on the rocks mark the thumb-over-hose deviation, so you don't
have to guess where it is: a cluster of birds parked on one stretch of wall
sits where the current break concentrates bait (`bydQzE3F9yM`). This is the
inshore riprap version of the broader rule that birds on structure mark a
repeatable decision — the bird doctrine is in
[bird reading](../conditions/bird-reading.md). The same read works with birds
already working the water, not just sitting: cormorants diving **30 ft off**
a wall put the bass off the rock face too, not on it — an angler who only
casts tight to the rock can fish right past a boil breaking behind the boat
(`jTXIr9O6zYk`).

| [Sand bass](../species/sand-bass.md) | present mixed with calico bass on riprap/jetty/breakwall structure (`bydQzE3F9yM`, `jTXIr9O6zYk`) | same ambush-at-the-deviation pattern as calico bass |
| [Spotted bay bass](../species/spotted-bay-bass.md) | present mixed with calico and sand bass, especially on riprap inside bays and harbors (`bydQzE3F9yM`) | same ambush pattern; see [bays and harbors](bays-and-harbors.md) for the harbor-scale version of this structure |

Crabs and other crustaceans live in the rock and turn up in bass stomachs at
the wall, but they are not the primary forage — there isn't enough
crab/crustacean biomass on a wall to sustain the bass population living
there. A crab in a fish's mouth is a byproduct of a slowed-down presentation,
not a signal to switch to a crab-imitating bait (`jTXIr9O6zYk`).

## How it fishes

Approach from the **up-current side** and work the presentation so it sweeps
down onto the deviation with the current, the way food naturally arrives.
Position so a hooked fish can be pulled **away** from the rocks rather than
into them — this applies to all rock structure. The same rock-deviation logic
applies in miniature to the riprap edges inside
[bays and harbors](bays-and-harbors.md).

Bass use a wall as one continuous piece of structure and travel its length
rather than holding in one fixed spot — camping on a single favorite rock is
a low-percentage play. Cover water: make a couple of casts, and if nothing
bites, move the boat roughly a cast-length and try again; once bites start,
slow down and work that zone thoroughly with multiple casts (`bydQzE3F9yM`).

Moving baits (a crankbait, a weedless swimbait) get cast parallel to the wall
where possible, or at roughly a **45° angle** off the bow when swell prevents
a true parallel cast; fish often take these baits a good distance off the
wall, not tight to it. A lead-head bait worked down the wall face instead
gets cast **perpendicular** to the wall and fished slower, giving the bait
time to fall down the rock face rather than getting dragged past it by a
moving boat (`bydQzE3F9yM`). Riprap inside a harbor is steep enough, and
sheltered enough from swell, that the boat sits close to the rock and casts
run parallel to it regardless of bait type (`bydQzE3F9yM`).

Bay/harbor riprap is protected water: after a blow drops water temperatures
and shuts down the exposed coast, harbor riprap stays fishable and can
outfish the open coast — useful practice water ahead of a spring/summer shift
to outside structure once conditions improve (`bydQzE3F9yM`).

Fishing tight to a wall, jetty, or other rock structure carries real risk in
any swell. Stay within your own comfort level, and build close-quarters
boat-handling skill on a protected, inside riprap area before working an
exposed outside wall — even where the rock ends abruptly, a boat can be
sitting in safe water just **5 ft** off it as long as wind isn't pushing the
boat in (`bydQzE3F9yM`).

Any wall can fish, famous or not — a wall is a self-contained rock/kelp
ecosystem regardless of its reputation. An angler without boat access gets
more from starting at whatever break wall sits closest (a kayak launch, a
local beach) than from chasing a named spot farther away (`XyKF34C5iB4`). A
diver's underwater assessment of a wall's rock composition can also point to
unusually productive walls invisible from the surface — network with
scuba/freediving contacts for that intel rather than fishing every wall cold
(`XyKF34C5iB4`).

A wall face's underwater angle also dictates how a sinking bait must be cast
onto it — close the bail as the lure lands and count the bait down to a
chosen depth rather than dropping slack into the rock: see
[countdown casting](../techniques/countdown-casting.md).
