# Current Diagnostics

This note reads current strength and direction off things visible on the
water — kelp lean, chum drift, anchor-line angle, bite pattern — and turns
that read into a presentation call in real time. For **why** current across
structure produces fish, see [current structure](current-structure.md); this
note is the field-read companion to that mechanism.

## Kelp lean = current-strength gauge

A stalk of kelp (or a paddy's trailing growth) reads out current like a wind
sock: current stronger than the kelp's own buoyancy pulls it under, and it
stands back up as the pull weakens. Match the presentation to what it shows.

| Kelp behavior | Current strength | Presentation call |
|---|---|---|
| **Pulled ~20 ft under** the surface | Raging | Surface presentations are dead — go deep / weighted |
| **Laid down** flat along the surface | Strong | Fishable but hard; work the down-current side |
| **Resurfacing** / standing back up | Slacking | Switch presentation back to the surface / lighter |

The transition from laid-down back to resurfacing is the cue to change gear
before the bite window opens — watch the kelp, not the clock (`HnqiE05vdXs`).

## Chum-drift angle vs boat lay — true current vs wind

The angle of the chum line relative to how the boat is lying separates true
current from wind push (`6TBxHnkYXI0`):

- Chum drifting straight back off the stern, in line with the boat = the boat
  and the water are moving together (wind-dominated lay, or aligned).
- Chum swinging off at an angle to the boat's lay = real current cutting
  across, independent of the wind — that angle is the current-direction
  sensor.

The chum bucket itself works as an artificial "thumb over the hose" — its
drift line is a current reading a boat can deploy on purpose (`6TBxHnkYXI0`).

A chart plotter's current-arrow layer reads wind, not current; confirm
direction with the chum-drift and kelp-lean tells above instead (`BskCsKaTiZw`).

## Anchor-line current read on hard, kelp-free structure

On hard-bottom/rock spots with no buoys and no kelp to read — called out
specifically for yellowtail/sea-bass rock structure — there is no visual
current tell to check before committing. The only way to find which way
current is running is to drop the anchor and watch which way the line
settles: it points in the direction the current is running (`sIoNELGlxmk`).
That first read can be wrong — the boat can swing differently than expected,
or the current can run a different direction than assumed — which is why
captains who know a spot well still sometimes have to reset the anchor after
the initial guess doesn't hold. On his own skiff, the presenter resets the
anchor 10–12 times in a day to stay precisely on the spot he wants — a
normal, expected cost of fishing the spot correctly, not a sign of a bad read
(`sIoNELGlxmk`). Ties to the same willing-to-reset-the-anchor framing in the
fathom-curve worked example in
[current structure](current-structure.md#fathom-curve-behavior-at-islands).

## "The treadmill is off" — no current at anchor (`6TBxHnkYXI0`)

When there is no current at anchor, the tell is bait pointing in all
directions (not lined up) and fish metering but not biting: the feeding
"treadmill" that carries bait past predators is switched off, and marks on
the sounder will not convert (`6TBxHnkYXI0`). Read this as a move-or-wait
signal — reposition to where current is running, or wait for the tide change
to restart the treadmill (see [tide and slack](tide-and-slack.md)).

A bite-pattern tell for the same shutoff, useful when the bait isn't visible
directly: a string of baits getting hit on nearly every drop, followed by
several drops in a row with nothing, is often the first sign current just
stopped — before the water visibly goes slack (`aFb221LUoD0`).

## Sand-drop current read (small-craft, close range)

In close, calm-looking water where a current edge isn't visually obvious —
a shallow inshore rock formation at a given tide stage, for example — drop a
handful of sand or beach sediment over the side and watch which way it
drifts as it sinks to the bottom: a low-tech visual tracer for current
direction, and for the bait that current concentrates, when there's no other
tell to read from a small craft (`LqW32NSkObY`).

- Don't infer subsurface current from the surface: what the top of the water
  is doing does not reliably tell you what's happening below it. Treat
  surface movement and subsurface current as separate reads, consistent with
  reading the kelp-lean and chum-drift diagnostics above at the depth that
  matters for the presentation in play (`LqW32NSkObY`).

## Line scope — reading and correcting drift off vertical

Scope is the line getting pulled off vertical and taken out toward the
horizon as the boat drifts, rather than staying straight up and down over the
mark. Keeping the line close to vertical matters for two reasons: bite feel
drops off the more the line scopes out, and once scoped, there is more slack
between rod tip and bait to catch up on when a bite comes, costing hookups
(`Vbf40qvH9-Q`).

Perfectly vertical isn't always achievable. The default corrective habit is
to cycle the presentation — drop it down, wind it up, drop it back down —
rather than leaving it to scope out unattended; if it does get away and ends
up too far out on the horizon, wind it in and drop it back down to reset
vertical rather than continuing to fish it scoped out (`Vbf40qvH9-Q`).

When the drift is strong enough that winding in can't stop the jig scoping
out, flick it a little further up-swell instead: as the boat drifts down
onto the spot, the jig sinks on the way and should end up back roughly
vertical by the time it reaches bottom — a drift-timed correction rather
than a retrieve-and-redrop one (`sJGs-Jqt9Vo`). Neither source reconciles the
two fixes; which applies turns on whether the drift lets you wind in and
reset in time — reach for the drift-timed flick when it doesn't.

This is the same drop/wind/drop corrective cadence documented for
[yo-yo iron](../techniques/yo-yo-iron.md#mechanics) (thumbing the spool so
the iron tracks straight down rather than scoping away from the boat) and
[flat-fall jigging](../techniques/flat-fall-jigging.md#tracking-depth-on-the-drop)
(line-out isn't true depth once the jig trails away from vertical on the
drift) — a general vertical-presentation read/fix, not specific to one
technique.

## How to use it in planning

- Read kelp lean before rigging: pulled under = go deep/weighted, laid down =
  work the down-current side, resurfacing = switch back to surface/lighter
  gear.
- At anchor or on a drift, watch the chum-drift angle against the boat's lay
  to separate current from wind — trust it over a plotter's current-arrow
  layer.
- On kelp-free hard bottom, expect to drop and reset the anchor (possibly
  several times) before the line settles on the true current direction;
  budget that as normal, not a bad read.
- Bait scattered in all directions plus marks that won't convert is the
  treadmill-off signal — reposition or wait for the tide change rather than
  working a dead zone.
- On a small craft with no visible current edge, drop sand or sediment over
  the side as a tracer, and never assume subsurface current matches what the
  surface is doing.
- Keep the presentation vertical with a drop/wind/drop cadence; if it scopes
  out anyway, wind in and redrop, or flick up-swell when the drift won't let
  you wind in fast enough.
