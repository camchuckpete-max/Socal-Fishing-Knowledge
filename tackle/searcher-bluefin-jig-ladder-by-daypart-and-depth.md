# Searcher Bluefin Jig Ladder by Daypart and Depth (Steve Carson, Penn/SearcherSportfishing, 2023-10-12)

Steve Carson, aboard the **Searcher** (`vVOkxHx58Eg`, "Tackle Tip Thursday Vol.
228 (Bluefin Tuna Lures)," uploaded 2023-10-12; `searcher-sportfishing`
registered channel). Front-matter confidence is `medium`: this is a single
citation of this specific ladder, so the mechanism/depth/hook-style doctrine
rates at the rubric's single-mention `medium`, not the registry's repeated-
doctrine `high`. Separately, the registry's own sponsored-claim caveat on
`searcher-sportfishing` (brand tie-ins with Penn/Izorline) caps **named
product/model picks below this note's blanket rating, at `low`, regardless of
repetition** — flagged inline below wherever a specific model name (Williamson
Gomame, the captioned "kensaki," the "Eddie bomb") is named. Frames jig selection as driven first
by **how well the fish can see the lure** (daytime vs. dawn/dusk vs. full
night) and second by **depth**, walking a single weight ladder from a
100 g daytime jig up to a 400 g personal ceiling. This is a **heavier tier**
than, and distinct from, the 50–170 g daytime dart-jig/Colt-Sniper class
already logged in [dart jig — daytime finesse tuna](../techniques/dart-jig-tuna.md)
and [Searcher daytime dart-jig outfit ladder](searcher-daytime-dart-jig-outfit-ladder.md) —
this video's smallest jig (100 g) sits at the top of that class's range and
the ladder here continues well past it. General jig-weight/depth and
assist-hook rigging doctrine for this whole class lives in
[knife jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md); this note adds one named speaker's own
full daytime-to-night progression, with a **hook-style rule that changes by
weight tier** not previously logged as its own throughline.

## The ladder

| Jig weight | When / depth | Leader | Hook style |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **~100 g** | Midday, full daylight — his favorite, most-used daytime jig; good down to about **200 ft** | not stated for this tier | heavy-duty **20-size treble hook** (upgraded from stock) |
| **~170 g** | Dawn/dusk (limited light), or any time fish are deeper than the 100 g jig's ~200 ft ceiling — as deep as **~250 ft** | variable — sometimes **130 lb fluorocarbon**, sometimes straight to the **50 or 60 lb mainline**, sometimes **80 lb fluorocarbon**; **check with the crew day-of** | still a treble hook — **the largest jig he pairs with a treble**; heavier jigs move to single hooks |
| **~280 g** | Good down to about **300 ft**, "depending on the current and the wind"; beyond that depth, or in wind, may not be usable | **200 lb fluorocarbon**, ~2.5–3 ft, to a **~400 lb test swivel** | heavy single hooks (captioned "70 5x" — read as a **7/0, 5X-strong** hook; exact number **asr-uncertain**), **one off the nose, one off the tail** |
| **~300 g** | Same depth range as the 280 g tier | **200 lb fluorocarbon**, ~3 ft | two single hooks, **one front, one back** — same nose/tail pairing as the 280 g tier |
| **Night deep-drop rig** ("Eddie bomb" — see below) | Night, fish down to **~400 ft** | heavy fluorocarbon, ~2.5–3 ft | one big treble off the bottom, **or** two assist hooks off the top (angler's choice) |
| **~400 g** | His personal heaviest jig — good to about **500 ft** | not stated | **two hooks on the tail end only** (not nose+tail) |
| **~220 g** | Mid-depth **speed jigging / fast-pitch jigging** (mechanical cranking retrieve) | not stated | **two hooks off the nose end** — "usually the best" for this retrieve style |

**His closing point, worth keeping as its own rule:** exactly which jig and
depth works isn't just a daily call — "that's probably going to change every
hour" — so treat this ladder as a starting point and **check with the crew**
rather than a fixed lookup.

## Daytime — ~100 g, the Williamson Gomame

The 100 g daytime jig he shows on camera (captioned "Williamson game" in this
video's auto-captions) is his **Williamson Gomame** (a named product pick —
per the `searcher-sportfishing` registry's sponsored-claim caveat, product/
model endorsements stay `low` confidence regardless of repetition, even
though Carson isn't shown naming a sponsor tie for this specific lure) — the
same named-favorite jig, same ~100 g weight, and same "general daytime
prospecting" framing already logged from clearer captions in three other
citations: [iron jigs —
dart-type jigs, "cold snipers," and a named favorite](../lures/iron-jigs.md),
[Searcher 40 lb all-around tuna outfit](searcher-40lb-all-around-tuna-outfit.md),
and [Searcher daytime dart-jig outfit ladder](searcher-daytime-dart-jig-outfit-ladder.md).
Reasoning given here for going smaller/lighter in daylight: bluefin — even big
ones — "generally eat little food," and in daytime they can see the lure well,
so presentation has to be more careful than at night.

**Hook upgrade, stated reasoning:** the stock hooks that ship on these jigs
are undersized — Carson changes "almost all" of his lures to heavier-duty
hooks because Searcher-grade fish run bigger than what these lure lines are
built for "pretty much anywhere else in the world." He runs a heavy-duty
20-size treble on the 100 g jig specifically. This is the same stock-hooks-
too-light problem already logged (at a different weight tier and hook
strength) in [Searcher daytime dart-jig outfit ladder — hooks](searcher-daytime-dart-jig-outfit-ladder.md#hooks)
(5X-strong treble on the 50–170 g dart-jig class); not reconciled, just the
same problem recurring across this speaker's jig lineup.

## Dawn/dusk — ~170 g

As light drops (dawn, dusk) or fish sit deeper than the 100 g jig's ~200 ft
ceiling (he cites fish as deep as ~250 ft in this window), he steps up to a
170 g jig — described on camera as new to his lineup at the time, already
"caught some fish." **Leader is situational, not fixed:** sometimes 130 lb
fluorocarbon to guard against bite-offs, sometimes tied straight to 50 or
60 lb mainline, sometimes 80 lb fluorocarbon — his explicit advice is to
**check with the crew the day you go out** rather than pick a leader test in
advance, since "the crew of the Searcher wants you to catch fish" and will
set anglers up correctly.

**This is the largest jig he'll pair with a treble hook.** Above this weight
tier he moves to single hooks.

**Safety rule, stated explicitly and worth preserving as its own line:**
never add extra hooks to a lure already rigged with a treble — he calls this
"a real safety issue for you and for the crew." Treble-hook lures stay
single-treble; the nose/tail double-single-hook rigs below are a different
lure class, not a trebled lure with hooks added.

## ~280 g and ~300 g — nose-and-tail single hooks

Stepping up again (roughly 280 g, "good down to probably about 300 ft
depending on the current and the wind" — beyond that depth or in windy
conditions, this size "may not be able to" reach the fish) and 300 g
(same depth range), Carson runs heavy single hooks with **one off the nose
and one off the tail** — his stated preference. Leader: **200 lb test
fluorocarbon**, roughly 2.5–3 ft (he adds a **~400 lb test swivel** on the
280 g rig specifically, needing "only about" 2.5–3 ft of leader ahead of it).

The 280 g jig is captioned "Williamson kensaki" in this transcript — **name
not independently confirmed elsewhere in this corpus** (unlike the Gomame
above); carried here as **asr-uncertain** rather than asserted as a real
Williamson model name. As with the Gomame, a named product pick from this
channel is also capped at `low` confidence per the registry's sponsored-claim
caveat, independent of the separate asr-uncertain name flag.

**Stated reasoning for the nose+tail pairing:** bluefin frequently bite the
jig **as it's sinking**, not on a clean, centered strike, so the fish "may not
be able to get like a really good aim on it and might bite it at either end" —
hooking both ends covers that ambiguity. **Trade-off, stated:** with a hook on
each end, a hooked fish can also get snagged on the *other* hook and come in
sideways, which takes more pulling force to land.

The 300 g jigs often run **glow zebra-stripe patterns**, but Carson states his
own doubt about whether the glow actually matters — "questionable whether or
not the... glow really matters" — an attributed doubt, not a settled claim.
This sits beside, not merged into, the broader fleet-wide glow-vs-doesn't-
matter split already logged in
[knife jigs — color](../lures/knife-jigs.md#color--mostly-angler-confidence-with-one-real-mechanism),
where multiple registered/attributed voices take both sides.

## Night deep-drop rig — the "Eddie bomb"

For fish down to **~400 ft** at night, Carson describes a weighted jig/sinker
hybrid captioned "Eddie bomb" in this transcript — **the name is not
independently confirmed elsewhere in this corpus and is flagged
asr-uncertain**; captured here by function since the term can't be verified
against another source. As with the other named picks in this note, treat any
product/model identification here as `low` confidence per the registry's
sponsored-claim caveat, independent of the asr-uncertain name flag. Shaped
and sized to look like a **12–16 oz** torpedo/bank-style sinker.

**Buying caution, stated explicitly:** some sinkers sold in tackle stores as
jig bodies have only a small loop of wire molded into the front and back —
"a big tuna is just going to literally pull the wire out." Make sure the one
you buy is **wired all the way through** the body.

**Rigging:** either one big treble hook off the bottom, or — an alternative
some anglers run — two assist hooks off the top end. Leader in either case is
roughly **2.5–3 ft** of heavy fluorocarbon — heavy enough that, per Carson,
"they don't care" it's there, because the fish are reacting to profile, not
leader visibility, at night.

**Stated reasoning for the whole rig:** it sinks **straight down**, so it
reaches a called depth quickly — useful when tuna are marked as deep as
400 ft — even at night, when Carson states the fish can still see it despite
the dark ("don't think that they can't"). Its smaller profile also matches
the smaller bait size tuna are keying on at night. **Captain Mike** (Searcher)
is named as a fan of this rig ("Captain Mike loves these").

This depth range and captain's-call framing matches, without adding a name to
it, the **300–400 g "when the captain says I'm reading big ones down at
400 feet, you better have a jig that can get down there"** doctrine already
logged in [Searcher big-tuna rig ladder — Rig 2](searcher-big-tuna-rig-ladder.md#rig-2--extra-heavy-rig-100-lb-penn-international-16visx).

## ~400 g — his personal ceiling, tail-only double hook

400 g is the heaviest jig Carson states he will personally fish — "if I have
to use a 500 g jig, I'm going to be resting" — though he notes 500 g jigs
"work really well" for anglers willing to fish them. The 400 g jig reaches
roughly **500 ft**; he doesn't give a depth ceiling for 500 g jigs beyond
noting they "will" work at that added depth.

**Hook style flips here, with a stated reason distinct from the nose+tail
rigs above:** on these longer, heavier jigs he runs **two hooks on the tail
end only**. His reasoning: this deep and this big, fish tend to just "suck it
all the way in" rather than striking cleanly at one end, so a nose+tail
double-hook setup risks hooking a big, deep fish in two different places at
once — which makes it "really hard to pull in." He flags this as his own
call, not a fixed rule: "your mileage may vary" across other slow-pitch/
speed-jigging styles.

## Aside — speed/fast-pitch jigging, nose-paired hooks

Carson also names the "newly popular" **speed jigging or fast-pitch
jigging** style (he notes it goes by several names) — a mechanical, cranking
retrieve rather than a drop-and-wait presentation. For this style he sizes a
**220 g** jig ("good size... for that mid-depth speed jigging") and runs
**two hooks off the nose end**, calling that placement "usually the best" for
the cranking retrieve. A third, situational hook-placement rule alongside the
nose+tail (280–300 g) and tail-only (400 g) rigs above — placement here is
driven by retrieve style, not by depth or bite behavior. Mechanics of the
speed-jigging retrieve itself are covered in
[knife / speed jigging](../techniques/knife-jigging.md); general hook-count/
placement conflicts across sources are tracked in
[knife jigs — assist-hook rigging](../lures/knife-jigs.md#the-class--models).
