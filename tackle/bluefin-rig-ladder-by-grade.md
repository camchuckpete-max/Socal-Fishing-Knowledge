# Bluefin Rig Ladder by Grade — Steve Carson / Penn (San Diego Rod & Reel Club)

Steve Carson (Penn), guest speaker at the San Diego Rod and Reel Club's
November 2023 meeting — "5 must-have rod and reel setups for bluefin tuna
fishing" (`9JnIS8HkZlA`, uploaded 2023-11-14, an edited version of the talk;
unregistered channel/speaker, single mention — medium confidence). His stated
starting premise: the "tuna chewing the paint off the boat" wide-open bite
that anglers hear about happens **less than 10% of the time** — most days
need a finesse rig to get bit at all, not just a rig strong enough to land
what's already eating.

This is a **line-class ladder matched to expected grade**, distinct from (and
a companion to) the application-based offshore quiver in
[rod & reel selection](rod-and-reel-selection.md) and the class lexicon in
[gear classes](gear-classes.md). The talk names four rigs by line class; the
50 lb rig does double duty (live bait for grade, and the trolling-lure rig),
which is where the source's "five setups" framing comes from.

## Rig 1 — 15 lb finesse (the one you need most)

- **When:** a couple of periods a year — usually late August/early
  September — but also "just lately," i.e. whenever the bite is tough rather
  than wide open.
- **Reel:** single-speed **star-drag** reel, Penn Fathom/Torque **15-size**
  class (a 10-fathom-capacity reel).
- **Line:** fill with braid, leaving roughly a **50 yd top shot**.
- **Top shot:** **20 lb** mono/fluoro test — his own pick, kept apart from
  what "the captains" publicly tell everybody (25 lb): *"don't get me in
  trouble... 20 lb test, there's something like that's the division — you get
  a lot more bites with 20 lb than 25, I don't know why; not that much
  difference between 20 and 25."* An attributed personal preference, not a
  correction of the captains' guidance.
- **Leader:** fluorocarbon between hook and main line, sized so **the knot
  never passes through the tip guide/roller on a cast** — for him, roughly
  **4 ft**; he flags this varies by rod length (8 ft vs. 9 ft) and casting
  style, so treat 4 ft as his number, not a fixed rule. Cross-check against
  the leader-length table in [line & leader](line-and-leader.md).
- **Hook:** J hook, **Owner Gorilla Light**, size **2** (he'd use a 4 if it
  came in that size) — "a nice light hook that lets your bait swim." (Also
  captioned "drill light" moments later on camera; "Gorilla Light" is
  corroborated as the real Owner product name by a separate transcript in
  this corpus, `ptoIvB2MspE`.) See [hooks](hooks.md) for the general
  circle-vs-J framework this sits inside.
- **Knot:** a **4-turn surgeon's knot** joins the monofilament to the
  fluorocarbon — see [essential knots](../rigging/essential-knots.md) for the
  turn-count detail and how it compares with this KB's existing mono-to-fluoro
  knot (Seaguar knot).
- **Ceiling:** **do not fish this rig on fish over about 40 lb.**

## Rig 2 — 30 lb, "big fish finesse"

- **When:** a couple of times a year there are 50–70 lb bluefin around the
  boat that simply won't eat a 40 or 50 lb outfit — "they're 50, they're 60,
  they're 70 lb, they won't bite on 40, they won't bite on 50... now you got
  to catch them on 30."
- **Reel:** **two-speed**, Penn Fathom **15-size** (his own build carries
  roughly **350 yd of braid plus a 50 yd top shot**).
- **Hook:** a small **Owner circle hook** — the speaker is explicit it is
  *not* the "Light" version and *not* the "hybrid" version he names on camera
  (exact model designation asr-uncertain beyond those two exclusions), size
  **4** preferred, size **2** as the largest he'll go — "nothing bigger than
  that," because the fish are still finicky even at this grade.
- **Why circle, this grade specifically:** a big bluefin's teeth chew through
  a leader "in 5 minutes, less than it takes to land [one]" — the circle
  hook's corner-of-the-mouth hookset keeps the leader off the teeth. Matches
  the circle-hook-for-bluefin mechanism already logged in
  [hooks](hooks.md#circle-vs-j--the-core-decision-swrsycmt4tw-m2q22sppkem).
- **The trick on this rig — a two-speed, not the drag:** once one of these
  50–70 lb fish is hooked and circling below the boat rather than committing
  to come up, the standard "pump up, reel down" technique lets the fish kick
  its tail and re-descend a little on every pump — over a 65 lb fish that can
  protract a fight to an hour or two, inviting sharks, gear tangles with
  other anglers, and other ways for it to go wrong. His fix is a continuous
  low-gear grind rather than pump-and-reel — full mechanism and the
  after-the-fight reminder in
  [two-speed low-gear fight](../techniques/two-speed-low-gear-fight.md).

## Rig 3 — 40 lb (the starter rig)

- **When:** "the rig you always start out with" — his default recommendation
  for anyone new to bluefin fishing, including family members along for their
  first trip.
- **Reel:** two-speed, a **~25-size narrow-spool** class reel (exact model
  syntax on camera is garbled — asr-uncertain beyond the size/narrow-spool
  description); 10-fathom capacity.
- **Line:** **50 lb braid**, filled to leave room for **50 yd of 40 lb
  monofilament** top shot. **Rule of thumb, his own:** run the braid **one
  class heavier than the top shot** — 50 lb braid to a 40 lb top shot is the
  example given, since he no longer had a true "in-between" braid weight to
  step just one size up.
- **Leader:** roughly **4 ft of fluorocarbon**, same 40 lb test as the top
  shot; **4-turn surgeon's knot** (as Rig 1).
- **Hook:** **Owner Mutu circle hook**, size **4**, sometimes **2** — same
  circle-hook-for-teeth reasoning as Rig 2. Consistent with the
  Owner-Mutu-by-grade doctrine already logged in
  [hooks — Owner Mutu circle hooks, sized by grade](hooks.md).
- **Ceiling / landing rate:** handles tuna **up to about 100 lb**; his own
  land percentage on this rig is "very good" once the drag is dialed in.
- **Two-speed reminder:** the same low-gear grind as Rig 2 applies once a
  fish is down deep and circling; stay in **high gear** for small/medium
  fish, drop to **low only for a genuinely big fish**, and — his most
  commonly seen mistake — **shift back to high after landing the fish**,
  since anglers celebrating a catch often keep fishing in low without
  noticing. See [two-speed low-gear fight](../techniques/two-speed-low-gear-fight.md).

## Rig 4 — 50 lb ("50 PB"): live bait for grade, and the trolling-lure rig

- **When (live bait):** for a grade in between Rig 3 and a 60 lb outfit —
  roughly **125 lb-class** fish that are too big to fight comfortably on the
  30 lb rig but won't commit to eating on 60 lb line. He is not emphatic that
  every angler needs this rig ("I hesitate to tell people they have to have
  it"), but calls the in-between class "becoming a thing."
- **Same outfit, second job — trolling a lure:** this 50 lb-class outfit also
  carries his favorite trolled tuna lure. Full lure/hook/leader detail (a
  Williamson-brand trolled lure in the "current/cold sniper" class, the
  small-dried-sardine bait-insert detail, the hook and treble-hook doctrine)
  is logged in
  [tuna feathers and skirts — a trolled current/cold-sniper-class lure (Steve Carson, Penn)](../lures/tuna-feathers-and-skirts.md#a-trolled-currentcold-sniper-class-lure-steve-carson-penn)
  rather than duplicated here, since it's lure implementation, not a rod/reel
  spec.
- **Leader (troll application):** tie the lure direct to the 50 lb top shot,
  or — his own build, and his preference when a 125 lb-class grade is
  expected — run a short piece of **100 lb fluorocarbon** ahead of the lure
  so the mainline itself is protected from the fish's teeth (he can't run a
  circle hook on this style of lure, so bite-through risk sits on whatever
  line is closest to the hook).

## Rig ladder for jigging — PE-rated rod classes (Brian, Pacific Coast Sportfishing Show 2024 jigging panel)

A separate, jigging-specific line-class ladder, distinct from the live-bait/
troll rigs above — panelist Brian (surname not confidently resolved from
captions — asr-uncertain), Pacific Coast Sportfishing Show 2024 "Jigging 101"
seminar panel alongside James Kikkawa, George, and Benny Ortiz (`Et2TUskzCSs`,
uploaded 2024-03-16; unregistered channel/panel — medium confidence). His
stated goal was making PE ratings "easily understandable to the West Coast
angler" by tying each class to the jig weight it **activates** (his term —
i.e., the jig weight that actually loads and works the blank) and to the
fighting-drag range the panel runs on bluefin at that class, rather than just
naming a line-test number.

- **PE 6–10:** activates **250–500 g** jigs. Fighting drag runs **~25 lb** as
  the comfortable starting point; some anglers push to **40 lb**, and a few
  claim **45 lb**, but the source is "not willing to test that theory just
  quite yet."
- **PE 4–8:** activates **150–350 g** jigs. Fighting drag **~20 lb up to
  30 lb**, "sometimes 35," but **30 lb** is the realistic ceiling.
- **PE 3–6** (the lightest class): activates **100–250 g** jigs. Fighting
  drag **~15 lb**, max around **25 lb**.

**Spiral-wrapped ("acid-wrap") guides**, standard on these blanks (James,
same panel): the guide train spirals to face progressively downward,
transitioning the rod into something closer to a spinning-rod guide layout
by the tip section. Two stated benefits — less side-to-side twisting play
under heavy load than a conventional guide train, and the geometry stops the
line from **grabbing the rod tip** during the jigging motion (the same
tip-wrap failure mode already logged as the reason for spiral/acid wraps in
[knife jigging](../techniques/knife-jigging.md#gear-class-detail)). The
trade-off: the transition zone is unforgiving of a poorly timed cadence — if
slack line jumps up and wraps a guide right as a big bluefin comes tight on
25 lb of drag, it can take guides off the blank with it.

Cross-reference: [knife / speed jigging](../techniques/knife-jigging.md) and
[slow-pitch jigging](../techniques/slow-pitch-jigging.md) own the mechanics
these classes are fished with; [gear classes](gear-classes.md) is the
class-term lexicon this ladder feeds.

## Drag — weakest-link setting, and a scope-shift trap

- **Setting method:** starting from the reel's **free-spool** position (drag
  lever backed all the way off), roll the drag lever forward by feel
  (pulling the line by hand) until it feels close to right, then confirm with
  a **drag scale**.
- **Weakest-link rule:** identify your setup's weakest link (top shot, braid,
  or a knot you're not fully confident in — have a crewmate re-tie it if
  so), then set the strike drag at **no more than one-third of that link's
  pound test**; his usual target is **25%** rather than the full third,
  reasoning that you can always advance the lever toward full for more drag
  once the fish's initial hard running run is over. Corroborates, without
  adding a new number beyond, the quarter-to-a-third rule already logged in
  [rod & reel selection — drag setting](rod-and-reel-selection.md#drag-setting-general-judgment).
- **A scope-shift trap — spool diameter changes effective drag (mechanism,
  not yet logged elsewhere in this KB):** dropping a jig 400 ft down can
  already put a reel a quarter to a third empty; if the hooked fish then runs
  another 100–200 yd, the spool can end up **two-thirds empty** with the
  drag lever never touched. A smaller-diameter spool multiplies the same
  lever setting into **much higher actual drag** — so a drag that was
  correctly set at hookup may be dangerously tight by the time a deep-hooked
  fish has taken a long run, without the angler having changed anything. Re-check
  (or back off) the drag after a long run on a rig that started deep or
  heavily spooled down, rather than trusting the initial setting through the
  whole fight.
