# Hooks — Style, Size, and Matching to Bait

A hook is chosen on four axes: **style** (how it sets), **size** (matched to the
bait, then the fish), **wire gauge** (how much it burdens a live bait vs. how
much load it can take), and **finish** (bare metal color, for stealth). Getting
the bait to swim naturally and the hook into the corner of the mouth is most of
the game. Live-bait hooking mechanics are in
[fishing live bait](../bait/fishing-live-bait.md); this note is the selection
logic behind them.

## Style — how the hook sets

| Style | Shape | How it sets | Reach for it when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Circle** | Point bent ~90° back toward the shank | **Don't swing.** Engage the reel, point the rod where the fish is going, wind tight — it pins the **corner of the mouth** on its own | Live bait when you can't or shouldn't set: sportboat crowds, clients who can't feel the bite, [bluefin](../species/bluefin-tuna.md) / [yellowfin](../species/yellowfin-tuna.md), [striped marlin](../species/striped-marlin.md) |
| **J hook** | Classic "J", point straight up | **Set it** — lift the rod tip, then wind tight | Reaction eaters and fish that run at the boat; [dorado](../species/dorado.md), island [yellowtail](../species/yellowtail.md), [dropper loop](../techniques/dropper-loop.md) |
| **Treble** | Three points | Self-hooking on the strike | Hard baits / plugs; the trailing hook on a flying-fish rig |
| **Assist** | Short cord + hook lashed to the jig's top | Hooks on the fall/lift | [Knife jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md) and slow-pitch |

**Offset vs. inline** is a separate axis from style, shape, size, wire gauge,
and finish — a hook's point can be bent out from the shank (offset) or kept
straight in line with it (inline), and this shows up on both circle hooks and
J hooks. See [offset hooks](offset-hooks.md) for what it means, named examples,
and why anglers reach for it.

### Circle vs. J — the core decision (`sWRSYCmt4Tw`, `m2q22sPPkEM`)

- **Circle** is the default live-bait hook offshore because it does the work for
  you — the water's tension alone drives it into the jaw hinge, which is why
  long-liners and commercial rock-cod boats use them. Nose-hook the bait and
  just come tight; it's forgiving of anglers who never feel the bite. Because
  there is no manual hookset to fall back on, the drag has to be doing its job —
  see [drag setting](drag-setting.md), which calls a correct drag setting an
  absolute key specifically for circle-hooked larger tuna.
- **J** wins where a circle can't get a good angle: dorado run straight at the
  boat, killing the corner-of-mouth geometry a circle needs, so switch to a J
  and set (`sWRSYCmt4Tw`). J hooks are also the pick for islands yellowtail,
  long-range, and [dropper-loop](../techniques/dropper-loop.md) work. Anglers
  who can feel the bite and like to set favor a long-shank J (e.g. 7/0 for
  marlin) on the theory that the long shank resists being sawed off and lands
  more fish (`m2q22sPPkEM`).
- Fly-lining a bait for **tuna or yellowtail**, give it a solid three count
  before coming tight, without swinging (`R48YdVFfEOI`). On a **circle hook**
  this alone seats it in the corner of the mouth. On a **J hook**, still don't
  swing — raise the rod to about a 45° angle and wind tight, the same
  unhurried motion. This is beginner-framed guidance for anglers new to the
  fishery; anglers who already swing and hook fish that way keep doing what
  works (`R48YdVFfEOI`).
- For light-line bluefin bait fishing, a small J hook goes on a lively bait
  fast — no need to scale the bait first — getting it back in the water
  quickly. A circle hook has a smaller gap from point to shank, making it
  trickier to hold the bait and thread the hook through: a baiting-speed
  tradeoff distinct from the corner-of-mouth hookset reasoning above
  (`icpm7gADxvU`).
- For school-sized yellowfin, yellowtail, and dorado — and for
  [dropper-loop work](../techniques/dropper-loop.md) — a **Mustad 94151** is
  a long-standing go-to over a circle hook: cheap, and roughly twice as fast
  to bait and get back in the water (`jDmHgRNnqhw`). On a kelp paddy holding
  **8–10 lb** dorado, the goal is limiting out off one school, so every extra
  second re-baiting a circle hook is a bait not in the water (`jDmHgRNnqhw`).
  The circle hook is not the end-all solution here — it fits best on light
  line, or on heavy line where chafe against the leader is the concern, an
  abrasion mechanism distinct from the teeth-driven bite-off reasoning below
  (`jDmHgRNnqhw`).
- For **bluefin** specifically, a full-circle, slightly offset point seats in
  the corner of the mouth and keeps the leader off the tuna's teeth, avoiding
  a bite-off (`RfiC8sfIWTk`). Bluefin carry more teeth than yellowfin and can
  saw through a leader; the circle hook's corner-of-mouth hookset keeps the
  leader out of their reach, which is why some anglers who otherwise prefer J
  hooks switch to circle specifically for big bluefin (`RbqOKkINSCM`). Fishing
  **mackerel** for bait, thick-wire Mustad circle hooks run **8/0, 6/0, and
  4/0** — noticeably larger than the 1/0–2/0 sardine-bait circle-hook sizing
  below, consistent with bait-sets-the-size and thick-wire-for-bigger-bait
  (`RbqOKkINSCM`).
- A bare weight threshold, on top of the mechanism above: **circle hooks for
  fish 40 lb and up; a standard J hook is fine under 40 lb** (`YntRJAN88fs`,
  `y0LVT59inEA` — the same 40 lb cutoff and mechanism from the same speaker
  across two dated videos). On a bigger fish a J hook sets deeper, down in the
  mouth or throat, where the leader rubs teeth on every headshake; a circle
  hook, fished on a correctly set drag, drags out and pins the corner of the
  mouth instead, keeping the leader off the teeth entirely. Carry an
  assortment of both circle and J hooks, and downsize hook size — never line
  size — when the bite is finicky (`YntRJAN88fs`). A **line-class-driven J-hook
  sizing rule**: fishing live sardines on Owner Gorilla live-bait J hooks,
  **25 or 30 lb test → a No. 1 or No. 2 hook; 40 or 50 lb test → a 4/0 or 5/0
  hook** (asr-uncertain reading), stocking an assortment across roughly 1
  through 6/0 of both the Owner Gorilla J hook and an Owner circle hook
  (asr-uncertain, plausibly Owner Mutu) (`y0LVT59inEA`).
- A different trigger applies to **wahoo-trip terminal tackle at Alijos
  Rocks**: not teeth or a fixed weight cutoff, but a mismatch between fish
  grade and the line test they're actually biting on — if the fish are 60–80
  lb and only biting 40 or 50 lb test, that calls for a circle hook. No
  manufacturer or size is recommended, since circle-hook sizing varies by
  manufacturer; the advice is to ask a tackle-store dealer for the right
  circle hook at that line test (`uWGGeDOprsE`). Full trip-tackle context in
  [Searcher five-outfit quiver — 7-day trip to Alijos Rocks and "The
  Ridge"](searcher-alijos-rocks-ridge-7-day-quiver.md#terminal-tackle).
- A Cortez Bank charter's pre-trip tackle briefing repeated the same split as
  trip-wide guidance: **circle hooks always for bluefin, J hooks recommended
  for yellowtail** (`UfuiWFVvz2E`).
- For yellowtail up to about 22 lb, a **J hook beats a circle hook** — the one
  exception to an otherwise circle-hook tuna program; a circle "will work" but
  isn't the preference. Sized **2/0**, stepping up to **4/0** for a larger bait
  (`yGXSrUauo2w`).
- One crew, fishing a finicky Cortez/Tanner Bank bluefin bite, preferred J
  over circle for faster baiting and a livelier bait — a single-trip
  preference set against the corner-of-mouth doctrine above, not reconciled
  with it (`LsFMBCa9DOQ`; full account in the [evidence file](evidence/hooks.md)).
- At the **smallest hook sizes**, a J hook can beat a circle: a small J's gap
  is noticeably bigger and easier to pin a bait through than the tighter gap a
  small circle hook leaves, worth the trade for a finicky bite. Faster, easier
  baiting means less handling time and a livelier bait, which itself draws
  more bites. Strike timing follows: on a circle hook at small sizes, give a
  **10 count** before engaging the reel — it needs to turn away and
  swallow-then-eject the bait back into the corner of the mouth. On a J hook
  at these sizes, a **3 count** is enough before putting it in gear, since the
  hookset goes directly into the corner of the mouth rather than waiting on
  the circle's self-hooking mechanism (`48ZFXnCTTQE`). Corroborated on a Baja
  beach roosterfish session with the same 10-count coaching (`mL4Ph7t0WcQ`,
  `mG8ZZLFGlT8`; full accounts in the [evidence file](evidence/hooks.md)).
- On a rockfish [dropper-loop
  gangion](../techniques/dropper-loop.md#rock-cod-quick-gangion-dave-hansen-build),
  circle hooks go on specifically because most charter clients can't feel the
  bite and swing too late — the circle hook seats itself, so the angler only
  has to wind. This is a judgment call about the angler's skill level, not the
  target species; the same source frames it as against his own personal
  J-hook preference (`zVIfArUrpDI`).
- A ringed hook ties straight on; a non-ringed hook works too with a
  [loop knot](../rigging/essential-knots.md). The preferred choice is
  non-ringed + loop knot: less hardware weight on an already-stressed live
  bait, and no ring-on-hook clank to spook a picky bite (`48ZFXnCTTQE`).
- For a bluefin sinker-rig circle hook (2/0–5/0), ring-or-no-ring is
  functionally a personal preference, not a performance rule — bait
  liveliness matters more to the bite than whether the hook is ringed. One
  stated exception: a ringed hook can help specifically on light line with a
  finicky bite. A non-ringed hook is one source's personal, "old school" pick
  (`eehDVb6_GoI`); a milder framing about five years earlier from the same
  channel names the countervailing view that a ringed hook may let the bait
  swim more freely (`y0LVT59inEA`).
- The circle shape — point bent back toward the shank — drives it into the
  corner of the mouth on its own; a J hook instead catches at any point it
  can. Getting bit on a circle hook, **nine times out of ten** the right move
  is to put the reel in gear, wind the handle, and lift the rod — no swing. A
  fly-lined bait or a J hook still needs a hookset, but not a hard swing: let
  the bait get bit, engage the reel, wind, then lift the rod — enough to set
  the hook (`IZHY3RXdyxg`, `PciIsDkBgj4`, `qh3hL2Dt3HY` — the same claim across
  three cuts of one source video).

### Downsize the hook, not the line, to save a slow bluefin bite

When bluefin aren't biting well on a standard circle hook (e.g. a 3/0), the
first lever is dropping **one hook size** — never dropping line test
(`FXWOIB0TPfE`). In the **2019** season, a slow bite had crews running hooks
as small as **1/0 and 2/0** just to get bit, with line class left as-is
(`FXWOIB0TPfE`, `YntRJAN88fs`) — carrying both circle and J assortments is the
reason to keep both sizes on hand rather than one fixed size per species/bait.
A third, later account makes the choice explicit — go down in hook size,
never in line class — with a worked example: a **1/0** circle stepped down to
a **No. 1** circle. Fishing line too light just to get a bite wastes the trip,
because a hooked fish that size can't be landed on under-gunned line. At
**Tanner Bank** in the **2020** season, on **40–60 lb** bluefin, a smaller
hook size got bit better regardless of bait size (`shZCjX2-fkI`).

### Wire gauge

Same style, different wire, does different jobs (`sWRSYCmt4Tw`, `m2q22sPPkEM`):

- **Thin wire** for live bait. A thin-wire hook adds little weight to the bait's
  nose, so a sardine or anchovy swims naturally and draws more bites. Straightening
  a thin-wire hook is rare with a normal drag.
- **Heavy-duty (thick) wire** where strength beats swim — islands yellowtail,
  Guadalupe, dropper loop, and big-fish rigs. Thick wire on a small bait weighs it
  down and swims poorly, so save it for larger baits and heavier targets.
- Fishing small (~3–4 in) anchovies for yellowfin to **40 lb**, on **30 lb**
  leader against **65 lb** braid, a thin-wire hook lacks the surface area and
  shock absorption to take the strain and typically fails outright rather
  than surviving the load (asr-uncertain on the exact failure mechanics).
  Thicker-shank hooks — **Mustad and VMC** named — hold up to repeated heavy
  pulls across multiple fish, stay sharp, keep their barbs, and don't let the
  bait fly off, even fished this small: a same-boat data point for where the
  wire-gauge tradeoff tips toward thick wire on small live bait (`_PGm-TlFU2A`).

### Finish — bronze/black vs. nickel/chrome

For **bait fishing**, run **bronze or black hooks, never nickel-plated or
chrome**, for two stacked reasons (`IMnoZVEYpm4`):

- **Stealth.** Nickel/chrome reflects light underwater; bronze/black goes
  effectively invisible once wet. This matters most for pressured SoCal fish
  that have been fished hard for a long time — [bluefin
  tuna](../species/bluefin-tuna.md) and kelp [calico
  bass](../species/calico-bass.md) named as examples. Swap a buddy onto
  nickel hooks without telling them and stealth alone should out-fish them.
- **Corrosion.** Bronze/black hooks start corroding the moment they're wet —
  one left in a fish's mouth after a break-off rusts away in about a week or
  less. Nickel-plated hooks resist corrosion and last almost indefinitely, so
  a lost nickel hook stays in the fish.

No jig/hardware-hook finish claim is made in this source — it's stated as
live-bait-specific doctrine.

### Factory sharpness — iron trebles vs. wahoo hooks

A fine file (worked across the sides and top of the point) or a retail hook
sharpener both work. **Surface-iron and yo-yo-iron treble hooks generally
don't need it** — factory-sharp with soldered rings, strong and good quality
out of the package (`49joKHD7Umc`). **Wahoo hooks are the exception:**
casting lures, bombs, and live-bait hooks are all sharpened out of the
package before fishing [wahoo bomb](../techniques/wahoo-bomb-casting.md),
because a wahoo's very bony jaw often won't take an unsharpened hook on the
hookset — demonstrated on camera with an already-fished lure showing bite
marks and a used, sharpened hook (`4uNPLknRAQg`).

### Picking by what's biting, mid-trip

On a mixed-species offshore trip, hook choice is decided on the water by
what's actually biting that day (`gevNj2Y1Ep8`):

- **Strictly tuna:** **size 4 circle hooks**.
- **Kelp paddy with dorado or yellowtail:** **size 2 live-bait J hooks**.
  Dorado at full speed swallow the bait and break off often on their teeth —
  carry a **50-count pack**, a hot bite burns through hooks fast.

An earlier trip on the same channel ran a dual-flyline spread with a **size 2
circle hook for bluefin** (flylined on 25–30 lb test) and a **2/0 live-bait J
hook for yellowtail/bonito** (`RfiC8sfIWTk`) — different sizes from the same
channel's later trip above, consistent with a mid-trip, whatever's-biting call
rather than one fixed rule.

## Size — bait first, fish second

Hook numbering: below "1/0" the sizes count **up as they shrink** (1, 2 = small,
light); at and above **1/0** they count up as they **grow** (2/0, 4/0, 6/0 … up to
a big 11/0). The size is really the **gap** measured from the point across to the
shank, so it varies a bit between manufacturers (`HIXTFWlwnM0`).

**The bait sets the hook size; the target fish is secondary** (`HIXTFWlwnM0`,
`m2q22sPPkEM`):

| Bait | Typical hook | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anchovy | **No. 4 thin-wire** (a small number-size, not an ought) | A big hook weighs the anchovy down and the fish see it; smaller = more eats. Historically San Clemente Island bluefin came on No. 4, even No. 8, thin wire — "just hook them" |
| Sardine (~5–7 in) | **2/0** for smaller-grade / picky tuna, up to **4/0–5/0** for mid-grade bluefin | 4/0 thin-wire is the classic sardine hook; nose-hook so it stays out of the bait's way and swims |
| Mackerel | Step up again — bigger hook | Bigger bait carries more hook |
| Live squid | **~6/0 thin-wire off an orange lead head**, or **4/0 with a sliding sinker** | See below |
| Size 1 / 2 | Very small, light duty | Scaling way down for finicky small-grade fish |
| 10/0–11/0 J + heavy treble | Flying-fish / kite rig | Line's out of the water, so hook size doesn't spook |

Across the three West Coast staple baits — anchovy, sardine, mackerel —
mackerel is the largest and sturdiest, so a much larger hook goes in with no
risk of hurting the bait. Anchovy is far more delicate and needs the
opposite: size down to a smaller, lighter hook so hooking it doesn't damage
the bait (`h311A3s-dkY`, `qh3hL2Dt3HY`) — the same bait-sets-the-hook-size
conclusion as the table above, framed here around bait durability rather than
weight/visibility alone.

### Bluefin, first trip

For a first bluefin trip: **1/0 or 2/0 circle hooks, not J hooks** — you want the
hook set in the corner of the mouth (`EyB98RuKfeQ`), paired with a short fluoro
leader (see [line & leader](line-and-leader.md)).

### Owner Mutu circle hooks, sized by grade

Owner Mutu circle hooks size to the line/grade pairing rather than one fixed
size: **size 4** on the lighter (20–50 lb grade) flyline outfit, stepping up
to **size 1/0, or up to 2/0**, on the heavier outfit for bigger fish. All of
one angler's yellowfin, bluefin, and skipjack over the 2021 season were
caught on Owner Mutu circle hooks (`jQW2HLkMsmY`) — a separate, brand-named
data point in range with the size-4 (`gevNj2Y1Ep8`) and 1/0–2/0 (`EyB98RuKfeQ`)
circle-hook figures already logged above.

### Owner Hybrid Mutu — thinner-gauge flyline hook

Owner's **Hybrid Mutu** sits in wire gauge between the light-wire tuna hook
and the Mutu circle hook above — a **thinner gauge**, for a better-swimming
bait. Comes both ringed and unringed; tie on and flyline a sardine
(`0AtmEH6aQt0`), consistent with the thin-wire-for-live-bait rule under **Wire
gauge** above. In stock about a year and a half later, it's a favorite pick at
**size 2** for light-line bluefin bait fishing (20–25 lb test) when the bite
is finicky (`icpm7gADxvU`; full scenario in [flyline — line sizing & bite
guard for finicky
tuna](../techniques/flyline.md#line-sizing--bite-guard-for-finicky-tuna)).

### Owner Ringed Flyliner — Coronado Islands and Channel Islands sizing

As the [Coronado Islands](../species/yellowtail.md#where--when) yellowtail
bite was picking up (dated 2018-11-20), smaller J hooks were the call: Owner
Ringed Flyliner in sizes **1, 2, and 4**, the most popular choice fished on

[halibut](../species/california-halibut.md), and
[yellowtail](../species/yellowtail.md), Owner Flyliner **size 2** is the
first grab, sizing down to **size 4** when anchovies are the bait present
that season and up to **size 1 or 1/0** for bigger bait (`nRIAgz5G_Bc`,
`wl27BWAWpq0` — the same figures repeated in a short re-cut of the same
source).

### Owner Live Bait hook — kelp-paddy dorado/yellowfin starter rig

A simple kelp-paddy rig for [dorado](../species/dorado.md) and
[yellowfin tuna](../species/yellowfin-tuna.md): Owner Live Bait hook, sized
**1 or 1/0** (smaller when bait has been running small), tied to the leader
with an [improved clinch
knot](../rigging/improved-clinch-knot.md#parameters--judgment) (`eZXPqiAtqi8`).
Full rig: a **25–40 lb class conventional reel**, a **3 ft, 25 lb test
fluorocarbon leader** matched to the mono main line's own test, joined with a
[surgeon's knot](../rigging/essential-knots.md#when-to-use-it) passed through
three times (`eZXPqiAtqi8`).

### A finesse-rig hook ladder by grade

Organizing bluefin rigs by line class rather than bait or technique gives

- **30 lb "big fish finesse" rig, 50–70 lb fish:** a small Owner circle
  hook — not the Light or hybrid version — size **4** preferred, size **2**
  the largest. An earlier citation names the model directly as an **Owner
  Mutu** circle hook at the same two sizes, with the preference order

### Gamakatsu Nautilus — lighter-wire alternative for small/weak bait

Owner is "the industry standard" for bluefin fished on 40 or 50 lb test
(sometimes 30 lb) with bigger bait. Across the small/weak sardine seasons of
**2022–2023** specifically (see [bluefin rig ladder by grade — daytime, 25 lb
setup](bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md#daytime-rig-ladder-by-grade-capt-matt-sd-fish-and-sips)),
a **Gamakatsu Nautilus** — a lighter-wire circle hook than the Owner — was the
workhorse instead, sized **4 through 3/0** and matched to line and bait size.
With small bait in the well, it fished down to **size 4, 2, and 1** and still
gave fish a full fight and landed them — a lighter wire holding up at small
sizes, not just a smaller Owner substituted in (`G1YIlakiRqg`).

### Gamakatsu Nautilus circle — regular vs. HD vs. Light

Three Nautilus variants, for tuna or yellowtail out of San Diego
(`YtlD1gQ_ULw`):

- **Nautilus (regular):** size **2/0** called right "for the bait right now"
  on a July 2020 trip, not stated as a fixed rule; **1/0** as an alternative
  (`YtlD1gQ_ULw`).
- **Nautilus HD:** a heavier-gauge step up for bigger fish on heavier line
  (50 or 60 lb), sizes **3/0, 4/0** (a 6/0 top end may also have been named
  but isn't confidently asserted here).
- **Nautilus Light:** explicitly too light for tuna or yellowtail — go with
  the regular Nautilus at 2/0 or step up to the HD instead.

### Live squid for white seabass

Rig squid on an **orange lead head with a ~6/0 thin-wire hook**, or a **4/0
hook with a [sliding sinker](../techniques/sliding-sinker.md)** (`m2q22sPPkEM`).
Fish the **least weight** that works — start light and drop to 1/8 or 1/4 oz —
so the bait sinks **slowly through the water column and along the kelp edge**.
[White seabass](../species/white-seabass.md) feed with the mouth out front,
not on the bottom; a bait lying on the bottom catches rays and shovelnose
sharks instead. A bullet weight slid down over the hook knot (point the
bullet at the rod tip so the hook eye seats up inside it) makes the hook
nearly invisible.

### Cut squid strips for structure/bottom bait

For sheephead, sargo, and small bass — especially as a backup when there's no
live bait — cut squid strips beat a chunk (`yKaHcxX46l4`, `EE0P4SvcNFg`):

- **Prep:** cut the head off (save the head and tentacles for
  [chum](../techniques/chunking.md)); split the body down the middle without
  cutting all the way through and scrape the guts out (into the chum too);
  cut the body into **thin strips**, close to thin noodles, not a thick wad.
- **Hook it at the very tip of the strip only**, spin it around, and bring the
  hook back through so the rest of the strip threads onto the shank and
  **hangs loose below the hook** rather than being bunched up. A big bunched
  chunk is too much for a small mouth to take; a thin hanging strip gets
  sucked straight in and picks up swimming action off the tail.
- **Any hook works** — a small size 2 or size 4, or a small lead head; the
  strip presentation matters more than the specific hook.
- Generalized beyond squid/sheephead to reef fishing at large: a **No. 6 or
  No. 4 hook**, with a small sliding sinker or split shot, is the terminal
  end taught for artificial-reef fishing generally — extending the size range
  one notch smaller than the size-2/size-4 figure above (`bM2vUS1B-yQ`).

### Sheephead — hook, weight, and bait

See [California sheephead](../species/sheephead.md) for the full router
(`OSbAHdB4uPs`).

- **Hook:** No. 2 or No. 4, thin-wire, bronze or black finish — never chrome
  (`OSbAHdB4uPs`). A third species corroborating the bronze/black-over-
  nickel/chrome stealth doctrine under **Finish** above (bluefin, calico
  bass, now sheephead).
- **Weight:** a small slider weight, 1/4 to 1/2 oz, never heavier than 1/2
  oz — enough to get the bait down without it flying by, light enough to
  keep it off bottom.
- Sheephead feed with the mouth at the front of the face, not underneath, so
  a bait on the bottom instead draws shovelnose sharks, bat rays, smoothhound
  sharks, and leopard sharks — the same mouth-position reasoning as white
  seabass above.
- **Bait, ranked:** pismo clam is the top-shelf pick for big sheephead;
  mussel and squid are the everyday options. Cut all three into strips, not
  chunks. Mussel suits a treble hook because it's a big wad of slime with
  nothing else to grip a single point.

### SoCal yellowtail/offshore baseline rotation

For most SoCal live-bait yellowtail and offshore fishing, **2/0 and size 1**
cover nearly everything: 2/0 is sufficient up to **30 lb** fish, and size 1
suits small **3–4 in** sardines or anchovies. A **3/0** is the step-up,
reached for only against **40+ lb** fish (`PrdPJy26H8c`).

### Owner ST-66 — Cedros yellowtail bait-hook upgrade

Fishing a bait heard on camera as "triple trout" (asr-uncertain; see
[Cedros Island](../locations/cedros-island.md) for the bait doctrine) at
Cedros Island, one angler swapped stock hooks for **Owner ST-66**'s: with the
bite running steady enough to be catching more than enough yellowtail on it,
he'd rather carry the stronger hook than risk one failing under volume
(`9kGpcEHqIUc`, `_C7TJoGT0nw`, `rd2Ia8HRbSg` — the same account across three
cuts of one source video). A second, independent data point for the Owner
ST-66 model logged again below for Sea of Cortez cabrilla jerkbaits, here for
a different bait, species, and Baja sub-region (Pacific side, not Cortez).

### Owner ringed offshore hook — Cedros mackerel/sardine slow-troll sizing

For [slow-trolled mackerel](../techniques/slow-trolling-bait.md#cedros-island-baja-pacific--mackerel-bait-and-switch-a-break-from-casting)
at Cedros Island, Owner ringed offshore hooks were carried in **1/0, 3/0, and
5/0** to match whatever bait size the dock had that day — from small sardines
up to **15 in** mackerel large enough that a fish engulfing the bait leaves no
mackerel visible (`_C7TJoGT0nw`, `qHS-ewTQs9Q` — the same trip and lineup
across two source cuts).

### Baja, Sea of Cortez sizing

From a Tony Reyes panga trip, November 2022 (`dEPuDrhoClM`):

- **Hardbait/jerkbait replacement trebles for cabrilla** (leopard grouper):
  size **1/0**, extra-strong wire — a reaction bite, so the hook's job is to
  hold and not straighten on a hard strike from a heavy fish, not to
  disappear like a live-bait hook. Paired with **80–100 lb** rated split
  rings (Owner hyper-wire, sized 6–8; 7 fits the jerkbaits best) (`dEPuDrhoClM`).
- **Live bait / troll hooks for cabrilla** when they're not eating the iron:
  **3/0–5/0**.
- **Bigger hooks** for sizable yellowtail, barracuda, and bonita (to bury the
  shank in a larger bait), and for grouper: roughly **12/0** (uncertain
  between 12/0 and 14/0, preserved as stated).

Named jerkbait-replacement models, from a later trip (`EmZO8QiOfik`): on
Current Sniper-class jerkbaits, whose stock hook runs light and thin, upgrade
**one hook size bigger and one wire gauge heavier** than stock — either an
**Owner STX, size 1, Z-wire** (lighter, keeps the bait's swimming action, the
finicky-bite pick) or an **Owner ST-66, size 1, 1X heavier wire** (holds up
better in a hot, wide-open bite, at some cost to swimming action). This
plausibly names the garbled model from the trip above, logged as a separate,
later attribution rather than merged into it. See
[jerkbaits](../lures/jerkbaits.md).

General bait/chunk and grouper hooks (`M8hOYQ_6rSg`):

- Nothing smaller than **8/0** for mackerel or other cut bait; an **8/0–12/0**
  range covers the general assortment, including chunk bait fished for
  grouper and pargo on the reefs.
- A big grouper or black sea bass has a heavy, wide jaw unlike a tuna's, so
  the hook needs a big, wide gap and a good cutting point to punch through
  the jawbone rather than just find flesh. Two rig styles are fished — a
  [trap-hook pair](../rigging/trap-rig.md) and a straight single hook — both
  built on Jobu hooks in this source's preference. A [grouper leader
  build](../techniques/sliding-sinker.md#baja-sea-of-cortez-grouper-leader-cesar)
  runs a **12/0-class Jobu hook**, corroborating the ~12/0 grouper-hook figure
  above from a second, independent source and trip — that specific figure
  reaches high confidence on this repetition.
- Replacement hooks for cabrilla jerkbaits are called important to carry,
  with no sizing or split-ring detail beyond the extra-strong-wire
  treble/split-ring specifics already given above.

### Mustad 94150 / 94151 — the historical anchovy/sardine J-hook baseline

A standard No. 2 Mustad J hook predates Owner and Gamakatsu hooks and predates
circle hooks by decades (`SCQnyVEQfHY`):

- **Mustad 94150** — the No. 2 anchovy hook, dating from roughly **40–50
  years** before the 2020 citation (predating approximately 1970–1980), from
  when anchovy was fished more than sardine.
- **Mustad 94151** — the No. 2 sardine hook, dated to when sardine came back
  into use as the dominant bait.

Both are framed as belonging in every tackle box then and still today — a
durability claim rather than a selection mechanism; no wire gauge, finish, or
rigging detail is given beyond No. 2 and the two model numbers. A named-model
data point consistent with the bait-sets-the-hook-size doctrine above
(anchovy/sardine sizing under **Size — bait first, fish second**), not merged
into that table since the source gives no separate sizing logic of its own.
A Mustad 94151 remained one angler's personal go-to J hook two years later,
for school-sized yellowfin, yellowtail, and dorado and for dropper-loop
work — see **Circle vs. J** above (`jDmHgRNnqhw`).
