# Searcher Tackle Tip Thursday — Big/Giant Tuna Rig Ladder (Steve Carson, Penn)

Two entries from Penn's own "Tackle Tip Thursday" series, filmed aboard the
**Searcher** (SearcherSportfishing channel) and presented by Penn's Steve
Carson. Moved/created here (split out of
[bluefin rig ladder by grade](bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md), 2026-08-18)
to keep that note under this repo's size guideline and to hold this channel's
own two-video tuna-rig progression together in one place. **Name note:** the
2020 video's auto-captions render the presenter's name "Steve Clarkson"; the
2023 video's captions render it "Steve Carson." Both introduce themselves as
Penn's tackle rep aboard the Searcher, in the same "Tackle Tip Thursday"
series — treated here as the same person, with each video's own caption
spelling preserved rather than silently merged (flagged asr-uncertain).

Distinct from, and a companion to, the line-class ladders in
[bluefin rig ladder by grade](bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md) and the
application-based quiver in
[rod & reel selection](rod-and-reel-selection.md).

## One-reel pick for giant tuna (150–250 lb+) — endurance over max drag (Steve Clarkson, Searcher Sportfishing, 2020-03-19)

Steve Clarkson, aboard the **Searcher**, speaking at the Fred Hall Show
(`0E9REoTjKrY`, "Tackle Tip Thursday Vol. 91 (Giant Tuna)," uploaded
2020-03-19; unregistered channel — medium confidence). Framed for anglers
after **150–250 lb+** bluefin specifically — heavier grade than the ladders
in [bluefin rig ladder by grade](bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md) target.

- **Reel — a Penn International 16VISX-class two-speed** (captioned "16 V is
  X" — asr-uncertain on the exact model designation, but named in the same
  breath as Penn International 80 and 130, so read as the same reel family,
  a size or two down). His stated reasoning is angler **endurance, not
  stopping power**: the big Penn International 80s and 130s can only be
  "held up for about 10 minutes" before an angler is "done for the day,"
  while the 16VISX-class reel is light enough that "even an old guy like me
  can hold it all day, wait for that big bite." Kept side by side with, not
  a correction of, the heavier 130 lb+ braid boat-driven doctrine in
  [fighting a big bluefin](../techniques/fighting-big-bluefin.md) — this is
  a hand-fought, angler-endurance framing rather than a boat-does-the-work
  one.
- **Daytime application:** fish live mackerel or live sardine on this same
  reel, same as with lighter gear.
- **Nighttime application — jig class and two non-negotiables:** a
  **flat-fall-class jig** (captioned "fall type jig"), **roughly 6–12 oz
  (≈170–340 g)** — he cross-checks his own example, a **320 g** jig, against
  "about 10 oz," which doesn't fully reconcile (320 g ≈ 11.3 oz) but is
  preserved as stated rather than corrected. Two things he calls
  non-negotiable for the night bite specifically: the jig must **glow in the
  dark**, and it needs **big, strong hooks**. Sits within, not beyond, this
  note's existing 150–400 g night knife-jig range in
  [knife jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md); his glow-is-essential framing is a
  **live conflict, kept side by side** with Capt. Dave Hansen's stated view
  elsewhere in this KB that glow-in-the-dark is "angler-confidence
  marketing, not a proven fish preference" (see
  [knife jigs — color](../lures/knife-jigs.md#color--mostly-angler-confidence-with-one-real-mechanism)).
- **Observed catch credited to this exact combo:** a **315 lb bluefin**, 59
  mi off Point Loma — full catch detail in
  [bluefin tuna](../species/bluefin-tuna.md#where--when).

## Three-rig ladder for big tuna, 80 lb up to 130 lb (Steve Carson, Penn/Searcher, 2023-07-13)

Steve Carson, Penn Fishing Tackle, aboard the **Searcher**
(`6wVlDDno2TE`, "Tackle Tip Thursday Vol. 224 (Big Tuna Tackle)," uploaded
2023-07-13; unregistered channel/speaker — medium confidence). Framed as
"the three rigs you want to have for Big Tuna," presented lightest to
heaviest; he separately references a "five rigs — don't leave the dock
without them" list (discussed elsewhere with a co-host named "Art," not in
this transcript) and identifies the lightest rig below as **rig number four**
of that list.

### Rig — 80 lb (60 lb viable), the all-around light-tuna/jig rig

- **When:** tuna from roughly **50–75 lb up to about 150 lb** — his stated
  "light rig for Big Tuna," though he frames a 150 lb-class local tuna as a
  comparatively recent occurrence rather than a historical constant (no
  specific date given on camera), so this is a bigger-fish rig than the name
  suggests. A **60 lb** version is viable if the fish aren't running as big;
  60 lb casts a little easier than 80.
- **Reel:** Penn Fathom 40 Narrow, **two-speed** — stressed as important:
  "a single-speed reel just... is not going to work for this." He also names
  a second reel option, captioned **"the 16 arrow"** (asr-uncertain — not
  confidently resolved to a specific model; distinct from the Penn
  International 16VISX named below, since that reel is documented on this
  same channel as a heavier 100 lb-class pick, not paired with 80 lb braid).
- **Line:** fill most of the way with **80 lb braid**; on his own personal
  rig, roughly **25 yd of 80 lb mono top shot** underneath. Cross-checked on
  camera with a crewmate ("Mike"): a top shot of roughly **a boat length**
  is the rule of thumb (captioned "bolt length" — read here as "boat
  length" from context; asr-uncertain) — the Searcher itself is about 95 ft
  (~33 yd) — so **something between 25 and 50 yd** is the right range.
- **Why mono over straight braid for the top shot (his stated reasons):**
  more abrasion resistance, better casting ability, a little stretch, and —
  "most important" — when this angler's fish tangles with another angler's
  big bluefin, mono is a lot easier for the crew to untangle than
  braid-to-braid.
- **Jig pairing:** 170–220 g jigs, fishable down to roughly 280–300 ft (the
  practical depth limit for that jig size).
- **Other applications on the same outfit:** [yo-yo iron](../techniques/yo-yo-iron.md)
  for yellowtail, [Wahoo bomb casting](../techniques/wahoo-bomb-casting.md) for
  wahoo — called a "great all-around rig for fishing on the Searcher," and
  popular on the boat's longer trips.
- **Rod:** not sized specifically for this rig in this segment, but see the
  general rod-length/rating notes below, which apply across all three rigs.
- **An earlier data point for this same rig, same speaker (Steve Carson,
  "Tackle Tip Thursday Vol. 189 (Yoyos for Yellows)," `ETw_3AFxEcM`,
  2022-07-14 — a year before the 2023-07-13 video above):** the same **Penn
  Fathom 40 Narrow**, two-speed, on a **7–7.5/8 ft rod** rated **30–60, 30–80,
  or as heavy as 40–100**, described here with **50–60 lb test top shot over
  80 lb braid** — a different top-shot line class than the ~25 yd of **80 lb
  mono** top shot given for the 2023 build above; kept side by side rather
  than reconciled, since both are stated by the same speaker on the same
  channel for what reads as the same underlying rig. This 2022 tip frames the
  rig as good for **any trip longer than four days**, and adds a **third
  application** to the two already logged above: it doubles as the
  **standard live-bait fly-lining rig** for **50–150 lb yellowfin tuna** at
  **Guadalupe Island** on the Searcher's six-day trips — see
  [yellowfin tuna](../species/yellowfin-tuna.md#where--when). Framed overall
  as "the rig that does the most number of different things for you when you
  come out on the Searcher."

### Rig — Penn International 16VISX, 100 lb

- **Who it's for:** explicitly framed around accessibility rather than only
  bigger fish — anglers who are "more mature," may have medical issues, or
  simply don't have the off-season ability to lift heavy weights.
- **Reel:** Penn International 16VISX, filled with **100 lb braid**; top
  shot only about **10 yd of 100 lb mono** — much shorter than the 80 lb
  rig's, for two stated reasons: he's fishing deeper on this rig and braid
  sinks better than mono, and he wants more braid yardage in reserve because
  a bigger fish hooked on this rig will run longer.
- **Reel weight:** roughly **36 oz** (his own estimate, not looked up on
  camera).
- **Rod:** **7 ft**, sometimes 7 ft 6 in or longer — explicitly not the
  "short five foot two inch rods" of the 1980s — rated **80–130 lb**.
- **Grade:** capable of landing 300 lb+ tuna. **Observed** (SearcherSportfishing,
  `6wVlDDno2TE`, 2023-07-13, location not stated on camera): Steve Carson
  states this exact reel/rod combo has landed a **313-pounder** — his own
  catch ("ask me how I know that"), no location or date given for the catch
  itself.

### Rig — Penn International 20VISX, 100–130 lb

- **Who it's for:** anglers who want (or are willing to take on) more
  stopping power for the biggest tuna, regardless of the physical toll —
  framed as the answer if "you're persistent enough, or young and strong
  enough."
- **Reel:** Penn International 20VISX, **100 or 130 lb braid**.
- **Reel weight:** **53 oz**, up from the 16VISX's 36 oz — "kind of a jump":
  a large increase in line capacity, plus a beefier frame, bigger handle, and
  bigger drag material.
- **Rod:** rated **80–150 lb** (heavier than the 16VISX rig's 80–130 rating).
- **Grade:** framed against a hypothetical **375-pounder** — landing one that
  big would "probably take all the deckhands on the Searcher."

### General rod principle across all three rigs — fish the middle of the rating

**Stated rule (Steve Carson):** on any rod, fish **the middle of its printed
line rating**, not the high end and not the low end, "to get the money that
you spent on a good quality rod" — the rod performs best there. Worked
examples from this same segment: 100 lb test sits in the middle of an
80–130 lb-rated rod (the 16VISX rig above); by the same logic the 20VISX
rig's heavier 80–150 lb rod is matched to 100–130 lb line rather than to the
rod's rated extremes.
