# Fishing Live Bait

Live bait is the backbone of SoCal fishing — sardine, mackerel, and anchovy fished
alive for [yellowtail](../species/yellowtail.md),
[white seabass](../species/white-seabass.md),
[calico bass](../species/calico-bass.md),
[halibut](../species/california-halibut.md),
[bluefin](../species/bluefin-tuna.md), and
[yellowfin](../species/yellowfin-tuna.md). The whole game is a **lively bait**,
the **right hook**, and the **right hook position** for the swim you want.
Catching and sourcing bait is in [making bait](making-bait.md); keeping it alive
is in [bait tanks](bait-tanks.md); this note is how you hook it and fish it.

## Match the hatch (mscHk0qiXnk)

Pick the bait **species and size to what the gamefish are eating**. San Diego has
sardine 24/7 from the bait barge, but it isn't always the best option — **sardine
isn't the heartiest bait, and the fish aren't always keyed on it** (mscHk0qiXnk).
When the gamefish are chewing mackerel, a **live mackerel** out-fishes a sardine.
Same logic on size: match the length of the bait class the fish are feeding on.
Making your own mackerel is in [making bait](making-bait.md).

## Hook position by situation

Hook the bait for the **swim** you want. The nose spot works on all three staple
baits — the **clear spot on the end of the nose, right before the eyes** — on
mackerel, sardine, and anchovy alike (SluBXkT3cuw).

| Hook position | Where | What the bait does | Reach for it when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Nose** | Clear spot in front of the eyes (one nostril to the other) | Swims naturally, stays up; can be **slow-trolled** or **[fly-lined](../techniques/flyline.md)** | The all-purpose hook; also sends the bait **down a [dropper loop](../techniques/dropper-loop.md)** (T3p1mrqNjIo, mscHk0qiXnk) |
| **Collar / shoulder / dorsal** | Through the shoulder or top, ahead of the dorsal | Keeps your **line up out of the water**, holds the bait **high, skating across the top** for a reaction bite | Offshore / long-range, when you want a stealthier presentation with the line off the water (mscHk0qiXnk) |
| **Belly** | Just above the anal fin | Sends the bait **down and away** from the boat, deeper in the column | Getting deeper; tuna on a paddy (T3p1mrqNjIo, mscHk0qiXnk, raUPkuaFXpw) |
| **Butt** | Near the vent, under the two rear fins | **Fly-lines great, swims well away and a bit deeper**, and **defeats sea lions** at the islands that pick off nose-hooked baits | Sea lions are eating your nose-hooked baits at the islands (T3p1mrqNjIo, 8YvmROeVL-0) |

On a butt/belly hook, hold the **two rear fins together and go right under the
"carnage"** if you set the hook too deep — that keeps a clean, swimming bait
(8YvmROeVL-0).

### Shoulder hook and a bone-anchored butt hook, for a finicky tensioned bite (Cesar, Eric's Tackle, via Dirty Hookers, 48ZFXnCTTQE, 2023-11-01; `medium` confidence — registered voice, but this specific mechanism is a single mention; his other logged videos are still pending extraction, so it isn't yet repeated doctrine)

When fishing a finicky bite with any tension on the line (rolling the reel to
keep a bait swimming), a **nose hook drags the bait's head back toward the
boat** — exactly the wrong direction. Two positions solve this:

- **Shoulder hook** (bait swims out more on the surface, may swim down a
  little): cradle the bait in the tank, thumb and index finger gently over its
  eyes to calm it, and run the hook through at roughly **where your thumb's
  knuckle sits, about an eighth of an inch down** from there.
- **Butt hook, anchored on the bone behind the fin** (sends the bait down and
  away — reach for this when fish are holding in deeper water): don't just
  hook sideways across the belly fin — that pulls out easily. Instead, cradle
  the bait from the top with its head in the heel of your hand and your thumb
  and index finger controlling the tail, start the hook from the tail side
  behind the fin, run it through the body cavity (there's a bone there you'll
  often feel/hit), and bring it back out the front side of that same fin —
  hooking **over the top of a bone** that anchors the hook so it won't pull
  free even on a hard cast. Orient the hook so its **eye faces the tail**: when
  you roll the reel and pull the bait backward, the fish feels wrong pointed
  tail-first and swims **down and away** from the boat — harder rolling
  pressure makes it want to dive and flee faster, the opposite problem a
  nose-hooked bait has under the same tension. Contrast: a plain sideways hook
  through the belly, just above that fin, pulls free if it misses the bone —
  the anchored version behind the fin holds even on a hard cast.

These positions solve the tension problem the nose hook has for
[flyline](../techniques/flyline.md) fishing specifically; see that note for
when to reach for each position by water column.

## Bait selection is king

**Roughly 90% of live-bait success is bait selection** (SluBXkT3cuw):

- Fish the **hardest bait to catch in the tank** — the **lively lime-green** one
  with **scales intact, no red/bloody nose**, that's already swimming strong
  (SluBXkT3cuw, raUPkuaFXpw).
- Cull the **easy-to-catch, bloody, scaly, lethargic** baits into the **chum
  bucket** or over the side — don't fish them (raUPkuaFXpw, SluBXkT3cuw).
- **60-second rule:** if a bait isn't **pulling line off the reel** shortly after
  it hits the water — swimming away, not sitting by the boat — **wind it in, flick
  it off, and grab a fresh one.** "We're not marrying these baits, we're speed
  dating" (SluBXkT3cuw, 8YvmROeVL-0).

## Hook size and style

- **Match the hook to the bait, not to the fish** — an oversized hook stops the
  bait swimming right and it won't get bit. **Bait selection sets it, then size to
  the bait in your hand.** Full selection logic (bait-by-bait sizes, thin vs.
  heavy wire, circle vs. J) is in [hooks](../tackle/hooks.md).
- A **1/0 or 2/0 circle hook** is a common all-around live-bait size; a circle
  pins the **corner of the mouth** on its own — engage the reel, point the rod
  where the fish is going, and wind tight instead of swinging (raUPkuaFXpw).
- **Circle-hook the bait correctly:** hold the hook **high on the arch and press
  straight down** into the bait. Holding it from the bottom the way you would a
  J hook **leaves no gap and squeezes the bait into a "potato chip"** (8YvmROeVL-0).

## Fly-lining vs. weighted

- **Fly-line** (no weight) is the most natural presentation — the bait swims out
  and away on its own. **Cast well away from the boat:** fish are finickier close
  to the hull, and bites drop off sharply near the boat (raUPkuaFXpw). Execution
  detail is in [flyline](../techniques/flyline.md).
- **Weighted** when you need to beat current or reach fish holding down: a
  [sliding sinker](../techniques/sliding-sinker.md) to sink a bait along the kelp
  edge, or a [dropper loop](../techniques/dropper-loop.md) to put it on the
  bottom for [yellowtail](../species/yellowtail.md).
- **Keep tension the moment a butt/belly bait lands** — a little pull makes it
  **swim away from you like a propeller**; give it slack and it turns and swims
  right back under the boat (8YvmROeVL-0).

## Chumming with live bait (lxFNVdDhMy4)

Live bait doubles as chum: throwing bait draws a school **up toward the surface
and into casting range**. On a charter for [yellowfin](../species/yellowfin-tuna.md)
or [skipjack](../species/skipjack-tuna.md), a couple of minutes of hard chumming
can raise fish holding 40–50 ft down right up under the boat (lxFNVdDhMy4). Fish
the **down-drift / down-wind side** — the drift carries your bait away from the
boat and keeps the wind at your back for the cast.

## Bridling big baits

For a large bait (big mackerel for tuna or marlin), a **bridle** — a loop of
floss or a rubber band lashed to the hook and passed through the tough spot ahead
of the eyes — hangs the bait off the hook instead of driving the hook through
flesh. The bait swims longer and stronger, and the open hook gap sits clear for a
better hookup. (General big-bait practice; not covered in the SoCal deckhand
clips above.)
