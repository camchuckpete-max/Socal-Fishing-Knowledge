# Fishing Live Bait

Live bait is the backbone of SoCal fishing — sardine, mackerel, and anchovy fished
alive for [yellowtail](../species/yellowtail.md),
[white seabass](../species/white-seabass.md),
[calico bass](../species/calico-bass.md),
[halibut](../species/california-halibut.md),
[bluefin](../species/bluefin-tuna.md), and
[yellowfin](../species/yellowfin-tuna.md). The whole game is a **lively bait**,
the **right hook**, and the **right hook position** for the swim you want.
Catching and sourcing bait is in [making bait](making-bait.md); keeping it alive
is in [bait tanks](bait-tanks.md); this note is how you hook it and fish it.

## Match the hatch

Pick the bait species and size to what the gamefish are eating. San Diego has
sardine 24/7 from the bait barge, but it isn't always the best option — sardine
isn't the heartiest bait, and the fish aren't always keyed on it (`mscHk0qiXnk`).
When the gamefish are chewing mackerel, a live mackerel out-fishes a sardine.
Same logic on size: match the length of the bait class the fish are feeding on.
Making your own mackerel is in [making bait](making-bait.md).

## Hook position by situation

Hook the bait for the swim you want. The nose spot works on all three staple
baits — the clear spot on the end of the nose, right before the eyes — on
mackerel, sardine, and anchovy alike (`SluBXkT3cuw`).

| Hook position | Where | What the bait does | Reach for it when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Nose** | Clear spot in front of the eyes (one nostril to the other) | Swims naturally, stays up; can be **slow-trolled** or **[fly-lined](../techniques/flyline.md)** | The all-purpose hook; also sends the bait **down a [dropper loop](../techniques/dropper-loop.md)** (`T3p1mrqNjIo`, `mscHk0qiXnk`) |
| **Collar / shoulder / dorsal** | Through the shoulder or top, ahead of the dorsal | Keeps your **line up out of the water**, holds the bait **high, skating across the top** for a reaction bite | Offshore / long-range, when you want a stealthier presentation with the line off the water (`mscHk0qiXnk`) |
| **Belly** | Just above the anal fin | Sends the bait **down and away** from the boat, deeper in the column | Getting deeper; tuna on a [kelp paddy](../conditions/kelp-paddies.md) (`T3p1mrqNjIo`, `mscHk0qiXnk`, `raUPkuaFXpw`) |
| **Butt** | Near the vent, under the two rear fins | **Fly-lines great, swims well away and a bit deeper**, and **defeats sea lions** at the islands that pick off nose-hooked baits | Sea lions are eating your nose-hooked baits at the islands (`T3p1mrqNjIo`, `8YvmROeVL-0`) |

On a butt/belly hook, hold the two rear fins together and go right under the
"carnage" if you set the hook too deep — that keeps a clean, swimming bait
(`8YvmROeVL-0`). The same down-and-away fix also stops harbor seals, not just
sea lions, from grabbing bait once they start following a chum line at the
islands (`Q-gQuOegAx4`).

A nose-hooked bait doubles as a bite-getter on the retrieve: winding one in to
swap it after roughly two or three minutes of soak still draws bites, because
it looks like a natural swimming bait coming in — named as the primary sardine
hook position for [yellowtail](../species/yellowtail.md) (`MuC0uGKDzxg`).

### Sardine

The nose hook goes side to side through the clear spot right at the tip of the
nose — not chin-to-top or top-to-chin — the only entry point that works. The
butt hook (side to side through the body, just above the anal vent) is the
option for anglers who specifically want the bait to swim down: hooked there, a
sardine tends to swim in the direction opposite the pull, typically down and
away from the boat — the actual mechanism behind the table's "swims away"
behavior above. Trade-off: the butt hook draws blood, so that bait doesn't last
as long as a nose-hooked one (`ZggReeO1nyU`).

### Anchovy: nose (weighted) vs. gill hook by rig

Anchovies split from the sardine/mackerel nose-hook default depending on
whether the rig carries weight (`ZggReeO1nyU`):

- **Any weighted rig** (a [sliding sinker](../techniques/sliding-sinker.md), a
  [dropper loop](../techniques/dropper-loop.md), or simply a swivel added to
  the line): nose-hook side to side through the small white spot of cartilage
  at the very tip of the nose. That cartilage takes the hook without hurting
  the bait, so it lives and swims naturally as the weight pulls it up or down
  through the column.
- **Gill / collar hook — "the strategic way" (`ZggReeO1nyU`):** sized to a
  No. 2 or No. 4 hook in practice (see [hooks](../tackle/hooks.md) for full
  anchovy sizing). Enter and exit right where the small fin near the
  gill/collar sits, barely passing the hook through — don't come out the top
  of the bait. Diagnostic: a clean gill hook draws no blood; if it bleeds,
  the hook went too deep. Two reasons to gill-hook at all: anchovies are a
  genuinely hard bait to cast, and the collar orientation adds
  resistance/friction that helps punch the cast into wind; and the hook then
  lies flush alongside the bait's body as it swims rather than sticking out,
  less visible to the fish.

Collar-hooking is also the preferred flyline position for anchovy — it swims
noticeably stronger that way, though anglers generally struggle more fly-lining
anchovy than other baits. Because anchovy draws bycatch from nearly everything
in the water, a fly-lined one pulls bycatch as readily as the target fish; see
[flyline — anchovy
bycatch](../techniques/flyline.md#anchovy-bycatch-when-to-switch-off-flyline)
for the fix (`8TO-tuYjwWk`).

### Mackerel: butt hook (no weight) vs. nose hook (any weight) vs. back/dorsal hook (kite only)

Only two positions get used on mackerel day to day — not the collar/gill hook
used on anchovy above, and not the back hook except on the kite (`w5_x6kkN-xE`).
Region: SoCal (San Clemente Island, Catalina, Coronado Islands), targeting
yellowtail; the same rig also takes bluefin and yellowfin tuna on the foamers,
and white seabass.

- **Butt hook** — through the meat back near, but not through, the vent — for
  fly-lining with zero weight only. A butt-hooked bait doesn't register a pull
  as an angler pulling it — it reads as something biting its tail, so the bait
  bolts the opposite way, down and away from the pull. Applied cast: throw the
  bait to the edge of a visible school or foamer, then give the tail a jerk on
  the water — the bait swims away from the jerk and straight toward the fish.
- **Nose hook** — through the clear cartilage spot in front of the eyes —
  becomes mandatory the moment any weight is added (dropper loop, sliding
  sinker, rubber-core sinker). A butt-hooked bait under a sinker gets dragged
  backward as the weight pulls it down, and a mackerel swimming backward
  doesn't read as a natural, feeding-fish silhouette; nose-hooked, the bait
  still kicks and swims head-first as it sinks.
- **Back / dorsal hook** — right in front of the dorsal fin, a hard spot that
  holds the hook well — reserved for a live mackerel fished off a kite for big
  bluefin (also drew yellowtail and calico bass on the kite). Hooked here the
  bait sits flat on the surface and flutters/"daps" each time it touches down,
  without the leader dragging through the water where a leader-shy tuna can see
  it — a simpler, live-bait alternative to the pre-rigged dead bait in [flying
  fish harness](../rigging/flying-fish-harness.md); see [kite
  fishing](../techniques/kite-fishing.md) for when to fly a bait at all.

Yellowtail have been seen eating mackerel up to 1.5 lb, sometimes in groups of
five or six fish attacking one mackerel at once — territorial behavior. Fishing
live mackerel (rather than the smaller sardine or anchovy) at San Clemente
Island, Catalina, or the Coronado Islands also skews the catch toward
yellowtail specifically, screening out calico bass, barracuda, and bonito
bycatch that a smaller bait draws — reach for mackerel when the goal is
straight yellowtail and those species are unwanted (`w5_x6kkN-xE`).

### Wahoo

On a live sardine for [wahoo](../species/wahoo.md), three hook positions are
used: sideways through the nose ("like a swim bait"), shoulder-hooked just in
front of the dorsal fin, and belly-hooked back toward the tail — matching the
nose / collar-shoulder-dorsal / belly positions above, here independently
confirmed for wahoo specifically, though no mechanism is given for choosing one
over another (`vNIazq1aVwc`). Leader and hookset detail from this source are in
[haywire twist — wahoo live-bait
leader](../rigging/haywire-twist.md#wahoo-live-bait-leader) and [wahoo — bite
response on live bait](../species/wahoo.md#doctrine--conflicts).

### Belly vs. butt hook for tuna and other game fish

Belly hook (just above the two lower fins) is the default over the butt hook
specifically for tuna, yellowtail, and other game fish (`UP_3fBu7VPA`):

- **Reaction-bite mechanism (`UP_3fBu7VPA`):** a belly-hooked bait, cast or
  fly-lined, "shoots out" and swims with an urgency that triggers a reaction
  strike — the primary reason to reach for it on tuna specifically.
- **Avoids internal-organ damage the butt hook risks:** the belly hook catches
  only a small amount of cartilage; a butt hook placed too far forward can
  catch internal organs and cause the bait to bleed, both shortening its life
  and putting off fish keying on a lively, undamaged bait.
- **Forces fresh bait:** a properly belly-hooked bait tends to wind off the
  hook on retrieve after its initial soak, forcing the angler to grab a fresh
  bait rather than re-fish a spent one — a good habit this hook position
  enforces, not an accident.
- **Reach for a nose hook instead** when casting at a visible target — pitching
  to boiling fish, or punching a bait into kelp at [calico
  bass](../species/calico-bass.md) — where distance matters; or in heavy
  current at the islands, or on a slow soak rather than an active presentation
  ("island fishing for the most part"). Most working deckhands belly-hook the
  bait they hand out nine times out of ten, reserving nose for these specific
  situations.

### Wishbone and shoulder hooking for distant bluefin

Any sinker at all — a sinker rig for bluefin or a [dropper
loop](../techniques/dropper-loop.md) for yellowtail, from as light as a half
ounce up through 16 oz — calls for a nose hook across the bridge of the nose,
on sardine or mackerel (`aD0Iim9C15o`, `QHY5kmU7OTU`). Two situational hook
positions for a fly-lined (no-weight) bait:

- **The sardine "wishbone" (`aD0Iim9C15o`, `QHY5kmU7OTU`)** — a bony structure near a couple of small fins in
  the chest, compared to a turkey or chicken wishbone, a strong anchor point
  for the hook — for fish sitting close under the boat, typically holding
  30–40 ft down, where a sinker would only restrict the bait's own dive. The
  tell: anglers around you hooking up within 10–15 seconds of a bait hitting
  the water. Hooked there, the bait doesn't need to be cast — just dropped or
  flipped out roughly 10 ft from the boat — and it dives straight down toward
  tuna or yellowtail holding underneath. Trade-off: a wishbone-hooked bait dies
  quicker — roughly 3 minutes of life versus 10–15 minutes hooked further
  toward the tail — and the hook tends to tear out as soon as the angler
  starts reeling in.
- **Shoulder hooking (`aD0Iim9C15o`, `QHY5kmU7OTU`)** — entered behind the head, in the nape-of-neck area,
  deep enough to hold but not so deep as to hit the spine (which kills the
  bait) — for bluefin boiling 50–150 (up to 200-plus) yards off the boat and not
  coming closer. As the bait swims away the hook folds down, cutting resistance
  so the bait carries more of its own distance from the boat under its own
  power, and riding folded down it's less visible to a tuna looking up from
  below than a hook standing proud on the nose. After letting the bait out
  100–150 yards, wind it back in slowly rather than leaving it planted — bites
  often come as the bait works its way back toward the boat.

### Hooking under tension (flyline)

Fishing a finicky bite with any tension on the line (rolling the reel to keep a
bait swimming), a nose hook drags the bait's head back toward the boat — the
wrong direction. Two positions fix this (`48ZFXnCTTQE`):

- **Shoulder hook** (bait swims out more on the surface, may swim down a
  little): cradle the bait in the tank, thumb and index finger gently over its
  eyes to calm it, and run the hook through at roughly where a thumb's knuckle
  sits, about 1/8 in down from there.
- **Butt hook, anchored on the bone behind the fin** (sends the bait down and
  away — for fish holding in deeper water): cradle the bait in the heel of
  your hand with your thumb and index finger controlling the tail, then start
  the hook from the tail side behind the fin, run it through the body cavity
  (there's a bone there you'll often feel/hit), and bring it back out the
  front side of that same fin, hooking over the top of the bone so it won't
  pull free even on a hard cast — a plain sideways hook through the belly,
  just above that fin, pulls free if it misses the bone. Orient the hook eye
  toward the tail: rolling the reel
  pulls the bait backward, tail-first, and it swims down and away from the
  boat — harder rolling pressure makes it dive and flee faster, the opposite of
  a nose-hooked bait's response under the same tension.

These positions solve the tension problem a nose hook has for
[flyline](../techniques/flyline.md) fishing specifically; see that note for
when to reach for each position by water column.

### Rockfish

For [rockfish](../species/rockfish-lingcod.md), nose or collar hook both work —
rockfish don't discriminate much on presentation — and belly-hooking isn't
recommended, since a belly-hooked bait tends to rip off the hook on the drop to
depth. For anchovy specifically (a genuinely fragile bait to keep hooked),
entering through the bottom of the jaw and out through the top seats more hook
and holds better on the drop than a simple nose hook. Any sinker on the line
still means nose hook, full stop, regardless of bait species (`UP_3fBu7VPA`).

### Deep sinker-rig bait: lower jaw through the nose to keep the mouth shut

On a bluefin sinker rig (rubber-band or the ring-and-tag-line "drop shot"
variant — see [rubber-band deep
rig](../rigging/rubber-band-deep-rig.md#ring-and-tag-line-variant-drop-shot-rig--built-to-reach-fish-too-deep-for-the-rubber-band-rig-capt-matt-sd-fish-and-sips)),
hook through the lower jaw and up through the tip of the nose, which keeps the
bait's mouth shut on the way down. Anglers who struggle to land that entry
point cleanly — it can beat the bait up trying to thread it — can instead go
simply through the mouth and out the tip of the nose (`G1YIlakiRqg`).

## Handling bait from tank to hook

Lean the rod on the rail rather than carrying it to the bait tank — that frees
both hands to work the net instead of fishing one-handed while netting bait
with the other (`6ueGWJek1gI`).

Two schools of thought on getting the bait from tank to hook:

- **Net-and-carry:** leave the net in the water, come up under the bait, and
  carry it to the rod still sitting in the net — don't lift it out and hold it
  by hand to look it over. Holding a bait up out of the water knocks the scales
  off it and starts killing it before it's even hooked (`6ueGWJek1gI`).
- **Hand-pick, minimize hold time:** come up underneath the bait slowly, then
  cup/scoop it out of the water rather than grabbing it, hook it, and let go
  right away. The longer a bait sits in a hand, the more scales rub off onto
  the angler's hand, and that scale loss is what kills it — squeezing strips
  scales the same way, so don't squeeze it either (`BacIrmOK-Bo`).

Both agree scale loss from handling is what kills a bait; they disagree on
whether picking it up by hand is an acceptable way to select one at all.
Net-and-carry avoiding the hand entirely is the safer default; hand-picking is
fine if hold time stays inside the 15-second (10 is better) target below.

A right-handed angler benefits from practicing catching bait with the left
hand, so the hook is already ready in the right; cup the bait gently rather
than squeezing it. Judge a bait's color and vigor while it's up and swimming,
not piled in the net or tank — piled bait all reads the same silver color from
above. Hold-time target: no more than 15 seconds, 10 is better, from the moment
the bait leaves the tank to the moment it's back in the water outside the boat
(`QHY5kmU7OTU`).

Once a bait is hooked, let go of it immediately rather than carrying it in hand
to the rail — scales already on an angler's hands from earlier baits rub off
onto this one and take life out of it. Instead, hold the line itself, about
12–18 in above the bait, and walk it — still swimming on its own "leash" — to
whichever side of the boat you're fishing (`MuC0uGKDzxg`, `Zhn-VDrlaLM`). Get it
back in the water fast once it's hooked: don't hook the bait and then hold it
up dangling on the line while sorting out the reel — a dangled bait over the
rail is exactly what a bird overhead is watching for. Figure out the reel with
the bait still in the water, then flick/cast it out rather than horsing it
around in the air first (`6ueGWJek1gI`).

Favorite hooking spots from the hand-pick method: shoulder (a thin part of the
body just in front of the dorsal fin) and butt (just in front of the anal fin)
— pulling a butt-hooked bait makes it dive down more, while a shoulder-hooked
bait gets pulled back up and stays nearer the surface (`BacIrmOK-Bo`).

## Bait selection

Roughly 90% of live-bait success is bait selection (`SluBXkT3cuw`):

- Fish the hardest bait to catch in the tank — the lively lime-green one with
  scales intact, no red/bloody nose, that's already swimming strong
  (`SluBXkT3cuw`, `raUPkuaFXpw`, `6ueGWJek1gI`). The lime-green-backed bait is
  the one to pick over a silver one, but that call has to be made while the
  bait is still up and swimming — piled bait all reads the same silver color
  from above (`6ueGWJek1gI`).
- Cull the easy-to-catch, bloody, scaly, lethargic baits into the chum bucket
  or over the side — don't fish them (`raUPkuaFXpw`, `SluBXkT3cuw`).
- A good sardine specifically shows a dark back and silver side with scales
  intact. Once a sardine has shed scales and gone weak, red spots appear on its
  sides — that's the bait to throw over the side, not hook (`MuC0uGKDzxg`). A
  slightly lighter-colored sardine, with no missing scales and no red/bloody
  nose, is the same tell restated (`QHY5kmU7OTU`). Rejecting a bait for visible
  redness at the tank is the same tell, applied on camera without further
  reasoning given (`yEsbyq3WjWE`). On a longer trip (5–7 days), fungus on the
  fins is another sign not to fish it (`QHY5kmU7OTU`).
- Open-ocean predators (tuna, wahoo) need a genuinely lively bait to eat at
  all, since they hunt by chasing something down and can barely see a bait
  that isn't moving; an ambush predator (calico bass, sand bass) will still
  take a wounded or dying bait (`QHY5kmU7OTU`).

Between bites, wind in and cast back out rather than let the bait sit idle —
fish moving through need a bait that stays presented, not parked (`D_Y2G0rBZCs`).

**Change cadence.** Several sources give different figures depending on how the
bait is being fished — none reconciled to a single number, since an actively
worked bait and a soaking one behave differently:

| Context | Change interval | Cite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Actively cast/retrieved, not pulling drag shortly after landing | Wind in, flick it off, grab a fresh one | `SluBXkT3cuw`, `8YvmROeVL-0` |
| General cadence, on top of chumming | Every 1 minute | `BdRX4b8Fo5w` |
| Bluefin flyline | Hard cap at 2–3 minutes | `eehDVb6_GoI` |
| Bluefin, soaking sinker-rig or flyline bait | 2–4 minutes typical; up to 6–8 minutes on a long soak, 6–7 minutes practical upper limit | `shZCjX2-fkI` |

"We're not marrying these baits, we're speed dating" (`SluBXkT3cuw`,
`8YvmROeVL-0`). A boat running six anglers plus the captain (seven fishing) at a
one-a-minute cadence goes through bait fast enough to run out on many days —
the reason to run as big a [bait tank](bait-tanks.md) as the boat will carry
(`BdRX4b8Fo5w`).

### Species selection over sardines — trophy bass

For calico or sand bass off a sport boat when hunting a trophy fish rather than
a limit, skip the sardines for a Spanish mackerel or "greeny" (green mackerel)
when either is in the tank — sizing the bait to the fish you're after rather
than for volume. "Brown bait" — an older term for queenfish, a small croaker —
is a second-choice pick when a mackerel/greeny isn't available, thrown into
kelp or the boilers the same way. Step the outfit up to 40 or 50 lb for this
program, not a 25 lb fly-lining outfit: a big fish committing to a bigger bait
in kelp/boilers needs the heavier class to land it (`XyKF34C5iB4`).

## Hook size and style

- **Match the hook to the bait, not to the fish** — an oversized hook stops the
  bait swimming right and it won't get bit. Bait selection sets it, then size
  to the bait in your hand. Full selection logic (bait-by-bait sizes, thin vs.
  heavy wire, circle vs. J) is in [hooks](../tackle/hooks.md).
- A 1/0 or 2/0 circle hook is a common all-around live-bait size; a circle pins
  the corner of the mouth on its own — engage the reel, point the rod where the
  fish is going, and wind tight instead of swinging (`raUPkuaFXpw`).
- Circle-hook the bait correctly: hold the hook high on the arch and press
  straight down into the bait. Holding it from the bottom the way you would a J
  hook leaves no gap and squeezes the bait into a "potato chip" (`8YvmROeVL-0`).

## Fly-lining vs. weighted

- Fly-line (no weight) is the most natural presentation — the bait swims out
  and away on its own. Cast well away from the boat: fish are finickier close
  to the hull, and bites drop off sharply near the boat (`raUPkuaFXpw`).
  Execution detail is in [flyline](../techniques/flyline.md).
- Weighted, when you need to beat current or reach fish holding down: a
  [sliding sinker](../techniques/sliding-sinker.md) to sink a bait along the
  kelp edge, or a [dropper loop](../techniques/dropper-loop.md) to put it on
  the bottom for [yellowtail](../species/yellowtail.md).
- Keep tension the moment a butt/belly bait lands — a little pull makes it swim
  away from you like a propeller; give it slack and it turns and swims right
  back under the boat (`8YvmROeVL-0`).

## Casting the bait out

A live bait doesn't need a full overhead swing to get out — see [underhand
casting](../techniques/underhand-casting.md) for the simpler lob technique,
reached for by newer casters, in a crowded rail corner, or with a lighter bait
(e.g. anchovy on a small rubber-core sinker). For an unweighted, fly-lined bait
specifically — no sinker to help load the cast — see [live-bait pendulum
cast](../techniques/live-bait-pendulum-cast.md), which also covers when hook
position rules out an overhead cast. Once the bait is out and soaking on a
multi-angler stop, where you stand and how you move along the rail matters as
much as the cast — see [wind-in-your-face rail
positioning](../techniques/wind-in-your-face-positioning.md).

## Chumming with live bait

Live bait doubles as chum: throwing bait draws a school up toward the surface
and into casting range. On a charter for [yellowfin](../species/yellowfin-tuna.md)
or [skipjack](../species/skipjack-tuna.md), a couple of minutes of hard
chumming can raise fish holding 40–50 ft down right up under the boat — fish the
down-drift/down-wind side, since the drift carries your bait away from the boat
and keeps the wind at your back for the cast (`lxFNVdDhMy4`). The same draw
shows at a [kelp paddy](../conditions/kelp-paddies.md) holding
[dorado](../species/dorado.md), pulling fish from the bow up to the corner of
the boat where anglers are positioned to fish (`r4J5nP5Bkl4`).

Anchovy is named as the standout chum — "nothing really raises fish like
anchovies" — an advantage "if you make them an advantage" (`8TO-tuYjwWk`). The
same trait that makes anchovy a bycatch liability once fly-lined on the hook
(see [flyline — anchovy
bycatch](../techniques/flyline.md#anchovy-bycatch-when-to-switch-off-flyline))
is what makes it work so well as chum.

On a shared multi-boat stop after the sport boats pull off a spot, private
boats chumming together intermittently hold a school the way a party boat's
steady chum line does, and keeping distance between boats avoids a running fish
sawing off a neighbor's line (`AIHvJj-paoo`).

At the islands, whole dead sardines pitched overboard draw seals in
immediately. Smashing the day's dead sardines in a dedicated chum bucket before
any of it goes in the water still helps raise bait fish up and around the boat,
without rallying seals the way whole dead bait does — a good practice to start
a stop with rather than pitching occasional single dead sardines (`Klfb433I3Uk`).

## Handling live squid

Squid is the staple [white seabass](../species/white-seabass.md) bait; the
usual complaint is getting inked and squirted netting one out of the tank.
Grip the mantle so a finger covers the siphon (the squirter) and the eyes when
lifting it from the net to the hook — covering the siphon stops it from jetting
water or ink, and covering the eyes calms the squid, keeping its tentacles from
finding your hand (the beak sits between the tentacles at the head, not near
the eyes/siphon grip point). Held this way, squid can go straight from net to
hook and back in the water with no ink on the boat, the gear, or the angler
(`Y2bXn44lfqo`, `yuXr3IJ8ybg`).

Pulling squid from the tank: because the beak sits at the head, grab a squid so
its eyes stay closed/covered when lifting it out — that keeps it from being
able to "come up and grab" the angler's hand with its arms, the same
eye-covering mechanism as above (`d93vB_EBu30`).

## Bridling big baits

For a large bait (big mackerel for tuna or marlin), a bridle — a loop of floss
or a rubber band lashed to the hook and passed through the tough spot ahead of
the eyes — hangs the bait off the hook instead of driving the hook through
flesh. The bait swims longer and stronger, and the open hook gap sits clear for
a better hookup. General big-bait practice; not covered in the SoCal deckhand
sources above.
