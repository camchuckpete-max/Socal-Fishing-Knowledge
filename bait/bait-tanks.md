# Bait Tanks

The bait tank is what turns bait you bought or made into bait you can still fish
hours later. A good tank keeps a **clean, circular flow** with enough turnover for
its volume, and doesn't beat up or crowd the bait. Get bait into the tank per
[making bait](making-bait.md); fish what survives per
[fishing live bait](fishing-live-bait.md).

## Tank design — shape and flow

Two design traditions diverge on how water moves through the tank, and the
difference decides whether anchovy and sardine survive (`1QWstxUibDA`):

- **East-coast style** runs a standpipe/drain tube screwed into the middle of
  the tank bottom; water drains out the top once the level rises to it.
  Anchovy and sardine get their noses stuck in the standpipe holes, bleed
  out, and die.
- **West-coast style** runs a round tank with circular flow: incoming water
  injects around the tank so the whole column moves and the bait swims with
  the current (either direction); a side drain sets the water level. Nothing
  for the bait to jam its nose into, so anchovy and sardine last much longer.
  This is the design strongly preferred for SoCal live-bait fishing.

The shape rule is restated with its own reasoning elsewhere in the corpus
(`sIoNELGlxmk`): live bait has to swim in one continuous direction, and a
square-cornered box doesn't allow that — "it's not an aquarium... this is
bait." Build round or oval, never square; run circular flow, never a center
standpipe; keep the whole column turning; and set the drain so the bait isn't
fighting to stay off a screen.

## Placement and sizing

Put the tank **dead center of the deck**, not tucked against a rail, so every
angler has equal access to it (`BdRX4b8Fo5w`). Size it as big as the boat can
carry — on a sportboat "the whole back of the boat is bait tank." Capacity is
what lets everyone keep changing bait as often as the bite demands (see the
bait-changing cadence in [fishing live bait](fishing-live-bait.md)) without
the boat running out mid-day.

The same rule, with the placement mechanism spelled out roughly three and a
half years earlier (`9hEa3sGTh40`): a tank on the swim step or transom stops
flow around the boat — only one angler at a time can reach it while
everyone else's movement stalls too. Dead center of the cockpit is the right
call on any boat from **17 ft to 80 ft**.

## Turnover, pump sizing, and aeration

A bait pump is rated in **GPH (gallons per hour)**. Match the pump's GPH to
the tank's gallons so the water turns over enough times per hour to stay
oxygenated without blasting the bait — too little flow and the bait uses up
the oxygen and dies.

- **Turnover target: the entire tank volume has to change every 8 minutes**,
  stated as holding regardless of tank size — "a thousand gallon tank or...
  a one gallon tank" (`sIoNELGlxmk`). ⚠ Flagged gap — no corpus source:
  mechanism for the specific 8-minute figure ("why eight minutes I have no
  clue but that's what works" — read it as a concrete target to size a
  pump's GPH against, not just "enough flow to keep it alive").
- **Aerate with circulated water, not bubbled air.** Bait-fish gills process
  dissolved oxygen in water, not air bubbles, so bubbles pulled into the
  tank drown the bait rather than oxygenating it. The water already carries
  the oxygen the bait needs; steady circular flow/turnover is what keeps
  that oxygen available (`sIoNELGlxmk`).

## Loading — how much bait

There's no fixed scoop count. Load lightly and watch how the bait swims in
the tank before adding more, rather than loading to a preset number — the
right amount changes every day and from boat to boat, even for the same
captain running a different boat (`7WapaxdtjQg`). Weigh it fresh at the
barge each trip against:

- **Quality of the bait that day** — lively bait tolerates a fuller tank;
  sluggish bait doesn't.
- **Water temperature.**
- **Distance you're about to run** — a longer run to the grounds calls for a
  lighter load than a short hop.
- **Bait type/shape** — smaller-bodied bait (anchovy) lets you take more
  scoops before the tank is loaded than a bulkier bait (big sardines); on
  one trip three scoops of anchovy filled the tank, part of why anchovy is
  preferred over "the big giant sardine" for tank management (`7WapaxdtjQg`).

**Offshore floor, not a target.** There's no bait once you're offshore, so
load for the whole trip, not just the ride out (`9hEa3sGTh40`): take at
least **three scoops** every time you run offshore, whether the run is
40–50 or 100 miles — asking for a half scoop before an offshore run is a
mistake, since bait gets used to throw, to chum, and to fish, with no
topping off once you're out there. Even a big tank runs dry: a 360-gallon
tank typically carrying ~20 scoops still runs out some days, because
offshore tuna fishing burns through bait fast between chumming and fishing
it — load against that, not against a "that should be enough" instinct. A
separate statement puts the personal floor higher, at **five scoops**
(`bM2vUS1B-yQ`), with no run distance given to explain the gap from the
three-scoop figure above: treat five as the safer floor when trip length or
bite intensity is uncertain, three as the bare minimum for a known short
run. A different long-range program corroborates the same light-and-lively
loading judgment at much larger scale — a multi-day trip loaded roughly 700
scoops and still treated day one as the trip's most important load (see
[evidence](evidence/bait-tanks.md)).

**Barge timing** — when to reach the barge to beat the sportboat line, and
what a late arrival costs — is bait-buying logistics, in
[making bait](making-bait.md#buying-at-the-bait-barge-etiquette-hailing-timing-dave-hansen-7wapaxdtjqg-2022-01-04-dana-point-example)
(`ztpj1Xll0-U`).

## Selecting bait at the receiver

A receiver-side routine feeds bait that survives well once it's aboard
(`MjPY-nWZJ54`):

- **Ask the receiver attendant which box to draw from.** The attendant's own
  guidance is to draw from the box holding the oldest, already-cured bait —
  the opposite instinct a newcomer might have.
- **Check the box before committing to it**: the bait should be swimming
  uniformly in a circle.
- **May stomp on top of the receiver** to test the bait's reaction — lively
  bait reacts "a little scared" (`MjPY-nWZJ54`).
- **Look for bait that's clean, with scales intact.**
- **If the box passes**, crowd the bait up with a crowder, then scoop it
  aboard.
- **Load quantities for the Searcher** (San Diego long-range boat): roughly
  **40 scoops per deck box** and **90 scoops in a well below deck** — trip
  length for this figure isn't stated on camera.

A dockside visual tell applies at the moment a scoop first hits the tank,
consistent with the swim-pattern and scale checks above: bait that balls up
and swims in a tight circle, with little loose scale in the water, tends to
last; a scoop that sheds scales/flakes on the way in is unlikely to survive
the full day and starts visibly reddening before it dies (see
[evidence](evidence/bait-tanks.md)).

### Curing

"Cured" bait is the oldest-box pick the receiver routine above recommends,
and there's a mechanism behind the preference (`QHY5kmU7OTU`): a bait boat's
catch goes into the receiver's live bait boxes, each dated and color-coded
by size on intake. A boat leaving same-day gets the freshest catch; a boat
departing on a **3-day-or-longer trip** typically draws from a box that's
already sat **5, 6, or 7 days** — that dwell time is what "curing" means.
On one long-range boat, roughly **80% of the time** the draw is already-cured
stock rather than same-day catch, and cured bait is described as swimming and
working better once fished, the way a spirit improves sitting in the barrel.
Boats with comparable bait capacity also cure their own leftover bait: when a
multi-day trip draws a cured box and doesn't use all of it in one stop, the
leftover is segregated into its own tank rather than mixed with fresher
bait, building a standing reserve of extra-cured bait as the trip runs on.

## Keeping the tank clean and oxygenated

- **Don't overload the tank** — too many baits for the volume crashes the
  oxygen and fouls the water; bait density has to sit within what the flow
  can support.
- **Squid need the same oxygen as finfish bait** — don't overfill on the
  loading pass. Packing squid too densely was described on camera as making
  them "mate quicker and die quicker" (`xFS3MW4GpDU`), a problem on a
  multi-day trip that needs the squid to last; balance the load against how
  many days it has to keep.
- **Cull dead and dying baits promptly** (bloody, scaly, lethargic) to the
  chum bucket, not back in the water column (see bait selection in
  [fishing live bait](fishing-live-bait.md)). Dead bait leaches a toxin that
  kills live bait around it, visible as an oil sheen on the surface when
  dead bait sits on the bottom (`L_FD-UzvEio`) — that's what makes prompt
  culling more than tidiness.
- **Steady flow is the whole job** — an interruption to the pump is what
  kills a tank of bait.

## Underway

- **Run with the lid off**, not sealed shut for the ride. A closed lid hides
  a dead pump: a boat can run 2.5 hours to Catalina with the lid sealed and
  only discover at the destination that the pump quit and the whole tank is
  dead (`L_FD-UzvEio`). A little water on the crew underway is the tradeoff
  for being able to see the tank.
- **Tap-scoop technique to cull without stressing the tank:** lower the
  scoop gently to the bottom, tap the bottom (`tap-tap-tap`) so the live
  bait scatters clear, then scoop the dead bait without pulling the net out
  of the water, picking dead baits out at the surface and releasing the live
  bait back (`L_FD-UzvEio`).
- **Never pull live bait out of the water just to look at it** — it knocks
  scales off and turns healthy bait into damaged bait you'll have to cull.
  The only times bait should leave the water are walking it to the rod to
  hook it, or to chum (`L_FD-UzvEio`). The same rule, repeated roughly three
  and a half years earlier (`6ueGWJek1gI`): carry the bait to the rod in the
  net rather than by hand, and lean the rod on the rail instead of bringing
  it to the tank — see the tank-to-hook workflow in
  [fishing live bait](fishing-live-bait.md).
- **Culled dead bait isn't wasted.** Unless the boat has an unusually large
  bait supply, save it in a bucket (5 gallons covers most boats) and use it
  for chum through the day, including on the anchor.

## Worked examples — tank sizing

- **21 ft Wellcraft (Dana Point):** a 60-gallon bait tank (`m-M0iwX8DjA`) —
  single mention, a small-end data point complementing the sportboat and
  panga figures above and below. The same 21 ft Wellcraft is the
  anchor-weight worked example (12 lb anchor) in
  [search and glassing](../planning/search-and-glassing.md).
- **Cameron's panga** ([Panga Marine Marquesas 22](../profiles/cameron/boat.md)):
  30-gallon tank, dialed — bait normally survives well in it (cameron).
  Pump: Rule 401 Tournament Series, 500 GPH, 12V, 2.1 A draw, 3 A fuse
  (cameron). The panga has no AC/shore-power inlet and no house battery, so
  the pump runs off the single starting battery; a NOCO onboard marine
  charger holds the battery up so the pump can run overnight without
  killing the start battery (cameron). He drains the tank coming in — 30
  gallons is heavy stern water on a small foam-filled hull (trim/ballast
  detail in [boat](../profiles/cameron/boat.md)) (cameron). When Cameron
  makes bait it's mackerel; when the barge has them he buys sardine — see
  [making bait](making-bait.md).
