# Dorado & General Fish Care

[Dorado](../species/dorado.md) get cared for differently from tuna: the
priority is speed to ice, not bleeding, because dorado break down unusually
fast once they're out of the water. The second half of this note carries the
salted-slurry and cold-chain principles that hold across every species.

## Dorado specifics

- **Gaffing:** dorado fall in the **3-in hook, ~6-ft gaff class** (fish up to
  40–50 lb) — see [gaffing](gaffing.md) for sizing, the shot, and safety.
- **No bleed — get them cold instead.** Dorado are not bled the way tuna are;
  the priority is icing, not bleeding (`5to3Q5P7w90`).
- **Ice immediately — dorado break down fast.** Dorado are among the
  fastest-growing fish in the ocean, and that high metabolism means the flesh
  breaks down just as fast once the fish is out of the water: a dorado left
  an hour or two warm in a dry gunny sack turns mushy, with the meat falling
  apart when the fish is cut open. On a sport boat, it's worth asking the
  landing how it handles dorado between catch and cleaning before counting on
  the fish holding up (`5to3Q5P7w90`).
- **A deck hose-down doesn't chill the fish.** Wetting a bagged dorado with
  the boat's deck hose looks like cooling, but that water runs in the **high
  70s °F** — it wets the bag without dropping the fish's temperature
  (`5to3Q5P7w90`).
- **Never freshwater-rinse.** Keep fresh water off the fish; salt water is
  used for every rinse in this chain, on dorado and every other species
  (`5to3Q5P7w90`, `2gHRrR3D8rY`). ⚠ Fact-check (unverifiable): freshwater's
  specific effect on dorado's color (dulling/washing it out) is not stated by
  any corpus source — carried as a legacy claim.
- **Bring a car cooler for the drive home.** If the drive from the landing is
  more than about **10 minutes**, bring an ice chest in the truck or car so
  the fillets have somewhere cold to go the moment they come off the boat,
  instead of warming up on the ride home (`5to3Q5P7w90`).

## General principles (all species)

These apply whether or not the species gets bled:

- **The salted slurry.** A below-freezing slurry chills faster and colder
  than bare ice water: **rock salt (~2 cups per 7–10 lb bag of ice)** plus a
  **splash of seawater** to wet it — the salt is what drops the mix below
  freezing (`5to3Q5P7w90`). The tuna chain uses the same slurry — see
  [tuna care](tuna-care.md).
- **Keep fish cold and out of the sun.** Get the fish into the cold quickly
  and keep it there; a fish baking on a hot deck is losing quality the whole
  time (`5to3Q5P7w90`).
- **Never rinse in fresh water.** Rinse a bled or gutted fish in salt water,
  never the boat's fresh-water hose — fresh water on the meat degrades
  quality (`5to3Q5P7w90`).

## Fillet handling & storage (all species)

Once a fish is filleted, the same cold-chain discipline continues past the
cut (`2gHRrR3D8rY`):

- **Ice-saltwater soak.** Drop fresh fillets straight into a bucket of
  saltwater with ice added, and let them soak before bagging.
- **Rinse in salt water only, never fresh** — same rule as the whole-fish
  handling above; fresh water degrades the meat.
- **Dry before freezing.** Let the fillets drip, then pat them dry before
  bagging — less residual water in the bag means the fillet holds up better
  in the freezer.
- **Water-displacement bagging (no vacuum sealer needed).** Fill a zip-top
  bag most of the way, leave a small opening, and slowly submerge it in a
  bucket of water; the water pressure pushes the air out through the opening,
  which you seal just before the bag goes fully under — a near-vacuum seal
  without a machine.
- **Storage windows:** refrigerated fillets are good for about **3 days**;
  vacuum-sealed and frozen, **1–3 months** before quality starts to drop;
  frozen in a plain ziplock bag (not vacuum-sealed), roughly **1 week to 1
  month** (`2gHRrR3D8rY`).
