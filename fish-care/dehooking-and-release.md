# Dehooking & Release Handling

Getting a hook out of a fish you intend to release, and measuring it, without
doing the damage that makes the release pointless. This note is procedure
only — the *ethic* of when to release (the Cedros voluntary catch-and-release
policy, crew keep-some/release-most practice) lives in
[calico bass](../species/calico-bass.md), and venomous-species handling is
separate in [sculpin handling](sculpin-handling.md).

## Deep-hooked fish — go in through the gill opening

A deep-hooked fish has taken the bait — squid, in the source example — well
down, so the hook sits out of sight and out of reach at the mouth
(`VUb7a3sP8zQ`).

- **Clear the bait first.** Pull the bait out of the fish's mouth so you can
  see where the hook is sitting before reaching for it.
- **Enter through the gill opening, not the mouth.** Put the pliers in
  through the gill opening, look down inside, grab the hook, and pull it
  back out that way.
- **Why:** this avoids "turning the fish inside out" — dragging the hook
  back up through the throat and stomach, which is what damages a fish
  meant for release (`VUb7a3sP8zQ`).

## Landing net mesh — protecting a released fish's fins

A net with big, coarse rope mesh lets a fish's fins pass through the holes;
working the fish in the net then cuts the fins, and the cut leads to
infection and death after release — stated as based on studies done on
California halibut specifically (`PJEG_RIkzF0`). A small-mesh, soft-mesh, or
rubberized net gives a much better survival rate on released fish, and is
especially worth carrying if you routinely release big fish.

Species-specific detail (the halibut fin-damage study) is logged with the
fighting/handling doctrine in
[California halibut](../species/california-halibut.md); this note carries
the general net-choice rule because it applies to any fish landed in a
coarse rope net and released, not only halibut.

## Landing from shore — a grabber with a float

A net isn't practical fished from the beach — surf/bay fishing on foot means
not lugging one around — so the tool instead is a **fish grabber** (a
BOGA-grip-style lip gripper, or a cheap grabber) (`fHMwcm1xRQk`).

- **Rig a small float onto the grabber** — a small lobster-buoy-style "mini
  football" float. If the grabber stays clipped to the fish and the fish
  gets away (breaks the line, or otherwise separates from the angler) while
  still gripped, the float keeps the rig floating and visible, giving the
  angler a chance to recover both the gear and the fish rather than losing
  them.
- **Angler safety, alongside the recovery reason:** grabbing the fish
  instead of hand-holding it avoids wrestling a fish barehanded while it
  thrashes, and avoids having to carry a hooked/grabbed fish all the way
  back to the car before dealing with it — landing is pop the hook, release
  the grabber, fish swims off.

This is the shore-specific counterpart to the boat-side landing-net-mesh fix
above; both solve the same get-the-fish-under-control-without-hurting-it
problem for different platforms.

## Hard-bait treble hooks — angler safety when unhooking

A hard bait rides multiple treble hooks, which makes unhooking a landed fish
its own angler-safety topic, separate from the single-hook procedures above.
A stock Lucky Craft hard bait ships with size-6 hooks, three of them — the
same stock treble figure logged for a Lucky Craft-style hard bait in
[inshore crankbaits — rigging](../techniques/inshore-crankbaits.md#rigging),
there in the context of it being undersized for a big halibut and swapped
out (`c_60Ms3QSMY`).

- **Landing/unhooking is dangerous** with a three-treble hard bait — a
  mishandled hookup "can really ruin your day in a hurry" (`c_60Ms3QSMY`).
- **Gear: a good pair of long hemostats** for working a treble hook out of a
  landed fish (`c_60Ms3QSMY`).
- **The core skill is reading the fish, not a fixed procedure.** An
  experienced angler knows "when the fish is going to freak out, when it's
  not," and handles it accordingly — judgment built with experience rather
  than a step-by-step rule. Until you have that judgment, be extra careful
  unhooking hard baits (`c_60Ms3QSMY`).
- **The mechanism:** roughly 90% of the time a halibut eats a hard bait from
  the side and the bottom, not from behind/the tail as a first-time angler
  expects. That strike geometry pins the front hook in the corner of the
  fish's mouth while the rest of the plug — including the back treble —
  dangles free alongside the boated fish. Working a fish in that state, the
  free back treble is what "will come back and get you in the hand,"
  described as "getting married to the fish" (`eOLoHxoqk6E`).

## Rig the treble out before the trip — a single hook for sport-boat safety and courtesy

A preventive complement to the hard-bait treble-hook danger above: rather
than dealing with a treble hook safely after the fish is landed, pull the
stock rear treble before the trip and fish a single hook instead — on
camera, an Owner Zo-Wire inline single swapped in for the stock rear treble
on a lure that ships with an assist hook at the nose and a treble trailing
at the back (`xgPEt4Zj35Q`).

- **Reasoning: a boat-wide safety/courtesy call, not just the angler's own
  hookup rate.** Which hook rides on a lure fished on a crowded sport boat
  affects everyone at the rail, not only the angler fishing it. Worked
  example: a barracuda taken with "the treble hook right in the barracuda's
  snout," boated and thrashing on deck, while "your assist hooks [are]
  getting in everybody's boots." As described, the treble is the hook
  embedded in the fish and the stock front assist hook is the one left
  riding loose and catching people — the source does not reconcile why
  removing the rear treble specifically addresses a hazard it attributes to
  the front assist hook, so both hooks are carried here exactly as named on
  camera rather than smoothed into one mechanism (`xgPEt4Zj35Q`).
- **Running a single hook instead of the stock treble does not cost you the
  fish** (`xgPEt4Zj35Q`).

This is a rigging-stage precaution, distinct from the post-landing
unhooking-technique advice above, and a separate axis from the
treble-vs-assist effectiveness doctrine in
[iron jigs — hook style: assist vs.
treble](../lures/iron-jigs.md#hook-style--assist-vs-treble), which weighs
swim action and hookup rate, not deck safety.

## Measuring a fish you may release

- **Mouth closed.** Close the fish's mouth before laying it on the measuring
  board — an open mouth adds length that isn't there (`VUb7a3sP8zQ`).
- **Hold, don't pull.** Lay the fish on the board and hold it in place;
  don't stretch it against the stop to make length (`VUb7a3sP8zQ`).

## Regulations

**California / CDFW, as of 2022-04-23 (`VUb7a3sP8zQ`) — verify current CDFW
rules before you keep or release:** calico bass minimum size is 14 in.
Fuller context: [calico bass](../species/calico-bass.md).

The same source also states on camera that a legal-size fish *must* be
killed — "if he's 15 inches … you got to kill him, that's what it says right
in the book." No such must-keep rule appears anywhere else in this corpus,
and it does not match how a minimum-size regulation works — a size limit
sets a floor for what *may* be kept, not an obligation to keep it. Recorded
here as a claim seen and deliberately not carried into doctrine
(`VUb7a3sP8zQ`).
