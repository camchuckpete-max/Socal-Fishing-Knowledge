# Dehooking & Release Handling

Getting a hook out of a fish you intend to release, and measuring it, without
doing the damage that makes the release pointless. This note is procedure
only — the *ethic* of when to release (the Cedros voluntary
catch-and-release policy, crew keep-some/release-most practice) lives in
[calico bass](../species/calico-bass.md), and venomous-species handling is
separate in [sculpin handling](sculpin-handling.md).

Source is a short dedicated tutorial from Capt. Dave Hansen (Your Saltwater
Guide, `VUb7a3sP8zQ`, 2022-04-23), demonstrated on a bait-fed calico bass.
`dave-hansen` is a [registered voice](../sources/source-registry.md), but
this is the first commit of this specific procedure — `medium` pending
repetition.

## Deep-hooked fish — go in through the gill opening

The problem case is a fish that has taken the bait (squid, in the source)
well down, so the hook is not visible or reachable at the mouth.

- **Clear the bait first.** Pull the bait out of the fish's mouth so you can
  actually see where the hook is sitting before you reach for it.
- **Enter through the gill opening, not the mouth.** Put the pliers in
  through the gill opening, look down inside, grab the hook, and pull it
  back out that way.
- **The point of the method:** it avoids "turning the fish inside out" —
  i.e. dragging the hook back up through the throat and stomach, which is
  what damages a fish you mean to release.

## Landing net mesh — protecting a released fish's fins

The net you land a fish in, not just how you handle it afterward, affects
whether a released fish survives (Tackle Express, `PJEG_RIkzF0`, 2026-02-07;
unregistered channel, single mention — medium confidence).

- **The problem: large-hole poly rope nets.** A net with big, coarse rope
  mesh lets a fish's fins pass through the holes; working the fish in the net
  then cuts the fins, and the cut leads to infection and death after release.
  Stated as based on studies done on California halibut specifically.
- **The fix: fine, soft mesh, or a rubberized net.** A small-mesh, soft-mesh,
  or rubberized net gives a much better survival rate on released fish —
  called out as especially worth having aboard if you're routinely releasing
  big fish.
- Species-specific detail (the halibut fin-damage study) is logged with the
  fighting/handling doctrine in [California halibut](../species/california-halibut.md);
  this note carries the general net-choice rule since it isn't halibut-specific
  in application — any fish landed in a coarse rope net and released is
  exposed to the same fin damage.

## Hard-bait treble hooks — angler safety when unhooking (Tackle Express, `c_60Ms3QSMY`, 2026-05-09; unregistered channel, single mention — medium confidence)

A hard bait rides **multiple treble hooks**, which makes unhooking a landed
fish its own angler-safety topic here, separate from the single-hook
procedures above — the source's example is a stock **Lucky Craft** hard
bait, which ships with **size 6 hooks, three of them**. This is the same
size-six stock treble figure already logged for a Lucky Craft-style hard
bait in [inshore crankbaits — rigging](../techniques/inshore-crankbaits.md#rigging)
(there, in the context of it being undersized for a big halibut and swapped
out — not a safety claim in that source, but the same hardware fact).

- **Landing/unhooking called out as dangerous:** the source states this
  right after describing the three-treble hardware, but does not spell out
  the mechanism — it frames a mishandled hookup as something that "can
  really ruin your day in a hurry."
- **Gear: a good pair of hemostats, long ones** — stated as the tool for
  working a treble hook out of a landed fish.
- **The core skill is reading the fish, not a fixed procedure:** an
  experienced angler is described as knowing "when the fish is going to
  freak out, when it's not," and handling it accordingly — the source
  frames this as judgment built with experience rather than a step-by-step
  rule, and its explicit advice to anglers still building that judgment is
  simply to **be extra careful** unhooking hard baits until they have it.
- No specific hand-placement or grip technique is given beyond the
  long-hemostats point above — the clip is 40 seconds and stops there.

## Measuring a fish you may release

- **Mouth closed.** Close the fish's mouth before laying it on the measuring
  board — an open mouth adds length that isn't there.
- **Hold, don't pull.** Lay the fish on the board and hold it in place;
  don't stretch it against the stop to make length.

## Legal size

- **Regulatory (California / CDFW, as reported in the 2022-04-23 source — a
  regulation, not fishery knowledge; verify current CDFW rules before you
  keep or release):** the calico bass minimum was stated on camera as
  **14 inches**. The same figure is logged with fuller context in
  [calico bass](../species/calico-bass.md); this note does not restate the
  citation, it points at it.

**Not carried from this source:** the video also states that a legal-size
fish *must* be killed ("if he's 15 inches … you got to kill him, that's what
it says right in the book"). No such must-keep rule is stated anywhere else
in this corpus, and it is not how a minimum-size regulation works — a size
limit sets a floor for what *may* be kept, not an obligation to keep. Logged
here as a claim seen and deliberately not carried, per the same treatment
given other implausible on-camera claims.
