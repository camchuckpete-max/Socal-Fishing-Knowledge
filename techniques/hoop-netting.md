# Hoop Netting

Hoop netting is the recreational method for [California spiny
lobster](../species/california-spiny-lobster.md): a rigid, baited hoop net is
dropped on rocky or kelp structure after dark, soaked to let the bait's scent
spread, then pulled and reset to cover ground rather than staked out on one
spot (`Qs9oEsh3b_w`). Gear (buoy, rope, bait cage), soak-and-pull timing, and
boat-approach mechanics drive catch rate as much as spot selection.

## Reach for this when

- Targeting spiny lobster on **rock, riprap, boulder fields, reef, or kelp
  edges** at **night**, in season. Drop **on the rock, not off it in open
  sand** (`b19_AJjYCok`). ⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-by-source): `b19_AJjYCok`'s
  transcript covers licensing, gear code, and the carapace-size gauge only —
  it never states a rock-vs-sand placement rule.

## The rig

- A **rigid "pro" hoop net** (holds its shape so it fishes even when bumped).
- A **clipped-in bait cage / closed bait bucket** — sea-lion-proof, so seals
  and sea lions can't strip the bait and kill the set.
- **~40-50 ft of rope** to the buoy (matches the working depth on the
  structure). ⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-by-source): `Qs9oEsh3b_w` states a
  ~30-35 ft working section for Mission Bay depth (matching the [Rope length](#rope-length--match-it-dont-over-carry-it)
  subsection below, same cite), not 40-50 ft; that figure instead appears in
  `b19_AJjYCok`.
- A **torpedo weight** at the buoy end of the rope to force the buoy upright
  and visible.
- An **LED-lit buoy** to relocate each net in the dark; the buoy must carry
  your **name and GO ID** (regs — see [Regulations](#regulations)).
- Full buoy/rope build, bait-cage lashing, and retrieval demo: [California
  Spiny Lobster Hoop Net (FULL SETUP)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qs9oEsh3b_w)
  (`Qs9oEsh3b_w`).

### Buoy build

- **Parts:** buoy light (or glow-stick backup), lobster gauge, bait cage, zip
  ties (a heavy-duty gauge and a smaller gauge — **11 in** ties for lashing
  bait/bait-cage closures, heavier-duty ties for cage-to-net-ring
  attachment), stainless-steel eye screws, electrical tape, permanent marker,
  reflective tape/stickers, marine sealant (silicone or 3M 5200), a torpedo
  sinker, a waterproof light, and a carabiner. Tools: drill with a **1/2 in**
  bit, cutters, and a rope cutter (or a lighter) to melt and seal cut rope
  ends (`Qs9oEsh3b_w`).
- **Weighting the buoy:** drill the torpedo sinker in flush (not protruding),
  seat it, and seal it with silicone/sealant. An **8 oz** torpedo sinker is
  the target weight — 4 oz or 6 oz would likely work, but the extra ballast
  keeps the buoy self-righting (sinker end down, light end up) in heavier
  seas (`Qs9oEsh3b_w`).
- **Light/glow-stick mounting:** two stainless eye screws, hand-threaded in,
  backed out, tipped with sealant, and hand-threaded back in, set in line
  with each other opposite the ballast — one for the primary
  waterproof/rechargeable light (zip-tied through its swiveling body so the
  cap stays fixed while the light spins on), one drilled shallower for a
  glow-stick backup if the primary light fails or is lost. Reflective
  tape/stickers on the buoy body back up both: if the light fails, a handheld
  light shone across the water reflects off the tape so the buoy is still
  findable (`Qs9oEsh3b_w`).
- **Detachable buoy:** rig the rope-to-buoy connection with a fused/taped
  overhand loop plus a **carabiner** rather than tying the buoy on directly.
  From roughly the third net up in a stack, an attached buoy makes the stack
  wobble and unstack; a detachable buoy comes off into a separate box, so bait
  cages nest flat and prep finishes on the dock, not on the water
  (`Qs9oEsh3b_w`).
- **Rope-end finishing:** melt/fuse a cut rope end with tape plus a hot rope
  cutter (or cutters + lighter) before tying into it, so the strands don't
  fray apart under load (`Qs9oEsh3b_w`).

### Rope length — match it, don't over-carry it

Rather than running the full ~100 ft factory rope on every net, split it into
a **working section sized to the home spot's typical depth** (e.g. ~30-35 ft
of rope for **25-35 ft** water at Mission Bay, San Diego) plus a separate
**extension/"rope shot"** section (the remainder, ~60-65 ft) kept spare for
occasional deeper drops (e.g. a crawl reported as deep as **~55 ft** in San
Diego Bay). Join the two with a loop-to-loop connection — [perfection
loop](../rigging/perfection-loop-knot.md) on each end, fed through and drawn
tight — so length can be added or left off in the field without recutting
rope. Carrying the full 100 ft rigged all the time gets in the way when the
home spot doesn't need it; size the main rig to the water actually fished and
keep the rest as a quick-add shot. Excess rope left hanging is also a prop
hazard — see [line management](#line-management-when-the-drop-is-shallower-than-your-rigged-rope)
below (`Qs9oEsh3b_w`).

## Bait

- **Mackerel, anchovies, or cut-up salmon heads** — anything oily draws bugs
  into the net.

### Freshness and bait choice

Fresh bait outfishes old bait: a lobster's long antennae read the electrical
field a bait gives off as it starts to break down, so bait right at the point
of breaking down draws lobsters in fastest (`YQsbwfQ4wzY`, `AH4Eiya1Hn0`).
⚠ Fact-check (single-source): both cites are the same speaker (Capt. Dave
Hansen) repeating the same mechanism across two of his own videos, not two
independent sources; the two videos even disagree on the mechanism's origin
(`YQsbwfQ4wzY` credits a commercial-fisherman friend, `AH4Eiya1Hn0` credits a
marine-biologist colleague instead), and the claim itself is unverified
against outside biology sources.

- **Ranking: fresh (never frozen/defrosted) sardines first**, ahead of
  anchovies and everything else — bought same-trip at the bait barge, cut
  into slices or run through a grinder, and packed into a bait tube (a
  **Promar "SealProof"** bait tube in one build). If sardines aren't
  available, fresh mackerel bought at a fish market is the fallback — never
  frozen bait of any kind (`AH4Eiya1Hn0`).
- **Buy and cut fresh each trip** rather than carrying bait over from a prior
  trip — barge-bought anchovies, sardines, or whatever mackerel is available
  that day (`YQsbwfQ4wzY`).
- **A little bait is enough.** The net lures lobsters in, not feeds them —
  ~90% of commercial hoop-net crews reportedly bait sparingly, roughly a
  small jar's worth rather than a bait cage packed to capacity
  (`YQsbwfQ4wzY`).
- **Skip fish scent, oil, or "pills."** A poured scent dissipates into the
  water column almost immediately, so within moments it's the fresh bait
  itself — still breaking down and giving off its own signal — doing the
  work, not a bottled additive on top of it (`AH4Eiya1Hn0`).
- **Skip salmon heads.** Effective but a hassle to source/handle, and —
  unlike anchovies/sardines — a particular sea-lion draw (`YQsbwfQ4wzY`,
  `AH4Eiya1Hn0`).

### Bait cage choice by sea-lion pressure

- **Stock/"volcano"-style cage** (ships with some nets): opens, fills,
  closes, hooks shut — needs a **separate bait cage**, since the net itself
  has no built-in bait compartment (`Qs9oEsh3b_w`).
- **Promar Ambush:** has extra netting built into the hoop net itself that
  bait packs into directly and zip-ties shut — **no separate bait cage
  required**. Either style is fine in light sea-lion areas (`Qs9oEsh3b_w`).
- **Heavy-duty cage in sea-lion-heavy areas:** where sea lions are actively
  working gear, step up to a heavy-duty cage (e.g. an LP Supply "Lobster
  Port" cage) proven against sea lions mangling lighter nets/cages. Lash it
  into the hoop net with **at least two zip ties per corner** (diagonal),
  using **11 in** ties for the bait-cage closure and heavier-duty ties for
  the cage-to-net-ring lashing — two is the floor, more is angler preference
  (`Qs9oEsh3b_w`). ⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-by-source): the transcript says
  two heavy-duty ties total, placed diagonally corner-to-corner ("put two of
  these big ones on there so I go diagonal"), not two per corner.
- **DIY black PVC bait tube:** a self-built alternative to a mesh bait cage —
  **2-inch-diameter PVC pipe, 18 inches long**, with a screw/unscrew cap for
  loading, small drilled holes (just enough to let scent out, not to expose
  the bait to sight), and holes drilled for a carabiner so it clips inside
  the hoop net and centers dead-middle in it. Loaded with chopped anchovies
  or sardines packed bottom to top. Sea lions are visual hunters — a mesh
  cage or open bait lets them see and target the food, but the tube's black
  color reads as invisible underwater, so sea lions smell the bait without
  being able to locate or attack it (`YQsbwfQ4wzY`).
  ⚠ Fact-check (single-source): running two full sets (20 tubes total) is
  reported to have eliminated sea-lion bait loss, fishing Catalina's shallow
  structure in 20-30 ft off a 65-ft Hatteras — one tutorial's own build and
  result, not corroborated by a second source (`YQsbwfQ4wzY`).

## Setting discipline

- Drop a **line / spread of nets** across the structure, give each a
  **soak**, then **pull and reset** — cover ground to find where the bugs are
  crawling that night rather than staking out one spot, the same
  run-the-structure logic as [search & glassing](../planning/search-and-glassing.md)
  (`EiItVWqFMYc`).
- Chart hard structure and boulder/kelp edges the same way you'd hunt
  bottomfish ([electronics & sounder](../planning/electronics-and-sounder.md)).
- **A depth finder is effectively mandatory** — it tells the crew exactly
  what's on the bottom before/while dropping (`Ow3an9lSVh4`). **Paying out
  rope on the drop:** pull the hand up high as the net sinks — a visual cue
  for driving the boat forward to keep the drop under control — and keep
  light, constant tension on the line the whole way down to feel it: the same
  feel-for-bottom principle as the taut-then-loop procedure below, run live
  during the drop itself (`Ow3an9lSVh4`).
- **Transiting with gear rigged:** before stowing a hoop net for the ride
  out, connect the buoy to the main line first — if the net goes overboard in
  transit (e.g. off a kayak bow) it's still attached to a float and
  recoverable. Uncouple only what's about to be set once at the drop spot
  (`Qs9oEsh3b_w`).

### Line management when the drop is shallower than your rigged rope

When a net is rigged for a given depth (e.g. ~30 ft) but the actual drop is
shallower (e.g. ~10 ft), the leftover rope would otherwise float loose near
the surface — a tangle and prop hazard. Procedure: drop the net and let it
sink with the main line held, take up slack until it goes taut on the bottom,
then tie an **overhand loop** a couple feet past that taut point (easy to
untie in the field — just push the bight through). Coil the remaining slack
into a bundle, run the tag end/loop of that coil through its own middle,
cinch it, and clip the whole bundle to the buoy's carabiner (or leave it
floating loose). Any tension from the pot then loads the overhand loop, not
the coil, so the coil doesn't get pulled apart — no separate zip tie or
weight needed to keep the un-needed rope out of the water column and off the
prop. To move to a new spot, unclip and undo the coil, then the overhand
loop, restoring a straight line and repeating the procedure at the new depth.
**When retrieving, pull from in front of the overhand-loop knot** (the net
side) — pulling from the buoy side first can pull the slack back out before
it's needed (`Qs9oEsh3b_w`).

### Soak-and-pull cadence

- Drop nets **~1 hour before dark** to give the bait's scent time to spread
  (`EiItVWqFMYc`).
- Start the first pull **~30 minutes after sunset**. The bottom goes dark as
  soon as the sun goes under the horizon even though the surface still shows
  gray light; it takes roughly that further half hour for it to actually be
  dark on the bottom, which is when lobsters start crawling — and they crawl
  right away since they've already smelled the bait (`EiItVWqFMYc`).
- **Multi-spot start:** in a good area with two or three fishable zones
  inside the same time window, spread the initial drop across those spots
  (e.g. 5 nets on one, 5 on another) to find which one they're crawling on
  that night, then commit gear to whichever spot produces (`EiItVWqFMYc`).
- **Once they're crawling, don't stop pulling.** Keep cycling the whole
  spread continuously instead of letting any one net soak a full hour —
  stated in direct contrast to "let them soak an hour" advice he says
  circulates online. A net that already has lobsters goes right back down
  **on the exact spot it caught them** — "if there's one there's way more";
  an empty net gets moved to whichever spot is producing. A full 10-hoop
  spread worked continuously on a big sportboat (his example: 65 ft Viking,
  Hatteras, or 44 ft Pacifica) naturally cycles each net back to roughly a
  30-minute soak anyway; on a smaller boat, transit time between 10 nets can
  eat a comparable ~30 minutes, so the same don't-let-it-sit-idle logic still
  applies even though the boat is slower (`EiItVWqFMYc`).
- **Reposition discipline:** always drop a re-set net back on the exact spot
  it last caught fish — moving it even a little risks losing the bite, since
  crawling lobsters are working the scent trail from where the bait first
  went in the water (~1 hr before dark) (`EiItVWqFMYc`).

## Retrieval

- **Keep a constant, steady pull** bringing the net up — don't stop-start. A
  steady upward pull keeps water drag pushing down/in on the lobsters,
  holding them in the net; any pause that lets the net settle back even
  briefly reverses that drag, and loosely caught lobsters can spill or climb
  out during the lull. It doesn't need to be fast, just unbroken
  (`Qs9oEsh3b_w`). A recounted near-miss on a possible U.S.-record lobster —
  lost when a crew member stopped pulling to reach for it by hand instead of
  continuing the lift — matches this rule verbatim: never stop pulling the
  rope, only stop when it's in the boat (see [evidence
  file](evidence/hoop-netting.md#retrieval) — `Q196GVwVqro`).
- Once the net is up: dump/shake it upside down to release the lobsters,
  measure immediately, and get legal lobsters into the bait tank/live well
  and any shorts back over the side right away rather than letting them sit
  loose on deck (`cEscIy278ew`) — see [Doctrine & conflicts](#doctrine--conflicts)
  for how this compares with the later short-lobster judgment call below.

### Boat approach and prop safety

Stated as a fixed habit across every boat size, from a 20-ft Wellcraft skiff
up to 65-ft Viking/Hatteras sport boats, 44-ft Pacifica, and 46-ft Bertram —
the same procedure regardless of hull size or motor count (`cEscIy278ew`).

- **Always approach the buoy stern-first, never bow-first.** Turn the boat
  around and back down onto every hoop-net buoy. Running up bow-first at
  night, the bow's flare and any windshield/cabin glass catch the spotlight
  beam and reflect it straight back at the driver, making it effectively
  impossible to see forward; backing in avoids the reflection entirely
  (`cEscIy278ew`).
- **Spotlight the buoy itself before closing in**, specifically to read which
  way the rope descends off it (the buoy's conical front end points down the
  line). The rope can lay in a different direction than the wind would
  suggest, so its actual lay has to be checked each time rather than assumed
  from the wind (`cEscIy278ew`).
- **Run two spotlights**: one on the charger at all times, one in active use.
  Swap to the charged light as soon as the working one starts to dim, so a
  light never goes fully dead mid-retrieval (`cEscIy278ew`).
- **The highest-risk moment is the instant the buoy disappears under/behind
  the transom** — the line is most likely to reach the propeller then,
  because the buoy is no longer visible to steer off of. Back straight onto
  it through that moment rather than drifting sideways onto the line
  (`cEscIy278ew`).
- **Crew communication and the bump-forward clear:** the crew member on the
  gaff or boat hook calls out the moment the line is hooked. On that call,
  bump the boat into forward gear briefly — not full throttle, just enough
  way to move forward a little — which walks the boat forward off the rope
  and washes it out from under the hull instead of leaving it hanging near
  the wheel. A second crew member holds a corner position and keeps a
  spotlight trained on the rope through that bump to confirm it isn't running
  under the boat (`cEscIy278ew`).

## Doctrine & conflicts

**Sublegal ("short") lobster handling — not reconciled.** An earlier
statement describes the conventional practice: measure immediately after
shaking the net out, put shorts straight into a bucket, and tip them over the
side right away rather than letting them sit loose on deck, framing
possession of a short as carrying a serious fine (`cEscIy278ew`,
2020-11-13). ⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-by-source): the transcript's actual
sequence is catch into a bucket first, then measured from the bucket, then
shorts over the side ("we try to put them right straight into a bucket when
we shake them out of the net and then measure them from the bucket and dump
the shorts over the side immediately") — not measure-then-bucket as stated
here. A later statement instead describes dropping a short back into
the hoop net and redeploying the net on the bottom, reasoning that a short
lobster left in the net can draw more lobsters in to investigate — "nothing
attracts a crowd like a crowd" — possibly including as a mating draw. That
statement frames the sublegal lobster as not "possessed" while it's free to
crawl back out of an open net at any time, calling it "the technicality of
the law" (`EiItVWqFMYc`, 2021-01-05). The decision turns on risk tolerance
against enforcement: the redeploy approach is a judgment call the source
himself frames as his own technicality reading, while the earlier,
release-immediately practice lines up with CDFW's stricter release-immediately
stance on the [species note](../species/california-spiny-lobster.md). Both
positions are kept here, attributed and dated. Verify current CDFW release
requirements (California / CDFW, as of 2026-08) before relying on either in
the field.

## Gear class

[Hoop-net class](../tackle/gear-classes.md) — no rod or reel; the gear is a
rigid hoop net, a bait cage, rope sized to depth, and an LED/torpedo-weighted
buoy, and the boat and hands are the retrieval tool. Class questions are
about the net (rigid "pro" hoop vs. the bait-cage-integrated Promar Ambush)
and cage toughness against sea-lion pressure, not line class or reel ratio —
see [Bait cage choice by sea-lion pressure](#bait-cage-choice-by-sea-lion-pressure).

## Common failures

- Setting **off the rock in open sand** — put nets on the hard structure.
- **Unprotected bait** — sea lions strip it; use the closed cage.
- **Losing nets in the dark** — LED buoy + torpedo weight keep it findable
  and upright.
- **Stop-start retrieval** — lets water drag reverse and can spill lobsters
  out of the net; pull with constant tension instead.
- **Skipping the pre-trip check** — factory zip-tie/lashing points on a new
  net can be loose from the shelf; check (or re-tie) all rim attachment
  points for even, solid tension before the first trip, not after a set is
  lost overboard.
- **Approaching the buoy bow-first at night** — the spotlight reflects off
  the bow flare and any glass, blinding the driver; back the stern down onto
  the buoy instead (`cEscIy278ew`).
- **Losing sight of the line the instant it goes under the transom** — the
  highest-risk moment for a prop wrap; back straight onto it and bump only
  lightly into forward once the line is hooked, rather than gunning it
  (`cEscIy278ew`).

## Regulations

**California / CDFW jurisdiction, as of 2026-08 — verify current gear counts
and season with CDFW before fishing.** Hoop-net gear counts: **5 nets per
angler from a boat, 10 maximum per boat** regardless of crew size, and **2
nets per person** from shore or a public pier — fishing the full 10-net boat
limit requires at least two people aboard (`b19_AJjYCok`, `Qs9oEsh3b_w`).
Season dates, license/lobster-report-card requirements, the 7/day bag limit,
the 3.25 in carapace-size gauge, and buoy-marking rules (name + GO ID) are
full regulatory detail that lives on the [species
note](../species/california-spiny-lobster.md#regulations) — this note
restates only the gear-count figure since it governs how many nets get
rigged.
