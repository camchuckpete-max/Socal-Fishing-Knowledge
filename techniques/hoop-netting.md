# Hoop Netting

The method for [California spiny lobster](../species/california-spiny-lobster.md):
baited rigid hoop nets dropped on rocky structure at night, soaked, then pulled
and reset to cover ground. Primary doctrine source is the BD spiny-lobster tips
video; the buoy/rope build and retrieval mechanics below add Capt. Scotty
(Brothers Sportfishing, La Jolla — registered voice, high confidence, presenter
inferred from the demo handoff) and Roman Castro's own line-management insert
(unregistered channel, caps at medium), Qs9oEsh3b_w, 2020-10-17, San Diego /
Mission Bay; the soak/pull cadence and short-lobster handling below add
dave-hansen (Your Saltwater Guide, registered voice), EiItVWqFMYc, 2021-01-05,
Catalina/SoCal general — California / CDFW jurisdiction, as of 2026-08: verify
gear counts and legality against current CDFW rules (the species note carries
the regulatory detail).

## Reach for this when

- Targeting spiny lobster on **rock, riprap, boulder fields, reef, or kelp
  edges** at **night**, in season. Drop **on the rock, not off it in open sand**.

## The rig

- A **rigid "pro" hoop net** (holds its shape so it fishes even when bumped).
- A **clipped-in bait cage / closed bait bucket** — sea-lion-proof, so seals and
  sea lions can't strip the bait and kill the set.
- **~40–50 ft of rope** to the buoy (matches the working depth on the structure).
- A **torpedo weight** at the buoy end of the rope to force the buoy upright and
  visible.
- An **LED-lit buoy** to relocate each net in the dark; the buoy must carry your
  **name and GO ID** (regs).

### Buoy build (Scotty/Brothers Sportfishing, Qs9oEsh3b_w)

- **Parts:** buoy light (or glow stick backup), lobster gauge, bait cage,
  zip ties (a heavy-duty gauge and a smaller gauge — the source used **11 in**
  zip ties for lashing bait/bait-cage closures and heavier-duty ties for
  cage-to-net-ring attachment), stainless-steel eye screws, electrical tape,
  permanent marker, reflective tape/stickers, marine sealant (silicone or
  3M 5200), a **torpedo sinker**, a waterproof light, and a carabiner. Tools:
  drill with a **1/2 in bit**, cutters, and a rope cutter (or a lighter) to melt
  and seal cut rope ends.
- **Weighting the buoy:** drill the torpedo sinker in **flush** (not
  protruding), seat it, and seal it in place with silicone/sealant. Scotty's
  rig uses an **8 oz torpedo sinker** — he notes 4 oz or 6 oz would likely
  work, but prefers 8 oz for the extra ballast in heavier seas so the buoy
  reliably self-rights (sinker end down, light end up).
- **Light/glow-stick mounting:** two stainless eye screws, hand-threaded in,
  backed out, tipped with sealant, and hand-threaded back in, set in line with
  each other on the buoy (opposite the ballast) — one for the primary
  waterproof/rechargeable light (zip-tied through its swiveling body so the
  cap stays fixed while the light spins on), one drilled shallower for a
  glow-stick backup in case the primary light fails or is lost. Reflective
  tape/stickers on the buoy body back up both: if the light fails, a handheld
  light shone across the water reflects off the tape so the buoy is still
  findable.
- **Detachable buoy:** rig the rope-to-buoy connection with a fused/taped
  overhand loop plus a **carabiner** rather than tying the buoy on directly.
  Judgment: when several hoop nets are stacked on top of each other (roughly
  from the third net up), a buoy left attached makes the stack wobble and
  unstack; a detachable buoy comes off and goes in a separate box, so bait
  cages (which nest flat) can stack cleanly and prep is finished on the dock,
  not on the water.
- **Rope-end finishing:** melt/fuse a cut rope end with tape plus a hot rope
  cutter (or cutters + lighter) before tying into it, so the strands don't
  fray apart under load.

### Rope length: match it, don't over-carry it (Qs9oEsh3b_w)

Rather than running the full ~100 ft factory rope on every net, split it into
a **working section sized to the home spot's typical depth** (Scotty's example:
~30–35 ft of rope for **Mission Bay**, San Diego, which the crew fishes in
roughly **25–35 ft of water**) plus a separate **extension/"rope shot" section**
(the remainder, ~60–65 ft) kept spare for occasional deeper drops (his example:
a hot crawl reported in **San Diego Bay** as deep as **~55 ft**). Join the two
with a loop-to-loop connection (perfection loop on each end, fed through and
drawn tight) so the extra length can be added or left off in the field without
recutting rope. Judgment: carrying the full 100 ft rigged all the time is
"pointless" and "gets in the way" when the home spot doesn't need it — size the
main rig to the water you actually fish, keep the rest as a quick-add shot.
Excess rope left hanging also matters for prop safety — see line management
below.

## Bait

- **Mackerel, anchovies, or cut-up salmon heads** — anything oily to draw bugs
  into the net.

### Bait cage choice by sea-lion pressure (Scotty, Qs9oEsh3b_w)

- **Stock/"volcano"-style cage** (ships with some nets): opens, fills, closes,
  hooks shut — needs a **separate bait cage**, since the net itself has no
  built-in bait compartment.
- **Promar Ambush**: has extra netting built into the hoop net itself that bait
  can be packed into directly and zip-tied shut — **no separate bait cage
  required**. In light sea-lion areas either style is fine.
- **Heavy-duty cage in sea-lion-heavy areas**: where sea lions are actively
  working gear, step up to a heavy-duty cage (source's example: an LP Supply
  "Lobster Port" cage) that has proven itself against sea lions mangling
  lighter nets/cages. Lash it into the hoop net with **at least two zip ties
  per corner** (diagonal), using **11 in** ties for the bait-cage closure and
  heavier-duty ties for the cage-to-net-ring lashing — more ties for extra
  security is angler preference, two minimum is the floor.

## Setting discipline

- Drop a **line / spread of nets** across the structure, give each a **soak**,
  then **pull and reset** — cover ground to find where the bugs are crawling that
  night rather than staking out one spot (same "run the structure, don't stake
  out" logic as [search & glassing](../planning/search-and-glassing.md)).
- Chart hard structure and boulder/kelp edges the same way you'd hunt bottomfish
  ([electronics & sounder](../planning/electronics-and-sounder.md)).
- **Transiting with gear rigged:** before stowing a hoop net for the ride out,
  connect the buoy to the main line first — if the net goes overboard in
  transit (e.g., off a kayak bow) it's still attached to a float and
  recoverable. Once at the drop spot, uncouple only what you're about to set.

### Line management when the drop is shallower than your rigged rope (Roman Castro, Qs9oEsh3b_w)

When a net is rigged for a given depth (e.g., ~30 ft) but the actual drop is
shallower (e.g., ~10 ft), the leftover rope would otherwise float around loose
near the surface — a tangle and prop hazard. Judgment/procedure (medium
confidence, single-source, unregistered channel): drop the net and let it sink
with the main line held, take up slack until you feel it go taut on the
bottom, then tie an **overhand loop** a couple feet past that taut point (easy
to untie in the field — just push the bight through). Coil the remaining slack
line into a bundle, run the tag end/loop of that coil through its own middle,
cinch it, and clip the whole bundle to the buoy's carabiner (or leave it
floating loose if preferred). Any tension from the pot then loads the overhand
loop, not the coil, so the coil doesn't get pulled apart — it keeps the
un-needed rope out of the water column and off the prop without extra
hardware (no separate zip tie or weight needed, unlike coiling and zip-tying
the excess). To move to a new spot, unclip and undo the coil, then the
overhand loop, restoring a straight line and repeating the taut-then-loop
procedure at the new depth. **When retrieving, pull from in front of the
overhand-loop knot** (i.e., from the net side) — pulling from the buoy side
first can pull the slack back out before it's needed.

### Soak-and-pull cadence (dave-hansen, Your Saltwater Guide, EiItVWqFMYc, 2021-01-05)

- Drop nets **~1 hour before dark** to give the bait's scent time to spread.
- Start the first pull **~30 minutes after sunset**. Mechanism: the bottom goes
  dark as soon as the sun goes under the horizon even though the surface still
  shows gray light; it takes roughly that further half hour for it to actually
  be dark on the bottom, which is when lobsters start crawling — and they crawl
  right away since they've already smelled the bait.
- **Multi-spot start:** in a good area with two or three fishable zones inside
  the same time window (his example: **Catalina**), spread the initial drop
  across those spots (e.g., 5 nets on one, 5 on another) to find which one
  they're crawling on that night, then commit gear to whichever spot produces.
- **Once they're crawling, don't stop pulling.** Keep cycling the whole spread
  continuously instead of letting any one net soak a full hour — stated in
  direct contrast to "let them soak an hour" advice he says circulates online.
  A net that already has lobsters goes right back down **on the exact spot it
  caught them** ("if there's one there's way more"); an empty net gets moved
  and dragged over to whichever spot is producing. Working a full 10-hoop
  spread continuously on a big sportboat (his example: 65 ft Viking, Hatteras,
  or 44 ft Pacifica) naturally cycles each net back to roughly a 30-minute soak
  anyway; on a smaller boat, transit time alone between 10 nets can eat a
  comparable ~30 minutes, so the same don't-let-it-sit-idle logic still applies
  even though the boat is slower.
- **Reposition discipline:** always drop a re-set net back on the exact spot it
  last caught fish — moving it even a little risks losing the bite, since
  crawling lobsters are working the scent trail from where the bait first went
  in the water (~1 hr before dark).

### Sublegal ("short") lobster handling (dave-hansen, Your Saltwater Guide, EiItVWqFMYc, 2021-01-05)

Judgment call, presenter's own framing: rather than releasing a short lobster
over the side, he drops it back into the hoop net and redeploys the net on the
bottom, reasoning a short lobster left in the net can draw more lobsters in to
investigate ("nothing attracts a crowd like a crowd"), possibly including as a
mating draw. He frames the sublegal lobster as not "possessed" while it's free
to crawl back out of an open net at any time, calling it "the technicality of
the law." Single-source technique judgment, presented beside CDFW's stricter
framing already on the species note — [short lobster: release it
immediately](../species/california-spiny-lobster.md) — flagged as a possible
tension and not reconciled here; verify current CDFW release requirements
(California / CDFW, as of 2026-08) before relying on this in the field.

## Retrieval (Scotty, Qs9oEsh3b_w)

- **Keep a constant, steady pull** bringing the trap up — don't stop-start.
  Mechanism: a steady upward pull keeps water drag pushing down/in on the
  lobsters, holding them in the net; any pause that lets the net settle back
  even briefly reverses that drag, and lobsters caught loosely can spill or
  climb out during the lull. Doesn't need to be fast (no need to gas yourself
  out) — just unbroken.

## Common failures

- Setting **off the rock in open sand** — put nets on the hard structure.
- **Unprotected bait** — sea lions strip it; use the closed cage.
- **Losing nets in the dark** — LED buoy + torpedo weight keep it findable and
  upright.
- **Stop-start retrieval** — lets water drag reverse and can spill lobsters
  out of the net; pull with constant tension instead.
- **Skipping the pre-trip check** — factory zip-tie/lashing points on a new net
  can be loose from the shelf; check (or re-tie) all rim attachment points for
  even, solid tension before the first trip, not after a set is lost overboard.

lobster report card, and the 3.25" carapace minimum are on the

season. Buoy-marking mechanics (where the GO ID goes and how to mark shared
gear) are also on the species note.
