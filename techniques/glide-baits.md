# Glide Baits

Execution for large, hard-bodied, jointed glide baits (marketed in SoCal as
"Hookup Baits") fished from a private boat, party boat, or bay bank for
calico and sand bass, plus yellowtail and rockfish over hard structure.
Distinct from the soft-plastic weedless slugs and paddle-tails in
[swimbaits](swimbaits.md) — a glide bait is a hard-shelled, articulated
bait that kicks side-to-side **on the pause** rather than a soft-plastic
tail working on the retrieve, and it calls for a different rod action and a
different weedless rig.

**Confidence note:** the entire source is a single seminar given by the
bait line's own designer/creator at the Fred Hall Show, Long Beach, 2018.
Per the sponsored/promotional confidence rule this stays **low** throughout
— including the mechanical claims (rod bend, rig geometry) that would
otherwise read as general technique doctrine — because the whole talk is a
product pitch and none of it is independently corroborated elsewhere in
this corpus (0HwYl0HO2tc).

## Reach for this when

- Working the same kelp/reef edges and bay structure as
  [swimbaits](swimbaits.md), and you want a bait that glides on the pause
  over a rock pile or structure edge rather than tracking straight back.
- Fishing a **drifting** party boat rock-cod trip and want a cast-and-drift
  presentation instead of straight-down bait — see the drift technique
  below.
- Deep structure to roughly 200 ft where you want to A/B two colors, or fish
  two depths/weights at once, off one dropper rig.

## Rod: parabolic bend, not a tip-only bend

- The most repeated point in the seminar: these baits need a rod that
  **bends all the way through the blank ("parabolic")**, not one that
  flexes at the tip and stiffens through the mid-section. Stated mechanism:
  a hooked fish gets **leverage against the extra weight of a 3–4 oz glide
  bait** hanging beside its face, and a stiff mid-section gives that
  leverage something to work against — the hook pulls "almost every time."
  A full-length soft bend absorbs the leverage and keeps the hook planted.
- This scales with bait size: the effect is worst on the **3–4 oz** glide
  baits; smaller (¼ oz) baits are more forgiving of rod choice.
- Line-class-by-bait-size, his own picks (brand names not carried — mostly
  ASR-uncertain product names in a promotional context): a **¼ oz** bait
  pairs with a rod rated roughly **8–17 lb**; **¼–⅝ oz** baits step up to a
  **10–25 lb**-rated rod; **3–4 oz** glide baits go on **40 lb-class or
  heavier** — he states he does not fish the big glide baits under 40 lb
  class after losing fish on 25 lb gear.
- Length: **8 ft** is his standing preference, valued for casting distance
  and for keeping the tip working through a long parabolic bend.

## Line, leader, and reading slack on the water

- **Braid** on every reel except when casting bare iron — roughly **50 lb**
  for general use, stepped down to **20–30 lb** for lighter bay
  presentations. He runs his braid dyed **yellow** on purpose (see below).
- **Fluorocarbon leader, always** — never fish braid straight to the bait.
  Stated reasons: fluoro is less visible and more abrasion-resistant on
  structure than braid or mono. His own back-to-back test — one bait on a
  double rig rigged with **25 lb mono**, the other with **25 lb
  fluorocarbon**, fished simultaneously — drew more bites on the
  fluorocarbon side.
- **Retire fluoro once it "goes furry"** rather than on a fixed interval —
  once the leader scuffs from fish or structure contact it stops passing
  light cleanly and loses the low-visibility advantage; cut back to fresh
  line whenever a fish roughs it up.
- **Read the slack line lying on the water, not just the rod tip.** Spooled
  to the lip on a spinning reel, the retrieve leaves a length of line lying
  flat on the surface between rod tip and bait; how that slack lies tells
  you the bait's state (dragging bottom, swimming clean, getting bit)
  before you feel anything through the rod. He fishes with a hand on the
  trigger/spool, ready to strike, and describes the tell often showing
  first as the slack jumping or snapping tight as a fish sucks the bait in
  from as much as three inches away — before there's a felt bite.
- **Yellow braid is a deliberate choice** to make that on-the-water slack
  read easy to see.

## Weedless rig for grass and heavy kelp (Owner "sled" system)

- The stock hook on these glide baits is not weedless and fouls in grass or
  heavy kelp. Fix: an **Owner-brand weighted "sled" head**, slid into a
  small slit cut in the bait's belly at the point the hook would normally
  exit, so the hook rides flush with the bait's topline instead of hanging
  free — it comes through grass and kelp clean.
- Trade-off, stated directly: the sled/weedless rig **does not glide as
  well** as the stock rigging (less side-to-side action), but is worth it
  whenever grass or kelp fouls the standard rig. Credited for a personal-best
  spotted bay bass on camera.

## Double-bait dropper rig (bass, yellowtail, and rockfish to ~200 ft)

- A **3-way ball-bearing swivel** with a **wide-gap split ring** carries two
  glide baits at once — built originally for rock/structure fishing, also
  fished for yellowtail and bass.
- **Matched-weight pair, sized to the depth wanted** — most often two
  **1.5 oz** baits for the standard rock rig; he notes two **1 oz** baits get
  down easier than two 1.5 oz when 200 ft is hard to reach, so weight is
  picked for depth, not paired as two different weights on one rig; fished
  down to **200 ft** for rockfish.
- **Two different colors on the same drop, deliberately, as an A/B test** —
  on tournament days he and his partner run two colors on the double rig to
  read which the fish prefer that day, then both switch the boat over to
  the winner.
- **Minimum ~3 in offset between the two leader lengths** is the load-bearing
  spec: same-length leaders let the baits swim side by side and crash/tangle
  on the retrieve; staggering the lengths keeps them apart. (Distinct from
  the soft-plastic double rig in
  [swimbaits](swimbaits.md#double-swimbait-rig-nick-california-current),
  which stages a **12 in / 24 in** split for a small lead bait ahead of a
  bigger trailing bait — same anti-tangle principle, a different
  application: glide-bait rockfish/color-testing vs. soft-plastic
  follower-bait.)
- **Sacrificial short dropper below the main leader:** roughly **40 lb**
  fluorocarbon leader above the swivel, then a separate, shorter, lighter
  (**~25 lb**) dropper down to each bait — a bottom snag costs the one
  hung-up bait and its short dropper, not the whole rig.
- For rockfish specifically, paired with **3–4 oz** glide baits on rock
  structure; for yellowtail, hooking two fish at once on the double rig is
  described as "a handful" but a real, repeatable occurrence.

## Party boat rock-fishing technique (cast–drift–bounce)

- Framed as the technique for a **drifting** rock/structure party boat trip
  (as opposed to an anchored trip): fish from the **bow**. Treat it like
  fishing a river — **cast up-current/up-drift** ahead of the boat's drift,
  let the bait **bounce down** as the boat comes over the spot, then reel
  up and re-cast before the boat passes it.
- **Cast away from the boat's drift line, not down it** — casting where the
  drift line will end up puts the presentation under the hull; cast out and
  slightly ahead so the bait clears the boat as it bounces down.
- **Detect bottom without over-fishing the touch:** once the bait is
  bouncing down-current, open the bail (or free-spool) rather than holding
  it tight to bottom, then flip back into gear and take up slack — this
  keeps the bait bouncing along instead of planting and dragging flat,
  which is described as much harder to get bit than an actively bouncing
  bait.
- **Observed** (0HwYl0HO2tc, referencing an earlier trip aboard a boat named
  on camera as "Alvarado," an Anchor Chronicle-sponsored trip out of a
  landing captioned "Turner's" — landing name asr-uncertain, not
  independently confirmed): fishing this cast-drift-bounce technique with
  glide baits against a boat-wide field fishing live squid and dropper
  loops, the speaker's group reported **out-fishing the rest of the boat in
  numbers**, plus what the transcript states as "the sixth biggest fish on
  the boat" (ASR-garbled — exact meaning unclear, not read here as "the
  day's biggest fish"). A single self-reported data point from the
  product's own designer — sponsor/promotional context, not independently
  corroborated elsewhere in this corpus.

## Yellowtail: surface pop-retrieve and a half-yo-yo variant

- **On top:** cast beyond a visible push/boil, rod tip low, retrieve on a
  **crank-pop-crank-pop** cadence. Stated as **easier on a spinning reel
  than a baitcaster** — a spinning reel lets you pop the tip and take up
  slack in the same motion, where baitcast anglers tend to fall into a
  choppier pop-pop-pop-crank pattern.
- **Down, half-yo-yo variant:** when fish aren't showing on top, sink the
  glide bait to bottom, retrieve **about halfway** back up, open the bail
  and let it fall back to bottom a second time, then retrieve **the rest of
  the way** to the boat — roughly **half the speed** of working a bare iron
  yo-yo. Illustrated on a group trip to Cedros Island, Baja, fishing this
  cadence into a yellowtail push moving through. A distinct cadence from
  the crank-then-freespool and continuous-wind-to-the-top cadences already
  documented in [yo-yo iron](yo-yo-iron.md#mechanics) — kept here as a
  glide-bait-specific variant, not merged into that note.

## Scent

A proprietary scent, reapplied roughly **every third cast** on tournament
days (more often on a slow bite), formulated deliberately thin so anglers
actually keep reapplying it. Two named lines: a **shrimp/crab** scent that
also tints the bait's belly reddish and leaves a holographic
flake/scale trail, and a **sardine/anchovy** scent for the same scale-trail
effect. Product-specific — carried at **low confidence** for the underlying
idea (scent plus a visual belly/scale cue, reapplied on a short cycle)
rather than for the named products.

## Common failures

- **Stiff (non-parabolic) rod on a big glide bait** — the failure mode
  called out repeatedly: the fish gets leverage on a stiff mid-section and
  throws the hook.
- **Same-length leaders on the double-bait dropper rig** — the baits swim
  together and tangle; stagger the lengths by several inches.
- **Letting the drift-bounce bait plant dead on the bottom** — stops
  looking like a fleeing/feeding baitfish and stops getting bit; keep it
  bouncing.
