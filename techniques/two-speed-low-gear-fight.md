# Two-Speed Reel Technique — Low-Gear Grind vs. Pump-and-Reel

A reel-side counter-move to a hooked big fish getting its nose down and
circling under the boat — distinct from, and a companion to, the
boat-driving methods (gear-tap, down-swell walk) in
[fighting a big bluefin](fighting-big-bluefin.md). Source: Steve Carson
(Penn), San Diego Rod and Reel Club talk, `9JnIS8HkZlA`, 2023-11-14;
unregistered channel/speaker, single mention — medium confidence.

## Reach for this when

- A **two-speed reel** is loaded with a fish too big to horse in on high
  gear — the source's own examples are 50–70 lb bluefin on a 30 lb rig and
  fish up to ~100 lb on a 40 lb rig (see
  [bluefin rig ladder by grade](../tackle/bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md)).
- The fish has stopped running and is circling somewhere below/around the
  boat, unwilling to commit to the surface.
- **Corroborating, at the light end of the line-class range (Capt. Matt, SD
  Fish and Sips, `G1YIlakiRqg`, uploaded 2024-03-13; unregistered
  channel/speaker — medium confidence):** he did not used to run two-speed
  reels on his 25 lb-class flyline setup, reasoning a fish that size didn't
  need one — but says that's changed: SoCal daytime bluefin trips on 25 lb
  test now regularly hook **40, 50, even 60–80 lb fish**, and a two-speed
  reel is what makes fighting a fish that outgrades the line manageable. He
  now runs two-speed even at this light class as standard practice; full rig
  detail in [bluefin rig ladder by grade — daytime, 25 lb
  setup](../tackle/bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md#daytime-rig-ladder-by-grade-capt-matt-sd-fish-and-sips).

## The problem with pump-and-reel

Standard "pump up, reel down" technique — lift the rod, then wind down while
lowering it — works against you on a big, circling fish fought on a
two-speed reel: every time you reel down, the fish gets its nose pointed
down and kicks its tail, giving up maybe 10 ft of the gained line even on a
fish as big as 65 lb. Repeated over and over, this **protracts the fight**
— potentially to an hour or more — and every extra minute is another chance
for something to go wrong: a shark shows up, the line tangles with another
angler's, or another failure the source only characterizes as "etc., etc."

## The fix — shift to low, then grind continuously

- **Shift the reel into low (second) gear** ("push the little easy button")
  and **wind continuously**, never pausing to let the fish point its nose
  down. A fish worked this way may only come up a few inches per circle, but
  it never gets the chance to kick back down the way it does under
  pump-and-reel.
- **Keep the line tight at all times** — stated as the single highest
  priority of the whole fight, independent of gear selection.
- **Don't lock the drag down** — the fish may still make another run; back
  off and let it run if it does, then resume the continuous low-gear grind
  once it stops.
- **Framed as an older-angler technique that out-performs raw strength:**
  the source's own observation is that older anglers tend to do this better
  than younger, stronger ones, because they aren't trying to overpower the
  fish — the continuous grind wins on technique, not muscle.
- **Net effect:** cutting the fight time down is what actually gets the fish
  landed more often, not just faster — a shorter fight is fewer chances for
  a shark, a tangle, or another mid-fight calamity to cost you the fish.

## After the fight — shift back to high

A commonly observed mistake: anglers land a fish on low gear, celebrate and
pose for a photo, and keep fishing in low gear without noticing. Stated
rule: **small and medium fish get fought in high gear almost all the time
— drop to low only for a genuinely big fish**, and shift back to high as
soon as that fish is landed.

## Common failures

- Falling back into pump-and-reel out of habit on a fish that's already
  circling — the exact situation this technique is meant to replace.
- Leaving the reel in low gear after landing a fish, so the next hookup
  starts under-geared for a smaller, faster fish.
- Locking the drag all the way down to force the fight to end faster —
  works against the "don't lock the drag" guidance and risks a break-off if
  the fish makes another hard run.
