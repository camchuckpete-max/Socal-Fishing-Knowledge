# Two-Speed Reel Technique — Low-Gear Grind vs. Pump-and-Reel

A reel-side counter-move to a hooked big fish getting its nose down and
circling under the boat — distinct from, and a companion to, the
boat-driving methods (gear-tap, down-swell walk) in
[fighting a big bluefin](fighting-big-bluefin.md). Assumes the angler is
already bracing the rod against the boat rail on the
[pump-and-reel baseline](fighting-fish-from-the-rail.md) and changes what
happens at the reel and drag, not the rail-bracing itself (`9JnIS8HkZlA`).

## Reach for this when

- A two-speed reel is loaded with a fish too big to horse in on high gear —
  example grades are 50–70 lb bluefin on a 30 lb rig and fish up to ~100 lb
  on a 40 lb rig (`9JnIS8HkZlA`; see
  [bluefin rig ladder by grade](../tackle/bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md)).
- The fish has stopped running and is circling somewhere below/around the
  boat, unwilling to commit to the surface.
- SoCal daytime bluefin trips on 25 lb test now regularly hook 40, 50, even
  60–80 lb fish; a two-speed reel is what makes fighting a fish that
  outgrades the line manageable, and running two-speed even at this light
  class is now standard practice (`G1YIlakiRqg`; full rig in
  [bluefin rig ladder by grade — daytime, 25 lb
  setup](../tackle/bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md#daytime-rig-ladder-by-grade-capt-matt-sd-fish-and-sips)).
- Offshore tuna hooked on 40 or 50 lb test tend to stay down and circle once
  hooked; the two-speed is what starts "bunching them up" — moving the fish
  from a flat, stalled circle into a tightening, corkscrewing climb that
  actually brings it boatside rather than leaving it parked below
  (`cpUq7Z3UOwU`; rod/reel pairing in [rod & reel
  selection](../tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md#offshore-tuna-favorite-setup-rod-length-for-casting-distance-nathan-winnicke-penn-searchersportfishing)).
- A direct comparison of two speed-jigging fights: an 80 lb-braid two-speed
  reel landed a 180 lb fish in 6 minutes, against a 65 lb-line single-speed
  reel that took 15 minutes to land a smaller, 140 lb fish — more than
  double the fight time on the lighter fish under the single-speed reel
  (`rxoaPT5Zaog`).

## Mechanism — what shifting to low actually changes

A two-speed reel's two gear ratios are printed on its spec sheet as two
numbers, e.g. **6:1 high / 3:1 low** — in high gear, one full turn of the
handle spins the spool six times; shift down and that same turn only spins
the spool three times. Low gear retrieves **less line per crank** but takes
**less effort to turn**; high gear retrieves more line per crank at the cost
of needing more force against a heavy, still-loaded fish — mechanically the
same trade-off as downshifting a bicycle on a hill: slower per pedal stroke,
but each stroke easier. Shifting is a single button near the reel's handle
side: push it mid-fight to drop from high to low once a fish gets heavy
enough that high gear becomes a grind to turn (`-OJ1FED7mxI`).

On a Penn two-speed lever drag, dropping into low gear is a single button
push on the reel's side; getting back to high is a separate motion — rotate
a knurled end piece clockwise until it clicks, then turn the handle. An Avet
two-speed shifts with the same two-motion pattern using a button instead of
a rotating collar: depress a smaller button to drop into low gear (it
engages as soon as the handle turns); push that same button away from you to
get back to high (`ejkr3z7xkJA`, `z5UoW9pntvE`). Full reel-spec comparison,
including bearing counts and a low-gear/high-gear application split, in
[Penn vs. Avet lever drag — feature
comparison](../tackle/tackle-express-penn-vs-avet-lever-drag.md). On a Penn
Fathom 25 Narrow, this low-gear shift button is nicknamed the "easy button,"
a name that recurs across multiple sources in this note (`fyJA3o2hVh0`;
full rig in [Searcher 40 lb all-around tuna
outfit](../tackle/searcher-40lb-all-around-tuna-outfit.md)).

On a 30 lb outfit for 50–70 lb bluefin, fighting in high gear forces a
lift-then-wind-down cycle — and every time the rod comes down, the fish
points its nose down and kicks, undoing part of the gained line, so the
fight "literally doubles" in duration. Shifting to low gear removes the
lift/wind-down cycle entirely: the angler winds continuously, never
lifting, the fish's head stays pointed up as it circles, and it
"corkscrews... right to the surface" instead of repeatedly diving back down
(`QHY5kmU7OTU`).

## Timing the shift — stay high through the run, drop to low once it's close and circling

Hook the fish with the reel in high gear. While a bigger bluefin is still on
the surface making its first long run(s), stay in high gear — a fish fought
like that has a high probability of turning and running straight back at
the boat, and in low gear the angler can't retrieve line fast enough to keep
up, producing slack. Only shift down once the fish comes in closer, starts
working more up-and-down, and begins to circle — low gear is what makes it
manageable to keep winding a fish that's up-and-down and close, under heavy
drag pressure. Shorthand for the whole sequence: "pop in, low; pop out,
high" (`vn4fmPxUqsU`).

The same stay-high-through-the-run / shift-to-low-once-circling sequence
applies on jig-hooked triple-digit bluefin specifically. Stay in high gear
through the fish's initial run(s), including a charge back at the boat,
where the fix for the resulting slack is winding faster in high gear to
catch back up, not downshifting. Only once a fought-out fish settles roughly
**100–200 ft** down and starts spiraling toward the surface — the
"pinwheel" — is that the cue to bump the two-speed into low for more torque
to lift it the rest of the way, since a stalled fish at that stage risks
catching a second wind and taking another run (`DuNkl8F45NE`; full
rigging/reel/rod detail in [trophy bluefin jig
outfit](../tackle/tackle-express-trophy-bluefin-jig-outfit.md)).

Stated generically, not tied to a species or line class: high gear is for
the beginning of the fight, when the fish may be running at the boat and
the angler needs to gain line quickly; low gear is for winching a fish
that's already dogging just under the boat at the end of the fight — shift
down, rest the reel on the rail, on a fighting belt, or on a knee, and work
it up with short rod pumps (`ejkr3z7xkJA`). This "short rod pumps" low-gear
retrieve is a lighter-detail, generic-audience description that differs from
the continuous, never-pause winding described above for a big circling
bluefin — the two describe different fight phases (final boat-side winching
vs. an extended mid-fight circle).

## The problem with pump-and-reel

Standard "pump up, reel down" technique — lift the rod, then wind down
while lowering it — works against you on a big, circling fish fought on a
two-speed reel: every time you reel down, the fish gets its nose pointed
down and kicks its tail, giving up maybe 10 ft of the gained line even on a
fish as big as 65 lb. Repeated over and over, this protracts the fight —
potentially to an hour or more — and every extra minute is another chance
for something to go wrong: a shark shows up, the line tangles with another
angler's, or another failure (`9JnIS8HkZlA`).

Fishing a 50–70 lb bluefin on 30 lb test specifically, the same continuous
low-gear grind does not wind the fish straight in — it keeps it circling
and **gaining roughly 1–2 ft of depth per circle**, versus a one-speed
reel's pump-and-reel giving line back on every down-stroke. The single
highest priority through the fight is keeping the line tight, since 30 lb
test against a fish that size means the angler is "not going to stop him...
he's going to run... at full speed," and any slack lets the hook fall out
(`riEkdu8PEds`; full rig in [Searcher 30 lb large-tuna
outfit](../tackle/searcher-30lb-large-tuna-outfit.md)).

## The fix — shift to low, then grind continuously

- Shift the reel into low (second) gear and wind continuously, never
  pausing to let the fish point its nose down. A fish worked this way may
  only come up a few inches per circle, but it never gets the chance to
  kick back down the way it does under pump-and-reel (`9JnIS8HkZlA`).
- Keep the line tight at all times — the single highest priority of the
  whole fight, independent of gear selection (`9JnIS8HkZlA`, `riEkdu8PEds`).
- Don't lock the drag down — the fish may still make another run; back off
  and let it run if it does, then resume the continuous low-gear grind once
  it stops (`9JnIS8HkZlA`).
- The continuous grind wins on technique, not muscle: older anglers tend to
  fight this way better than younger, stronger ones, because they aren't
  trying to overpower the fish (`9JnIS8HkZlA`).
- Net effect: cutting the fight time down is what actually gets the fish
  landed more often, not just faster — a shorter fight is fewer chances for
  a shark, a tangle, or another mid-fight calamity to cost the fish
  (`9JnIS8HkZlA`).

## After the fight — shift back to high

A commonly observed mistake: anglers land a fish on low gear, celebrate and
pose for a photo, and keep fishing in low gear without noticing. Small and
medium fish get fought in high gear almost all the time — drop to low only
for a genuinely big fish — and shift back to high as soon as that fish is
landed (`9JnIS8HkZlA`).

## Structure fishing — pulling a fish out of rocks quickly

A different trigger for the same shift-to-low mechanism: avoiding a
structure break-off rather than managing a big circling pelagic. Drop a jig
to bottom, hook a bigger fish, shift into low gear, pull it out of the rocks
quickly, then shift back into high — ready to cast again. Example outfit: an
Okuma Andros 5 (narrow) lever-drag two-speed on a 20–50 lb Phenix rod, 9 ft,
spooled with 65 lb line and a 60 lb fluorocarbon leader (~5 ft), casting
Colt Sniper irons and Z-Man plastics and also dropping yo-yo jigs to bottom
(`1nBqYdvVrXY`; same casting rig in [star drag vs. lever
drag](../tackle/star-drag-vs-lever-drag.md)). This is a tighter-timeframe,
structure-avoidance version of the shift-to-low mechanism above — the goal
is getting a fish clear of rock structure before it breaks off, not
out-lasting a long circling fight — on lighter tackle than the bluefin
examples elsewhere in this note.

## Gear class

The dominant application is the [rail-rod / heavy-tuna
class](../tackle/gear-classes.md) — an 8 ft-class, 3X-H rod paired with a
two-speed reel for bluefin; full rod/line-class detail in [fighting a big
bluefin](fighting-big-bluefin.md#gear-class). The technique itself is
reel-mechanism-specific, not tied to one rod class: it also runs on lighter
gear, including a 20–50 lb structure-fishing casting rod (`1nBqYdvVrXY`) and
a 25 lb-class flyline outfit (`G1YIlakiRqg`). The defining spec is the
reel's high/low gear-ratio pair (e.g. 6:1/3:1); a San Diego-specific retune
(raised high gear for wahoo, lowered low gear for grinding big bluefin) is
in [Penn Fathom II and Penn Authority — ICAST 2022 launch
parameters](../tackle/tackle-express-icast2022-penn-fathom2-authority.md).

## Common failures

- Falling back into pump-and-reel out of habit on a fish that's already
  circling — the exact situation this technique is meant to replace.
- Leaving the reel in low gear after landing a fish, so the next hookup
  starts under-geared for a smaller, faster fish.
- Locking the drag all the way down to force the fight to end faster —
  works against the "don't lock the drag" guidance and risks a break-off if
  the fish makes another hard run (`9JnIS8HkZlA`).
