# Knife / Speed Jigging

**Rip a slender jig up through the water column fast**, imitating a fleeing
baitfish — a reaction bite, distinct from the fall-triggered
[slow-pitch](slow-pitch-jigging.md) presentation. The SoCal application that owns
this note is **night bluefin** on deep marks. Execution only; the reverse map is
in **Linked from**.

## Reach for this when

- Night [bluefin](../species/bluefin-tuna.md) marked deep on the meter,
  especially sitting in or below the
  [deep scattering layer](../conditions/deep-scattering-layer.md) in current —
  the application this note is built around.
- Deep [yellowtail](../species/yellowtail.md) and other gamefish holding in a
  strong drift, where a fast vertical rip out-fishes a fluttering
  [slow-pitch](slow-pitch-jigging.md) jig.
- More active, aggressive fish generally — tuna and yellowtail specifically —
  favor a faster-moving jig that covers more water and draws more attention
  than a fall-triggered presentation; applies offshore or nearshore alike
  (`6SClBs16L2Y`).
- Heavy current over deep structure in Baja/Sea of Cortez, for yellowtail and
  for snowy grouper on deep structure (`M8hOYQ_6rSg`).
- A slow "pick bite" on bluefin: when a boat working a
  [sinker rig](../rigging/rubber-band-deep-rig.md) is getting fish
  sporadically, one at a time, a couple of anglers working a speed jig
  through the area adds commotion that draws fish in closer to the boat — a
  tactic layered on the existing rig, not a replacement (`6SClBs16L2Y`; full
  situation row in
  [bluefin tuna](../species/bluefin-tuna.md#situations--techniques)).
- Depth: captain-called fish from **150–240 ft** is the confident daytime
  SoCal bluefin zone, still workable to **300 ft**; past roughly **500–600
  ft** it stops paying off — too much drop-and-retrieve for the return on a
  heavy jig, and that depth is
  [deep-drop swordfish](deep-drop-swordfishing.md) territory instead
  (`gimFbgN5Jjk`). A documented outlier: a bite at roughly 90 fathoms
  (~540 ft) in the Sea of Cortez — worth noting, not worth planning around
  (`gimFbgN5Jjk`).
- Gear scales by rail crowding, not just the fish. Sportboats (~70% of SoCal
  trips) put a social floor under tackle weight — fishing lighter than the
  rail around you tangles on a crossed fish, so gear effectively matches the
  boat, not the ideal presentation. Private/4- to 6-pack boats remove that
  constraint; scale down there, especially for the more gear-shy daytime bite
  (`Et2TUskzCSs`).
- Bite timing flips day vs. night, which is why tackle should too. At night
  roughly 70% of bluefin bites come on the fall — a fall-triggered bite even
  on a mechanically-worked speed jig ("when that jig stops, it's game on").
  By day that ratio flips to mostly bites on the rise, the reaction-bite
  mechanism this technique is built around. Night tackle can run
  heavier/thicker because fish are less leader-shy in the dark; day tackle
  scales down — thinner line, smaller jigs, more refined presentation
  (`Et2TUskzCSs`).

## Mechanics

- **Weight rule: ~1 g per foot of depth** for a speed jig — lighter than
  [slow-pitch](slow-pitch-jigging.md)'s ~1.5 g/ft — enough to punch down and
  stay vertical.
- **Stay directly up and down.** Yo-yo logic with a lighter, darting jig:
  drop straight to the called depth, then rip it up in fast, continuous
  sweeps.
- **Boat position decides whether the jig ever goes vertical, and the fix
  depends on which rail you're fishing.** From the bow, throw up-current, let
  the jig sink straight down, and walk back toward the stern as it settles —
  dropping with the crowd off the stern instead lets the line scope out, so a
  300 ft mark can leave the jig only ~80 ft down (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`). From the
  stern, cast down-swell (or downwind) so the boat drifts onto the jig by the
  time it reaches the bite zone; fish from the very front or very back of the
  boat, never midship — midship blocks the path around the bow or stern once
  a fish is on (`UfuiWFVvz2E`, `G1YIlakiRqg`). Cast distance follows
  conditions, not a fixed number: a fast wind-driven drift needs a long
  downwind cast to give the jig time to sink; a flat-calm, slow drift needs
  as little as 20–30 ft, since a long cast on a slow day scopes the jig out
  past vertical (`G1YIlakiRqg`). Ask the deckhand which way the boat is
  expected to drift and where it's been biting.
- **More jigs working the water reads to the fish as a moving bait school.**
  Five idle rods on the rail don't produce the same draw as five worked
  jigs — participation is framed as mattering most at night, though it helps
  by day too (`UfuiWFVvz2E`).
- The short parabolic jigging rod lets the jig **dart** and gives leverage to
  fight a big fish with less angler fatigue than a rail rod.
- **The coordinated stroke:** on the down-stroke, drop the jig; on the
  wind-up, lift the rod tip **while winding a full turn of the handle at the
  same time** — a timing/coordination skill and upper-body endurance, not
  just a fast wind (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`). Worked plainly as **pump up, wind down**,
  paced faster than slow-pitch but not frantic (`M8hOYQ_6rSg`). If fish are
  bumping the jig without a solid hookup at the working pace, **speed up
  rather than slow down** — yellowtail specifically respond better to more
  speed (`M8hOYQ_6rSg`).
- **Species and daytime warmth both set the pace.** Pelagics that never stop
  swimming — tuna, dorado, wahoo — generally want the jig moved faster,
  matching the reaction-bite framing above. Daytime warmth can override that
  by species state: as water temperature rises through the day, fish
  reported going comparatively dormant and sitting lower in the column can
  call for slowing down instead — "as slow as you want, don't stop, but as
  slow as you want" (`Et2TUskzCSs`). Default to speed; if the working pace
  isn't drawing bites in warm daytime water, try slowing down before
  assuming the fish aren't there. A full-day San Diego trip corroborates the
  same read from the other edge: the crew worked out mid-trip that the
  present fish wanted a fast retrieve with a darting action in the strike
  zone, landing 9–11 bluefin once they matched it — "finding the cadence"
  through the day and adjusting light-to-heavy jigs as needed beats fishing
  one jig/pace all day (`gimFbgN5Jjk`).
- **Slack it, don't tightline it, on the drop.** Whip the rod tip up
  quickly, then slack the line down quickly — a tight line on the drop puts
  resistance on the jig's nose and kills the action (it can't camber, rock,
  flutter, or dart); slack lets the jig do what it's built to do. This is
  specific to this note's assist-hook rig; a treble-hooked iron takes the
  opposite advice for a different reason (tight line keeps the trebles from
  fouling, at the cost of some flutter) — see
  [iron jigs — hook style](../lures/iron-jigs.md#hook-style--assist-vs-treble)
  (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`).
- **At night, work slower.** Most anglers aren't pumping and winding fast
  after dark; a slow, worked drop ("blinking") out-produces speed at night.
  Pair the slower night pace with a center-weighted **flip-slider** jig
  rather than a tail-weighted speed jig for more fall action worked slowly —
  see [knife jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md) (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`).
- **Reaction time on the fall decides whether a bite converts.** Roughly 80%
  of night bluefin bites happen while the lure is sinking (`G1YIlakiRqg`),
  consistent with the 70%-on-the-fall figure above. Hold the rod butt tucked
  under the left armpit, right-hand fingers resting lightly on the spool
  during free-spool — just enough tension to prevent a backlash without
  slowing the drop — and the right thumb resting on the reel's
  gear-engagement lever at all times, ready to engage the instant a bite is
  felt. Held any other way, the gap from feeling a bite to the reel in gear
  and the handle turning can run 5–6 seconds, long enough for the fish to
  spit the jig and be 30 ft away; held this way the gap runs roughly half a
  second (`G1YIlakiRqg`).
- **Missed a hookup? Free-spool and redrop immediately — don't wind up
  first.** A common mistake: engage the reel, wind several turns, realize
  the fish isn't there, then keep winding the lure all the way to the
  surface — taking it out of the water column the fish are actually in. The
  fix: the moment you realize you missed the fish, put the reel straight
  back into free spool and get the lure back to the same depth immediately.
  About 8 out of 10 times you'll get bit again. This matters most on
  smaller, aggressive fish (working grade 15–60 lb), which can hit a lure
  five to eight times before actually getting hooked — winding all the way
  up after a miss can cost several of those follow-up chances in a row
  (`G1YIlakiRqg`).

## Depth control & jig calling

- **Mark your line so you can put the jig on the captain's called depth**
  ("fish at 180," "fish at 230") — a Sharpie mark, a wrap of contrasting
  braid, or color-metered braid that changes on an interval. Documented
  intervals span **25, 30, 50, 60, and 100 ft** depending on the angler and
  the product; pick one and know it cold (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`, `q_ciF1xiDiI`,
  `fgTmUq78ofQ`, `eehDVb6_GoI`). Make each mark **12–18 in long, not a
  dot** — line runs off the reel fast enough on a drop that a short mark
  gets missed entirely (`fgTmUq78ofQ`). Named 100 ft-metered products and
  full rig ladders built around them are in
  [bluefin rig ladder by grade](../tackle/bluefin-rig-ladder-by-grade.md#daytime-rig-ladder-by-grade-capt-matt-sd-fish-and-sips),
  [Tackle Express — night bluefin TG-jig rig](../tackle/tackle-express-night-bluefin-tg-jig-rig.md),
  and
  [Maxcuatro metered braid — Shogun trip](../tackle/tackle-express-shogun-maxcuatro-braid.md).
- **Multi-color marking beats a single 100 ft interval specifically for
  tuna, because there's no bottom to read off.** With no depth reference to
  fish against, note the meter-mark color you were on when the jig hesitates
  or stops on the drop, work the jig through that zone, and drop back to the
  same color to return to it. 100 ft marks are fine when there's a depth
  reference (bottom, or a captain's call) and light current (`t0kkwlrNwHk`).
- **Failing to mark line at all is the single most common beginner
  mistake** — a fish holding at 350 ft will not eat a jig worked at 250 or
  550 ft (`UP_3fBu7VPA`). Without marked line, three fallback methods are
  documented: pull line out in arm spans (roughly 3 ft per pull for an
  average reach) and count spans — slower (10–15 minutes) but doable on an
  overnight trip (`UP_3fBu7VPA`); measure a run at home (a driveway, 60 or
  100 ft) and Sharpie the line at that interval before the trip
  (`fgTmUq78ofQ`, `eehDVb6_GoI`); or, with no marked line at all, count a
  slow ("Mississippi") count — roughly 1 second per 10 ft of fall, so fish
  called at 170 ft is a count to "17" (`gimFbgN5Jjk`).
- **Thumb tension on the drop keeps the jig vertical** — counterintuitive
  but effective for either speed or slow-pitch jigging. Light drag on the
  spool as the jig falls keeps it vertical instead of scoping out, shooting
  it down to the strike zone faster and straighter than a free fall. The
  first drop of a drift also calibrates the rest of it: without a stated
  drift speed, if a jig that should sink straight starts to scope out
  instead, that's the signal to go narrower/longer (cuts water, sinks
  faster) or heavier (`Et2TUskzCSs`).
- **Work the called depth, not past it.** With a captain's call of, e.g.,
  "marking 200 ft," go down to roughly 220 ft and work the jig through that
  column rather than 100 ft above or below it — efficiency, not coverage,
  is the goal once fish are pinpointed (`Et2TUskzCSs`).
- **"When you hit bottom, you're bit."** Knife jigs for bluefin are
  typically fished over water too deep for the jig to ever reach bottom, so
  when free-spooling line suddenly stops paying out the way it would on
  bottom, that's read as a bite, not bottom. Keep light thumb pressure
  through the fall regardless — thumbing off completely means a sudden stop
  on a bite can backlash the reel. Most bites come on the sink, but bites on
  the retrieve happen too (`6_E5JCRvazc`).
- **Track the called depth continuously, not once.** The captain's call
  changes fast — "fish at 200 ft," then "fish at 300 ft" two minutes
  later — so update where the jig actually is off the line marks in real
  time rather than dropping once and assuming (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`).
- **Working a depth call in 100 ft increments (a rockfish-style pattern):**
  drop to the called depth, put the reel in gear, pop the rod tip up high,
  then drop it and let the slack-line jig flutter all the way down — repeat
  2–3 times without a bite. Then wind 10 full turns (≈30–40 ft) and repeat
  the flutter-drop sequence for another 3–4 sweeps. Ten turns at a time
  works roughly 100 ft of column while keeping the jig fluttering in the
  strike zone the whole way — more time in the zone than a straight yo-yo
  wind (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`).
- **Overshoot the called depth, then work up.** Rather than starting the
  retrieve right at a called depth, drop past it and work up through the
  zone — a captain's call of 240 ft becomes a drop to about 300 ft, then a
  retrieve back up through 180–240 ft. Documented independently, with the
  same 240→300 ft figures, roughly three years apart (`gimFbgN5Jjk`,
  `AyN9MBWg-XY`).
- **Jig weight is called by the captain, 150–400 g,** by how deep the fish
  are and how hard the current runs: more current or deeper fish → heavier
  (200–300 g+) to hold the zone and stay horizontal in current; a lighter
  (~220 g) fluttery jig works the upper column when fish sit 150–200 ft
  down. Data points outside that baseline range, all consistent with
  matching sink rate to depth/current rather than contradicting it: a night
  floor of 300 g, with 250 g allowed only for anglers experienced enough to
  fish it well; up to 500 g on a windy, hard-drifting night when standard
  250–350 g jigs wouldn't hold the zone; and a seasonal progression on one
  boat/channel — 80–100 g companion jigs early season before fish key on
  microbait, sized down to 35–60 g through the June–August microbait window
  (see [bluefin tuna](../species/bluefin-tuna.md#where--when)), then back up
  to 400–500 g in windy fall conditions (`UfuiWFVvz2E`, `LsFMBCa9DOQ`,
  `qLKLlZdiflA`).
- **The "Eddie bomb":** the bluntest tool in the same lineup — a
  wire-through 16 oz sinker with a treble hook on the bottom and a bite
  leader. Multiple 200 lb night bluefin have come on a straight sinker; it's
  the heaviest, cheapest, fastest-sinking option for deep marks in hard
  current (`nRFFM8DT-og`).

## Hookset

- Traditional bait-fishing habit — "don't set the hook, let the fish load up
  and wind on him" — is the worst thing you can do on a jig. A jig-hooked
  fish typically gets stuck just above the barb on a hard point of the jaw;
  without driving the point past the barb, the hook can bend open under load
  no matter how heavy the wire.
- On the bite: grab the spool (claw or palm it) and give three or four hard
  sideways swings to drive the hook home. Don't strike on the first
  thump — the fish may not have the jig fully in its mouth yet. Wait until
  drag is actually running off the reel (meaning the hooks are already
  partially buried) before swinging hard (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`).
- **Set strike drag at 19–20 lb.** Online advice to run 35 lb strike drag is
  dangerous — a hookup at that setting can rip a jigging outfit out of an
  angler's hands entirely; one trip lost five jigging outfits to it. The
  lighter 19–20 lb setting is the deliberately conservative, documented
  number (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`).

## Fighting

Fight technique for jig-hooked bluefin specifically — distinct from the
boat-driven landing method for trolled/kited/flylined bluefin in
[fighting a big bluefin](fighting-big-bluefin.md) (`Et2TUskzCSs`).

- **Leader material is a genuine, unreconciled choice between two fighting
  styles.** A mono top shot (20–25 ft) absorbs the sudden shock of a jig
  bite 200–500 ft down — bites come too fast to watch the fish eat it, so
  stretch reduces hook-pulls and raises the hook-to-land ratio; that stretch
  also helps in the end game (below). The alternative runs fluoro instead,
  trading away shock absorption for abrasion resistance — relevant because
  some tuna swallow the jig down the throat, especially at night, putting
  the leader against teeth — and compensates for the lack of stretch with a
  progressive drag increase: start light (7–10 lb) and ratchet up as the
  fight goes on, on the reasoning that a fish that's already run hard and
  tired itself in the first ~10 minutes is easier to finish without
  over-stressing gear early. Pick mono when the bite is blind and sudden
  (the common case, night especially); pick fluoro when swallowed baits and
  abrasion are the bigger risk, and manage the fight with drag instead of
  stretch (`Et2TUskzCSs`).
- **Point the rod tip at the fish's face for roughly 90% of the fight**,
  rather than lifting and pumping — the same point-and-winch doctrine as
  [slow-pitch jigging](slow-pitch-jigging.md#mechanics--the-pitch-and-the-fall)
  and [fighting a big bluefin](fighting-big-bluefin.md), worked from lighter
  jig tackle. A drag set to read, say, 25 lb on a scale at home is not what
  the fish feels at the hook — bending the rod and stretching the line each
  consume a chunk of that pressure before it reaches the fish, "well over"
  5 lb off a 25 lb setting. Pointing the rod tip directly at the fish's nose
  instead of lifting into a bend removes most of that loss and keeps the
  fish from turning broadside, where its body gains extra water
  resistance — framed as leverage, not finesse or brute force. It's also
  the stated reason to avoid shifting a two-speed reel to low gear here: the
  torque gain can tempt an angler into fighting the fish's circling instead
  of staying pinned nose-on and taking line straight in (`Et2TUskzCSs`,
  `gimFbgN5Jjk`).
- **In the end game, cut off the fish's circles.** Once the fish is
  circling under the boat, pull the rod the opposite direction from its swim
  to keep the circle tight rather than letting the fish fully extend its
  body — a fish that extends fully gets "a deep breath" and prolongs the
  fight, the same mechanism as "avoid the death circle" in
  [fighting a big bluefin](fighting-big-bluefin.md#walk-it-down-swell-avoid-the-death-circle).
  Mono's stretch helps specifically through this stage, absorbing the
  fish's short, hard head-shakes as it's cut off in tight circles rather
  than loading every shake straight onto the hook. Anglers fighting a jig
  stuck on bottom rockfish already know the same mechanism by feel —
  bouncing or pulling against a stuck rod does little, but pointing the rod
  tip straight down at the water and pulling applies full drag pressure
  without the rod absorbing it (`Et2TUskzCSs`, `gimFbgN5Jjk`).

## Gear class

[Knife/speed-jig class](../tackle/gear-classes.md) tackle throughout — short,
high-power blanks built to rip a 150–400 g jig, paired with thin, low-stretch
braid and assist hooks, because a stretchy line or a slow, weak rod can't
transmit the rip that makes this presentation work.

- **Rod:** short, parabolic, high-power, often with spiral/acid-wrapped
  guides to kill rod twist and stop the line wrapping the tip during the
  jigging motion. A budget broomstick (130–150 class) covers schoolie grade;
  a stiffer composite (fiberglass-tipped) blank for cow-grade fish. Power is
  deceptive for the rod's size — a demoed speed-jig rod rated **55 kg
  (≈110 lb) of pulling power at a 60° rod angle** despite a slow action,
  because the blank loads and bends all the way through to the butt; fishing
  it flat on the rail throws away roughly half the rod's power, so fish it
  in hand, not racked (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`). A slower-action, small-diameter
  blank — nicknamed a "trout pole" for its light-tackle look — carries far
  more fighting power than it appears to, provided the angler keeps the rod
  at 60° or lower and doesn't bounce or high-stick the fish; an ultralight
  ~30 lb-braid outfit landed a **32 lb yellowtail on a 60 g jig** on an
  offshore tuna trip on that logic. The mechanism: the rod's slower action
  works as a shock absorber that keeps the hook pinned without
  high-sticking, while the actual fighting load is carried by the reel's
  drag, not the rod (`GeydGK-62fw`, `ZrcwcugaEe4`, `bB8zs0lXvvo`).
  Tackle-shop shorthand recommends a **1x, 2x, or even 3x-rated rail rod**
  for this style — a heavier "X" rating generally helps, without a stated
  mechanism beyond that (`6_E5JCRvazc`).
- **Reel:** any narrow two-speed — "narrow" meaning **under 2 in of spool
  width**; wider spools let line dig into itself under the 15–30 lb of drag
  jigging commonly runs, and once it digs in it can't pay out cleanly
  (`gimFbgN5Jjk`). Rod guide construction (spiral/acid-wrap vs. traditional)
  is a related choice for the same stay-vertical/stay-in-control goal — see
  [spiral-wrap guides and narrow reels for jigging rods](../tackle/jigging-rod-guide-wrap.md).
  A premium two-speed (Talica, Daiwa, or comparable) if
  budget allows; a Fathom is called out as strong value — big,
  high-capacity, two-speed — with the caveat that its stock handle is thin
  enough to snap, so budget for an aftermarket handle (thicker and a bit
  longer, which also speeds up the low-gear retrieve). Two-speeds draw some
  pushback from speed-jig purists but are the broader trend, including in
  Japan (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`). Named benchmarks spanning the class: a **Shimano
  Talica 20** on the light end, valued for a free-spool that gets a jig
  down fast, and a **Penn International 50** spooled with **130 lb line**
  on the heavy end (`6_E5JCRvazc`).
- **Line:** 80–100 lb braid — 80 lb sinks faster and matters more on smaller
  jigs — straight to an FG connection, then a mono or fluoro top shot. Split
  by rod and daypart: a light/day outfit (200 g and under jigs) runs
  **65 lb braid to a 25 ft, 80 lb top shot**; a heavy/night outfit runs
  **100 lb braid to a 130 lb top shot**, specifically mono rather than
  fluoro for the stretch. Thinner braid means less line angle in the water
  and a jig that hangs straighter under the boat; 65–100 lb covers the
  class, and much heavier braid is only worth it on the biggest jigs
  (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`). A rod-and-reel outfit can't generate enough drag to break
  80 lb braid by pulling alone, and thinner braid usually cuts through a
  tangle with thicker braid rather than losing that fight — so tangles
  aren't a reason to size up: **80 lb braid** (with a ~15 ft, 130 lb mono
  top shot) for **350 g+ jigs**, **65 lb braid** (with a 100 lb mono top
  shot) for **200 g and lighter** jigs. Two dated fight-time examples
  support the lighter end: a 180 lb bluefin landed on 80 lb braid and a
  two-speed reel in 6 minutes, and a 140 lb bluefin landed on 65 lb braid
  and a single-speed reel in 15 minutes — both faster than anglers on the
  same trip fishing heavier 100–130 lb braid on 450–500 g jigs at the same
  depth, whose heavier line put more scope/belly in the water for the extra
  weight (`ZrcwcugaEe4`). Documented night-class floors from other sources
  sit within this same 65–150 lb range: a minimum of 80 lb (80–100 lb
  recommended) with leader to 200 lb for the night bite specifically, since
  night fish can run anywhere from ~20 lb schoolies to 200 lb+ cows on the
  same stop (`RfiC8sfIWTk`); a recommended 80–150 lb class reasoned as
  sizing for the biggest fish that could show even though most landed fish
  graded 30–60 lb (`149px8WQ2Ng`); and higher floors still on individual
  trips — 80 lb braid/130 lb leader on one boat, 100 lb braid/100 lb mono
  minimum to a 200–400 lb leader running 350 g+ jigs on another, with an
  explicit caution against dropping to 30 lb line on a knife jig
  (`UfuiWFVvz2E`, `oB4BpIUTTl4`). Daytime jigging with lighter
  flat-fall-style jigs (Colt-Sniper class, 80–120 g) fishes noticeably
  lighter — **40–50 lb test** (`RfiC8sfIWTk`).
- **Terminal — single bottom assist pair, no top hook, is the baseline.**
  The fish suck the jig in, so a top hook adds little; a top hook also
  risks fouling the fish in the cheek or pec on the swinging jig, moving
  the fight off the mouth and putting a hook near the rail or a deckhand at
  gaff. An attributed variant fished side by side: a single top assist plus
  a short double on the bottom, tied with deliberately light-wire hooks,
  reported landing fish consistently on the bottom hook (sometimes pinned
  through the mouth on both) and framed as an increasingly common San Diego
  rig, standard in Japan. Full rigging detail (hooks-must-not-cross rule;
  speed-jig assists run shorter than slow-pitch assists) is in
  [knife jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md) (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`).
- **Ring vs. swivel at the jig follows how much flutter the jig already
  has.** A welded or solid ring, split-ringed to the jig rather than a
  ball-bearing swivel, is the default for longer bluefin jigs — a swivel
  accentuates a jig's flutter, and on a jig that already flutters heavily
  that extra action makes it less attractive to bluefin, not more; a solid
  ring also lets the jig stay in plane as it rises on the rip. The exception
  is jig-dependent: a jig that flutters less on its own benefits from the
  swivel instead — the choice tracks the specific jig's built-in action, not
  a blanket rule (`Et2TUskzCSs`). A related mechanical-connection axis for
  this same choice is in [assist hooks — connecting to the jig, solid ring
  vs swivel](../rigging/assist-hooks.md#connecting-to-the-jig--solid-ring-vs-swivel-unaratgf_a).
- **Leader-to-jig connection has several attributed builds fished side by
  side, not reconciled:** a ~2–3 ft, 200 lb fluoro/mono bite leader crimped
  directly to the jig, so a big fish can't bite through it (`nRFFM8DT-og`,
  `Ul5FLB2dFgQ`; see [bite leaders](../rigging/bite-leaders.md)); no crimp
  at all, with 80–100 lb braid running through an FG connection to a
  15–20 ft mono/fluoro top shot tied direct to the jig (`j37zxs33gws`); a
  pre-made 200 lb fluoro leader split-ringed onto the jig's front, no crimp
  or direct tie (`6_E5JCRvazc`); a swivel-plus-split-ring build — a 200 lb
  leader to a 400 lb swivel to a 300 lb split ring that replaces the jig's
  factory split ring, paired with upsized assist hooks, reasoned as
  reducing abrasion (`AyN9MBWg-XY`); and a no-crimp, no-swivel build —
  braid straight to a fluoro leader (documented at both 6–10 ft and 10 ft
  on different trips) — where a bulky knot's resistance through the guides
  on the retrieve is the deliberate warning sign to slow down before it
  winds into the rod tip and cracks the guide (`gevNj2Y1Ep8`,
  `-MP2RqJC7B0`).
- **Jig:** [knife-jig class](../lures/knife-jigs.md), 150–400 g slender
  profiles.

**Yellowtail-specific sizing and a secondhand starter rig are queued for a
species-technique spin-out** (pending, `species/yellowtail-knife-jigging.md`):
a Sea of Cortez sizing range that starts around 150 g and works up to about
350 g — heavier becomes a lot of work for little added benefit — paired with
the speed-up-not-slow-down bite response logged above (`M8hOYQ_6rSg`); and a
separate, explicitly secondhand SoCal starter build pairing a Phoenix
Megalodon 607 (rated 200–300 g) with a Shimano Trinidad 20A and 25–40 yd of
40–60 lb mono top shot tied straight to the jig, no fluoro leader
(`LTCFjqtSI8g`).

A separate night deep-drop rig for bluefin marked to ~400 ft (a wired-through
weighted jig body, not a slender knife profile) and a speed/fast-pitch
jigging hook-placement note (two hooks off the nose for that retrieve style)
are logged in
[Searcher bluefin jig ladder by daypart and depth](../tackle/searcher-bluefin-jig-ladder-by-daypart-and-depth.md).

## Common failures

- **Dropping off the stern** — the line scopes out and the jig never
  reaches the called depth; throw up-current and stay vertical
  (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`).
- **No depth reference** — unmarked or uncolored braid means the jig can't
  be put on the captain's called number.
- **Over-rigged hooks** (multiple assists top and bottom) — foul-hooks
  fish, moves the fight off the mouth, and risks a hook at the rail; a
  single bottom pair is the safer baseline.
- **Tightlining the drop** — resistance on the jig's nose kills the
  flutter/dart; slack it down instead. This is specific to this note's
  assist-hook rig; a treble-hooked iron wants the opposite for a different
  reason — see
  [iron jigs — hook style](../lures/iron-jigs.md#hook-style--assist-vs-treble)
  (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`).
- **Winding on the bite like bait fishing, or striking before the fish has
  it** — opens the hook on the barb; wait for drag to run, then drive it
  home with hard swings off the spool (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`).
- **Strike drag set too heavy (35 lb+)** — can rip the outfit out of an
  angler's hands on the first hookup; 19–20 lb is the safer, documented
  setting (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`).
