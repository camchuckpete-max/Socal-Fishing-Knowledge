# Ned Rig

Execution for the Ned rig in the bays: a **small soft stickbait on a mushroom-head
jig, hopped subtly along the bottom, with the bite read in the line before you
feel it in the rod.** A finesse presentation for pressured, not-giant bay fish
that will refuse bigger, faster baits. Where these fish sit in the
[bays and harbors](../locations/bays-and-harbors.md) is the router; this note
owns the rig and the cadence.

## Reach for this when

- **Pressured [spotted bay bass](../species/spotted-bay-bass.md)** in the back
  bay — and anything else bottom-oriented that will bite; the rig catches
  "basically anything."
- You want a **finesse** presentation: small profile, light line, subtle action
  for fish that won't commit to a full-size swimbait.

## Building the rig (Nate Wii)

- **Two parts only:** a **mushroom-style Ned head** (a small lead head) and a
  small soft bait — a **ZMan finesse TRD** is a favorite because the elastic
  plastic **stretches and survives — you can catch 50 fish on one bait**, which
  matters when the back bay hands you fish after fish.
- The TRD has a **top and a bottom**: it is **slightly thinner toward the top and
  tapers to a thicker bottom** (which helps it sink better), and markings on top
  show where the hook exits. Thread it by **stretching the plastic farther than
  you normally would, then bringing it back** so it seats straight on the head.
- Fish it on **light spinning: 10 lb braid, 8 lb leader, a 4-to-10 lb rod.** The
  fish aren't giants — if you hook a big one, **let it run and play it out** on
  the light tackle.
- **Attributed alternative combo (Roman Castro, 6L8nIFeqvkw, 2019-04-24; medium
  confidence):** a heavier-rod variant for the same TRD-on-Ned-head
  presentation — a 6'6" medium-heavy, fast-action spinning rod (rated 8–15 lb
  line, 3/16–3/4 oz lure weight) and a 2500-class spinning reel, 10 lb mainline
  to a 10 lb fluorocarbon leader. Side by side with Winnicki's lighter
  4–10 lb-rod / 8 lb-leader class above — both are finesse-class combos, not a
  contradiction, just two working setups.

## Cadence — and watch the line

- **Let it sink all the way to the bottom, watching your line the whole way
  down.** Pick up the slack once it lands.
- **Bounce it** off the bottom, then **let it fall back to the bottom** — a
  subtle hop-and-drop, not a steady swim (the TRD has no tail, so a straight
  retrieve does nothing for it).
- **Read the bite in your line.** You will **see the bite in the line before you
  feel it in the rod** — when the line **taps and then starts moving off faster**,
  that's the eat. Watching how the line moves is the core skill of the rig.
- **Close the bail the instant the bait hits the water** — don't let it free-spool
  to bottom. A lot of bites come on the initial drop, and free-spooling misses
  them; engaging the reel immediately makes the bait fall at a slight angle
  instead of straight down, and a fish can still be felt taking it on that
  angled fall. You'll know it's reached bottom when the line — which looked
  straight from rod tip to the surface — visibly bows (Roman Castro,
  6L8nIFeqvkw, 2019-04-24; medium confidence).
- **Two retrieve styles once it's down** (Roman Castro, 6L8nIFeqvkw, 2019-04-24;
  medium confidence):
  - **Reel-only hop:** hold the rod level to slightly up (~20°) and barely move
    it; give the reel a **half crank** to lift the bait off bottom, let it drop
    back, and let it sit 2–4 seconds before the next half crank. Speed up to a
    full or double crank to cover more water when the half-crank pace isn't
    producing.
  - **Rod-lift-and-slack-pickup:** in grass, or wherever the bait keeps
    snagging, lift the rod tip (roughly 20° up to 45–80° depending on how big a
    hop you want) to pop the bait up and clear of the grass, then take up the
    resulting slack with the reel as it falls — this pulls the bait up and out
    of standing grass instead of dragging it through, and doubles as a way to
    feel out where the grass actually is.
- **Don't work the last quarter to third of the retrieve.** Once the bait is
  coming up off bottom toward the boat near the end of a cast, stop imparting
  action and just wind it in — bites concentrate on bottom contact, not on the
  rise back to the boat, so working that last stretch mostly costs time (Roman
  Castro, 6L8nIFeqvkw, 2019-04-24; medium confidence).

## Bite detection & hookset

(Roman Castro, 6L8nIFeqvkw, 2019-04-24; medium confidence)

- **Don't set the hook — reel into it.** On light line with a thin-wire Ned
  hook, swinging on a tap rips the hook out of the fish's mouth, bends it out,
  or snaps the line. Instead, start reeling; the fish loads the rod and hooks
  itself. By the time you feel the tap-tap, the fish is usually already hooked
  and you're just winding it up.
- **Set the drag below your line's rated strength** — e.g., on 10 lb line, run
  the drag around 7–8 lb — so an instinctive hard hookset can't snap the line
  outright. Contrasts with a heavier-tackle habit (65 lb braid to 15 lb
  fluorocarbon, drag near max, swing hard on the bite) that doesn't transfer to
  this finesse gear.
- **Learn the difference between bottom contact and a bite** by keeping the
  bait on the bottom and feeling out the normal nicks, bumps, and scrapes of
  rock and eelgrass — a bite reads as the bait suddenly *not* touching bottom
  anymore, or as a different kind of tap than a snag.

## Common failures

- **Retrieving it like a swimbait** — the tailless TRD wants a bottom hop, not a
  straight wind.
- **Not watching the line** — the take is visual and light; wait to feel it in
  the rod and you miss it.
- **Rigging it crooked or on the wrong end** — seat it off the thicker bottom
  with the hook exiting the marked top so it sinks and sits right.
