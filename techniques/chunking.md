# Chunking

Execution for chunking: cutting bait into pieces to build a **chum line**, then
drifting a hook bait back through it so it looks like just another falling chunk.
The tool for fish you can see or mark but can't get to bite. Where the fish are
and why they've shut off are the routers:
[dorado](../species/dorado.md) and [bluefin tuna](../species/bluefin-tuna.md).

## Reach for this when

- Fish are **marking on the meter or visible but not biting** — the chunk line is
  what wakes them up.
- **Dorado on a paddy** that has gone quiet — chunking gets a shut-off school
  competing again. See [dorado](../species/dorado.md).
- **Tuna at anchor or on a drift** where a live-bait flyline alone isn't drawing
  them up. See [bluefin tuna](../species/bluefin-tuna.md).
- **Guadalupe Island, Baja big tuna** that are hungry and indiscriminate — see
  [Guadalupe Island big tuna](#guadalupe-island-big-tuna) below; tackle for this
  application is in [Gear class](#gear-class).

## Building the chum line

- Cut bait into pieces and feed them **steadily, a few at a time** — enough to
  hold a scent and sink trail without stuffing the fish. The chunk line is an
  artificial current-fed feed lane leading back to your hook bait.
- Watch the **drift angle of the chunks** relative to the boat — it reads the
  true current and tells you where your bait needs to be. If the chunks and the
  boat lie the same way, there's no current working the line.

## Drifting the hook bait

- **Match the hook bait's fall to the chunks.** Free-spool line so the hooked
  cut bait sinks back **at the same rate** as the free chunks around it — any
  drag that makes it fall unnaturally or hang in the column gives it away.
- Feed line as the drift takes it; a chunk that behaves exactly like the others
  is the one that gets eaten.

## Chum bait selection

A tougher chunk bait can out-fish premium live bait outright, not just serve as
filler chum. After a trolling bite came off a current break (see [electronics
and sounder](../planning/electronics-and-sounder.md) for the SST-break method
that found the spot), the key to keeping a school around the boat was chumming
with **chunked bonita, mackerel, skipjack, and/or squid** rather than premium
live sardines — on one trip a dorado in the mix refused the sardine outright but
ate the cut chunk as soon as it was dropped in (`uyjTdgIw-1k`). That's a
bait-*class* preference distinct from, but compatible with, the picky-dorado
leader-stepdown doctrine in [dorado](../species/dorado.md): when a fish won't
commit to the "good" live bait, switching to a chunked/cut offering may draw a
bite before stepping the leader down. Region: SoCal, Mission Bay/offshore.

**Stock the chunk supply proactively.** If you run into a school of skipjack
while running to the grounds, catch a couple and keep them for chunk bait
rather than cutting into the fin (sardine) bait supply for chum — having chunk
bait aboard and deploying it "when the timing is right" is what keeps fish
around the boat once you're on them (`Ow3an9lSVh4`).

## On a paddy (dorado wake-up)

A quiet paddy holding dorado often just needs a trigger: chunking cut bait
around the kelp gets the school fired up and racing for food, at which point a
drifted bait or a hooked chunk gets crushed. Chunking sits alongside the other
paddy wake-up tricks in the [dorado](../species/dorado.md) note.

## Drift chumming — downhill corner

Every boat drifts **stern-first**, from big sportboats down to small runabouts
(`e16i7zKq1FY`). Because of that, the wind always ends up hitting one side of
the boat, and the lines trail off that same side. Whichever side the
wind/current has set the drift and lines on (say, port), throw the chum **as
far as you can off that downhill (lee) corner**, feeding it there constantly
rather than off the stern generically. This is the corner-selection call for
the moving/drifting scenario in [building the chum line](#building-the-chum-line)
above; it's a different geometry from the anchored-boat entries below, where
the boat isn't drifting and the current runs past a stationary hull instead.

## Anchored chumming

The drift-chumming mechanics above assume you're moving with the current; at
anchor over structure the geometry flips, and the placement rule is: **don't
chum off the stern — chum from the bow or up-current side.** Chum tossed off
the back on an anchored boat drifts away from your own baits, toward whoever's
anchored downcurrent instead (`yKaHcxX46l4`). Throw chum **as far up-current
along the side of the boat as you can**; by the time it sinks through the
column it settles in close beside the hull, putting the boat itself — and your
bait — inside the chum line rather than watching it wash off (`yKaHcxX46l4`).
Cut squid heads, tentacles, and guts (see [hooks](../tackle/hooks.md) for the
strip-bait prep) are a common cheap chum source for this style of anchored
structure fishing — sheephead, sargo, and small bass.

**Sources give two different explanations for why the stern doesn't work, and
they don't need to agree for the rule to hold.** One account frames an
anchored/drifting boat's current as running **down the sides, not off the
stern** (`yKaHcxX46l4`); another frames it as running **fore-to-aft, off the
stern** (`xI9tPJFXbUM`). Both reach the identical operational rule — avoid a
stern-mounted or stern-tossed chum source, favor the bow/forward/up-current
placement instead — so the placement instruction doesn't depend on which
current model is right; pick the mechanism that matches what you see off your
own hull and follow the same rule.

**Chum bucket rig — bow, not stern.** A bait-shop **chum bucket** with
**4–5 holes cut in it**, tied off the side of the boat rather than tossed
loose. Tie it at the **bow, not the stern**: on the fore-to-aft current model
above, a bucket tied at the stern chums "way back behind you" instead of near
your baits, and a hooked fish's running line has a good chance of tangling in
a stern-mounted bucket — a hazard beyond current geometry (`xI9tPJFXbUM`). Tied
at the bow instead, by the time the chum sinks through the column it arrives
roughly at the stern, chumming for your own boat rather than for boats anchored
or drifting behind you (`xI9tPJFXbUM`). Tie-up point scales with comfort — as
far up the side as feels comfortable, with the bow as the default.

**Sink-timing, worked example.** At the Pendleton artificial reef in about
**35–40 ft**, a chum bucket loaded with ground-up sardines, mackerel, or
anchovies and tied off the bow reaches full fishing depth by the time it drifts
back under the anglers at the stern on a mid-length boat: by the time the
chum's plume is visible in the water it's already sunk **3–4 ft**, and the trip
from bow to stern happens faster than a person can walk the same distance on
deck (`bM2vUS1B-yQ`). ⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-by-source): the transcript
states only that the chum's plume has sunk 3–4 ft by the time it's visible near
the bow — it never says the bucket reaches the 35–40 ft bottom by the stern;
the "how fast can you walk 30 feet" line in the source is answered "pretty
fast" and pivots into a comparison to how fast a fish can flick its tail from
bottom to top, not into a stated walking-vs-drift-time comparison for the
bucket. Pair this with a hooked-weight caution: dropping a bait
on a heavier weight straight down past a mark spooks the fish holding at that
depth; a small weight flicked off the bow and allowed to settle on the way back
to the stern reaches the fish already moving naturally with the chum instead of
falling through them (`bM2vUS1B-yQ`).

## Kelp-bed sequencing

On a kelp edge with current running parallel down the coast, the anchoring
sequence itself carries the chum. With the boat sitting on the target edge,
toss a couple of scoops/netfuls of chum right there first. Then motor up
**ahead of that spot, further up-current**, and drop the anchor from there,
backing the boat down in reverse onto the water just chummed. Timed right, the
current has been carrying scent out of the kelp the whole time, and fish come
out of the kelp and start biting **the moment the anchor is down** and the boat
settles back onto the chum line (`RXNebDr4j7s`). See
[calico bass](../species/calico-bass.md) for the kelp-edge species routing this
serves. A separate on-the-water session recorded an immediate, aggressive bite
at the moment chum hit an isolated kelp stringer — see the
[evidence file](evidence/chunking.md).

## Prey density and feeding frenzy

As you chum, periodically toss another handful of cut squid chunks into the
water to raise the **prey density level**. Past a certain density, even fish
that weren't actively feeding come over and start eating because they see the
others doing it — that's what turns a slow pick into a genuine feeding frenzy
rather than a trickle of bites (`f4qYtHACGyk`).

**Artificial reefs** are built, fish-holding structure sitting close to most
SoCal harbors, and most have been fished hard for years; big fish are still
there because most anglers don't fish them properly — proper anchoring, proper
chumming, and proper bait presentation together, not chumming alone
(`f4qYtHACGyk`). Once a frenzy is going on one of these reefs you don't
actually know what's holding on it: sargo up to **5–6 lb**, big white seabass,
big sheephead, and calico bass all come this way, and even yellowtail off an
artificial reef using this exact chum method (`f4qYtHACGyk`).

**Origin and species mix.** The SoCal artificial-reef system was built
specifically for sport fishermen in the **1950s–70s**, funded by sportfishing
money rather than a commercial-fishing program (`bM2vUS1B-yQ`, `f4qYtHACGyk`).
Species mix named on these reefs: **calico bass, sand bass, halibut, white
seabass, a range of rockfish, sculpin, and sargo** (`bM2vUS1B-yQ`).

**Generalized beyond artificial reefs.** The same prey-density mechanism holds
as a general SoCal principle, not reef-specific: increase the prey density
level in the water by feeding the fish what they want, and fish that don't even
want to eat will feed because they don't know when the next food is coming
(`BdRX4b8Fo5w`). Applied to **offshore tuna fishing and inshore calico bass
fishing alike**. The mechanism pairs with a big [bait tank](../bait/bait-tanks.md)
and a fast bait-changing cadence (see [fishing live bait](../bait/fishing-live-bait.md))
— density comes from chum, changed baits, and hooked fish all going into the
water together (`BdRX4b8Fo5w`).

**Bait size drives the density, not just chum volume.** Smaller bait thrown as
chum raises the prey-density level higher than the same volume of big bait
would, and that density spike is what causes a **reaction bite** — demonstrated
back to back on yellowfin tuna working a chum slick and on calico bass working
a piece of bait around a kelp string (`qv0QbLgp72o`). The hook bait itself
stays big; it's the traveled/thrown chum bait that gets sized small
specifically to maximize density (`qv0QbLgp72o`).

## Chum tube

A sportboat can carry dozens of scoops of live bait; a private 6-pack or
personal boat (e.g. a Sea Swirl or a Boston Whaler) can't, so raising the
prey-density level above has to come from a device that stretches a small bait
supply instead of from sheer volume (`JK2-cpaXkI8`).

- **The device:** a tube loaded with cut sardines, anchovies, squid, or
  mackerel — whatever's on hand — with an internal plunger/chopper worked up
  and down. The chopper smashes the bait inside the tube; the crushed bait
  works its way out through small holes cut around the tube, feeding a
  continuous scent/particle trail into the water at anchor rather than one big
  dump over the side (`JK2-cpaXkI8`).
- It converts a limited amount of cut bait into the same sustained
  prey-density effect described above without needing the 30–40-scoop bait
  supply a sportboat carries (`JK2-cpaXkI8`).
- Fished the same way as the anchored-chumming entries above — alongside a
  live-bait flyline at anchor over structure — to draw fish up onto the
  presented baits (`JK2-cpaXkI8`). ⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-by-source): the
  cited transcript describes the chum-tube device itself (loading, plunger,
  holes, continuous trail) but never mentions pairing it with a live-bait
  flyline or names fishing it at anchor over structure — that pairing isn't
  stated in this source.

## Guadalupe Island big tuna

Framed as a Guadalupe Island, Baja (baja-pacific-north) tip: big tuna there are
"very indiscriminate" when hungry, taking a chunk of mackerel or "a chunk of
something else" readily (`NJ9ZLAU3sls`). Species is not named on camera beyond
"tuna"; grade is called out at **100, 120, 130, and 140 lb**. ⚠ Fact-check
(single-source): the behavior claim and the grade figures both rest on this
one Searcher Tackle Tip Thursday video, uncorroborated elsewhere in this
note's citation set.

The stated secret: when letting the chunk out, **strip line so it's sinking
naturally, as if there's nothing attached to it** — the same match-the-fall
principle in [drifting the hook bait](#drifting-the-hook-bait) above, restated
as this presenter's own explicit "secret" for Guadalupe's big tuna
(`NJ9ZLAU3sls`). Full tackle for this application is in
[Gear class](#gear-class) below.

## Fighting fish out of shallow rock

Region: SoCal, Northwest Harbor, San Clemente Island — anchored tight to
island rock structure (named on camera as "Coiler Rock," asr-uncertain, not
independently confirmed) in about **6 ft** of water, fishing sardines; calico
to **8 lb** taken here on this pattern (`KCcEqHSZ84k`).

Once a calico is hooked this shallow and this tight to rock, the fight
technique matters as much as the chum/bait: **hold the rod tip as high as
possible and wind as fast as possible without ever pumping the rod** — grind
the fish straight to the boat rather than pump-and-reel. **Never let the tip
drop**: a dropped tip gives the fish the leverage to bury its head into the
rock (or kelp) below and root the leader off — once that happens the fish is
lost. Constant upward pressure from hookup to gaff is the rule for this
shallow, tight-to-rock scenario specifically (`KCcEqHSZ84k`). Line for this
application is in [Gear class](#gear-class) below. A second session at another
shallow rock spot in the same harbor repeated the pattern — see the
[evidence file](evidence/chunking.md).

## Gear class

- **Flyline/soft-rod class** — the hook bait is drifted back through the chum
  on a rod soft enough not to kill its natural swim/fall; the standard
  chunking hookup for dorado and tuna at anchor or on a drift. See
  [gear classes](../tackle/gear-classes.md).
- **Finesse fly-line class — light, pressured reef fish.** At shallow
  artificial reefs fished hard for years, scale everything down: hook tied
  **straight to the mono line** — no swivel, no beads, no leader break — and a
  small split shot **2–3 in above the hook**, sized to current: **1/16 oz** by
  default, stepping up to **1/8 oz** only if current demands it, **never
  heavier than 1/4 oz**. Going heavier drags the bait to bottom, which draws
  shovelnose guitarfish or bat rays instead of the target species. Bait
  presentation is a strip — "like a piece of spaghetti" — not a big wad of
  squid, which doesn't get eaten. Light spinning rods or small level-wind
  outfits, flicking chum/squid strips out behind the anchored boat
  (`f4qYtHACGyk`). ⚠ Fact-check (single-source): the 1/16–1/8–1/4 oz split-shot
  ladder rests on this one video, uncorroborated by another source in this
  note's citation set. The light shot is what lets the bait fall at the chum's
  own rate — see [drifting the hook bait](#drifting-the-hook-bait) above.
- **Rail-rod / heavy-tuna class — Guadalupe big tuna.** Full reel/line/rod/
  hook/swivel build (Penn International 16VISX, 100 lb braid, 60–130 rod, "a
  nice big hook that'll hold them," optional swivel) is logged in
  [Searcher big-tuna rig ladder](../tackle/searcher-big-tuna-rig-ladder.md)
  (`NJ9ZLAU3sls`).
- **Fighting leader — shallow rock structure.** **65 lb braid straight to a
  30 lb fluorocarbon top shot** took calico to 8 lb out of 6 ft of water tight
  to island rock (`KCcEqHSZ84k`) — notably lighter than the 40–65 lb fluoro
  leader logged for kelp-stringer swimbait work in
  [calico bass](../species/calico-bass.md); a second data point (open rock on
  bait vs. dense kelp on a weedless lure), kept side by side rather than
  reconciled.

## Common failures

- **Over-chumming** — feeding so much cut bait the fish fill up on free chunks
  and ignore the hook.
- **Under-reading the current** — dropping chunks with no current to carry them,
  so there's no line for fish to follow.
- A hook bait that **falls wrong** — too much line drag, so it doesn't match the
  free chunks and the fish refuse it.
- **Fishing too heavy on pressured reef fish** — big line, big hooks, swivels
  and beads spook fish that have been caught around for years, and a heavier
  split shot (past 1/4 oz) drags the bait to bottom, drawing bycatch (shovelnose
  guitarfish, bat rays) instead of the target species; go straight-tied and
  shot-sized-to-current instead (`f4qYtHACGyk`).
- **A dropped rod tip on a hooked fish tight to rock or kelp** gives it the
  leverage to bury its head and root the leader off; hold the tip high and
  wind through it, never pump (`KCcEqHSZ84k`).
