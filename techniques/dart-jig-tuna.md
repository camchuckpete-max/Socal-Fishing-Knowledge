# Dart Jig — Daytime Finesse Tuna

A castable, daytime-only finesse presentation for [bluefin](../species/bluefin-tuna.md)
and [yellowfin tuna](../species/yellowfin-tuna.md) that are feeding on small
bait and won't commit to the bigger, "crazy looking" lures fished at night —
sink it on a controlled drop and crank hard the instant it stops (Steve
Carson, Penn/SearcherSportfishing, "Tackle Tip Thursday Vol. 234 (How to Use
Dart Lures)," `TU5quAG4atM`, 2024-06-13; `searcher-sportfishing` registered
channel, first citation of this specific technique — medium confidence).
Distinct from [yo-yo iron](yo-yo-iron.md) — no fixed bottom mark to drop to,
the boat's drift (not a structure mark) sets the drop angle, and the lure
class is lighter and specifically castable. Also distinct from [foamer
casting](foamer-casting.md) — this is for fish marked or suspected around
the boat and biting poorly, not fish actively breaking on top.

## Reach for this when

- Tuna are keyed on **small bait** and refusing bigger presentations — the
  lure class this note covers runs roughly **50 g up to 100 g, maybe 125 g
  at the most**, sized well below night-bite jig weight for that reason.
- It's **daytime** specifically — framed as the finesse counterpart to the
  bigger lures thrown after dark.
- Fish are **around the boat but not biting well** — the source frames the
  full drop-and-crank routine below as one of the most effective ways to
  connect "on a day when the fish are around the boat and they're not biting
  super good," for bluefin and yellowfin "later in the year" (i.e., later in
  the SoCal season).
- **You have to be able to cast** for this to fish effectively — it's a
  jig-stick/casting presentation, not a straight-down drop.

## Mechanics

Two ways to work it, both ending in the same rule: **free-spool the sink,
crank hard the instant it stops.** There is no bottom under tuna, so a jig
that stops sinking didn't hit bottom — a tuna took it, and the window to
come tight is only a few seconds because "these don't taste very good" and
get spit fast. **Don't set the hook like a TV fisherman** — just put the
reel in gear and crank hard and fast to come tight.

- **Windy-side drop.** Walk to the windward side of the boat (typically the
  bow), put the reel in **free-spool**, and let the jig sink as the wind
  pushes the boat off the sinking line. Thumb/manage the drop so it tracks **as
  straight down as possible** despite the boat sliding away from it. **Always
  mark your line.** If it's still dropping past about **300 ft**, start
  winding back rather than let it keep going: tuck the rod under your left
  armpit, point the rod tip down, and wind back fast — you don't have to wind
  all the way to the surface, winding up to roughly **50 ft down** saves
  energy before dropping it again. As the boat drifts further from the drop
  point, re-position (wind in and reset) to keep the line running vertical
  rather than let it swing out at an angle.
- **Bow-casting technique.** From the bow, make a "nice healthy cast" (no
  need for a world-record cast) at an angle, out in the direction the boat is
  drifting. Let it sink in free-spool — **roughly 40% of all bites come on
  the sink**, so stay attentive through the drop. Timed right, the jig is
  running close to straight down and sitting around **250–300 ft** by the
  time your line crosses the boat's anchor stanchion; put it in gear and wind
  back **fast, only about halfway to the boat**. No bite: drop it back into
  free-spool and let it sink again. Move to the windy-side rail as the boat
  continues to drift, and repeat the sink/wind-halfway cycle from there; on
  the next drop (now angling out the other side) wind it **all the way back
  to the boat** and start the whole cycle over from the bow. Worked as a
  continuous bow → windy rail → full retrieve loop while the boat drifts.

## Common failures

- **Setting the hook instead of cranking through it** — with a tuna, the
  cure for the drop-stopping suddenly is to engage gear and grind, not to
  strike like a bass angler; a TV-style hookset wastes the few seconds
  before the fish spits the jig.
- **Fishing it straight down instead of managing the drift angle** — the
  whole point of both variants is keeping the sink tracking vertical
  (windy-side) or timed to the boat's drift (bow cast); a drop that's
  allowed to swing out sideways fishes a different, less-vertical part of
  the column than intended.
- **Undersized hooks** — stock hooks on these lures are not tuna-rated (thin,
  weak); see the hook swap in [iron jigs](../lures/iron-jigs.md#hook-style--assist-vs-treble)
  and the rig ladder link above.
