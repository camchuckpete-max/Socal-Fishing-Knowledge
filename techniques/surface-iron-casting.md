# Surface Iron — The Cast

The cast is the half of [surface iron](surface-iron.md) that decides whether
you get a shot at all — a cast that reaches the fish out-produces a short
cast every time, live bait or jig. The [jig-stick class](../tackle/gear-classes.md)
rod (9–10 ft) is what buys that distance, and the rod does the work: the most
common error is shoving the lure out with the arms instead of loading the
blank. This note owns getting the jig out there and putting it where you want
it; [surface iron](surface-iron.md) picks up once it lands.

## Reach for this when

- You need maximum distance to reach fish that spook off the boat — [boiling
  or breezing](../planning/search-and-glassing.md#surface-sign-vocabulary)
  [yellowtail](../species/yellowtail.md), a foamer, or fish sitting well off
  the rail.
- Wind is a factor: trajectory changes with the wind — a tailwind or no wind
  calls for a high arc, a headwind calls for a low line drive (see
  [Trajectory](#trajectory--the-wind-decides-it)).
- You're on a crowded sport-boat bow or rail with no room for a full
  pendulum swing (see [Mechanics](#mechanics)).
- You want to rehearse the cast off the water before it has to work on a
  moving school (see [Learning it off the water](#learning-it-off-the-water)).
- You're picking a first outfit for the technique — see [the baitcaster path
  in](#the-baitcaster-path-in--a-route-and-its-limit) for a lighter starting
  route on sub-2 oz irons.

## Mechanics

- Hang the jig about a leader length below the tip — roughly **18 in** with
  the short leader below.
- Take the rod back to a **2 o'clock** position, let the blank load up, then
  launch and slowly turn the wrist through — the sweep is 2 o'clock down to
  9 o'clock. Let the equipment unload; don't muscle it.
- **Pendulum swing, not a side-arm sling.** Hang the jig taut near the
  reel/handle and build the swing by moving the rod tip **up and down**, not
  side to side — the up-down motion is what creates the pendulum, and a
  longer rod gives a longer pendulum arc between tip and jig, hence more
  distance (`VpW91AKOFVQ`). On a crowded sport-boat bow there's often no room
  for a full pendulum swing; the fallback is a shorter, compact one-motion
  lob (bring the jig down to about handle height, release in one motion) —
  safe in tight quarters, at the cost of distance. Always look behind you
  before casting on a crowded rail, and watch the **direction** of the
  cast — landing a fresh lure on top of a rail-mate's hooked fish tangles
  lines and can cost that angler his fish (`VpW91AKOFVQ`, `eL1Qm33-Mj0`).
- **Release point:** let the jig go on the **outside part of the swing** as
  it comes back around, not at the top or bottom of the arc (`3dVc-2rsYII`).
- **Lay the reel on its side through the cast.** Rotate the reel onto its
  side during the cast so the spool rests against only one bearing instead of
  sitting upright — the stated payoff is a noticeably farther cast
  (`KLoEJInlmZo`), independently corroborated by a second source six years
  later coaching a rail-mate mid-trip: "No, no, no. Look at your reel.
  Sideways." (`ejUTAnONf7o`).
- **Locating the reel on a rod with no fixed reel seat.** Some jig sticks
  built for this technique ship with no permanent reel seat. Set the reel by
  measuring from the **crook of the elbow to the tip of the middle finger** —
  that span, within about an inch either way, is where the reel goes. Too
  high and the off hand can't get in front of the reel to pull on a hooked
  fish; too low and the cast loses the leverage/action that makes it work
  (`49joKHD7Umc`).

## Trajectory — the wind decides it

> "You got to think about jig fishing and casting surface irons like
> football, like you're throwing a Hail Mary — you want to throw it into the
> sky. Unless there is wind and you're trying to push right through it."
> (`IhBY5RHFvuk`)

- **Wind at your back, or no wind: throw it high.** Finish with **hands
  high** and the jig into the sky — the wind carries it, and the arc is where
  the distance comes from.
- **Wind in your face: line drive.** Cast hard and fast to push the jig
  through and under the wind; lobbing into a headwind gets the jig blown back
  at you.

This is the one place in surface-iron doctrine where **casting hard is
correct** — everywhere else, hard is the mistake (see
[Effort](#effort--softer-casts-go-as-far) below).

## Aim — the follow-through, not your feet

You don't have to reposition on a rail or a bow to change where the jig
goes:

> "No matter where your feet are — wherever your left shoulder is facing, if
> you follow through like this, you're going to cast right into that
> section. So just make sure when you're casting that your hands follow
> through."
> (`IhBY5RHFvuk`)

That is the practical answer to a boil that pops up 90° off your stance: you
redirect with the follow-through in the time you have, rather than shuffling
around a crowded rail.

- **Distance is secondary to placement.** Landing the jig ON moving fish
  beats maximizing cast distance — a **75 yd** cast at fish sitting only
  **40 yd** out overshoots them for no benefit (`IhBY5RHFvuk`). When a school
  is moving (e.g. tracking under working birds), get ahead of its line of
  travel and cut it off rather than casting into its wake, so the fish keep
  closing on the jig instead of moving away from it. Individually spotted
  fish, not a full boil, can be sight-cast directly, with birds working
  overhead as a supporting visual sign (`6EDQtQHEwFE`).

## Effort — softer casts go as far

Directly demonstrated on camera, casting the same jig both ways at the same
target (`IhBY5RHFvuk`):

> "I was trying to cast this really fast, which is not the way to go… You
> could cast it a lot softer and still get the same distance."

Soft casts reached within **2–5 yd** of a target roughly **70–80 yd** out;
hard casts went no farther and produced backlashes. On a **parabolic** blank,
whipping it fast is when backlashes happen. The correction is the same one
[Mechanics](#mechanics) makes: let the blank load and unload.

The stated exception is the headwind line drive above.

## Matching the cast to the rod

- **Rod action sets the hang length.** On a **slow/parabolic** rod, let more
  line out below the tip and load it properly. On a **fast-action** rod, keep
  the jig up shorter and use the quick whip — every rod needs a different
  casting action, and the ~18 in hang length in [Mechanics](#mechanics) is
  the fast-rod baseline, not a universal (`IhBY5RHFvuk`).
- Test a blank for how parabolic it is by flexing it and watching for the
  bend to reach the butt section — that wiggle tells you which of the two
  loading styles above applies (`IhBY5RHFvuk`).
- **Reset spool tension whenever the jig weight changes.** Going from a
  sub-2 oz, 5 in iron to a heavier 6 in iron, a reel started pushing too much
  line and overran; a small tightening of the spool tensioner fixed it on the
  next cast. Treat a jig change as a reel adjustment, not just a lure change
  (`IhBY5RHFvuk`).

## Getting the line right before you need the cast

Before arriving at a stop, free-spool the jig back to about casting length
behind the moving boat, then reel it in under tension at **7–10 mph** so the
line lays tightly on the spool — "that way your next cast is butter"
(`0DKmYId1NN4`). A loosely wound spool is what unloads into an overrun on the
first real cast of the stop.

## Learning it off the water

Surface-iron casting is practicable from shore — rehearse it at a bay-front
park with a rail (`IhBY5RHFvuk`):

- Fish a rail the way you would a sport-boat rail, or set up in a corner the
  way you would the bow.
- **Rehearse the boil drill:** sit in free spool as if waiting, call an
  imaginary boil, and cast to it — the sequence, not just the cast.
- Practice with the specific rod you'll fish, since loading differs rod to
  rod.

## The baitcaster path in — a route, and its limit

Stated as a change of the source's own mind — "I used to be anti these
setups" (`IhBY5RHFvuk`):

- A conventional reel on a full 10 ft jig stick is a lot to handle at once —
  going straight to a 10-footer proved "too much," so the source dropped to a
  9 ft.
- A **high-speed low-profile baitcaster on a softer, shorter swimbait-class
  blank** casts small irons a long way and is far more forgiving of finger
  control while still learning to avoid backlashes.
- **The limit is jig size:** "if you're fishing like a Tady 45 and up, I'd
  probably go with a different rod, not this." The light-blank route is for
  **sub-2 oz, 5–6 in** irons; full-size irons still need the jig stick.
- This is a **starting-path claim, not a replacement** for the jig-stick
  class in [gear classes](../tackle/gear-classes.md), and it sits against
  the high-ratio-reel caution in [surface iron](surface-iron.md#gear-class),
  which is about *retrieve* speed, not casting.

**Why pros pick it too, not just beginners.** Two speakers give stated
reasons for choosing a low-profile baitcaster over a conventional reel like a
[Torium](../tackle/gear-classes.md) for casting irons (`hAryw1v3I68`):

- **The level wind removes line management.** "It's just easier to fish,
  especially with irons. You just have to turn the handle. You don't have to
  worry about your line management."
- **The external brake and spool tensioner let you dial in per cast, on the
  water, switching lure weight** — a Torium or similar conventional reel
  can't do this: "you're fishing a surface iron, then you go to a 60 g Colt
  Sniper, and it's like, 'Oh, I backlash. Let me dial it in a little bit,'
  and where you can't do that with a Torium or any of those kind of reels."
  This is the same underlying problem as the spool-tension bullet in
  [Matching the cast to the rod](#matching-the-cast-to-the-rod) above — here
  it's given as the reason to prefer a baitcaster's external adjustment over
  a conventional reel that lacks it.
- Stated conclusion: "with a low-profile baitcaster, you have a lot more
  control on your casting."

## Guide material — a minor factor next to technique

Ceramic guides run smoother, with less resistance, than standard metal
guides, but guide material "doesn't make that big a difference" for casting
distance — technique and practice account for far more of it than a guide
upgrade does (`49joKHD7Umc`).

## Line choice, as it affects the cast

Braid casts well and is what the distance demonstrations above were run on
(**65–80 lb**) — but the overruns in that same session drew "that's the one
reason I don't like braid" (`IhBY5RHFvuk`). The mono-vs-braid casting-latitude
trade-off is in [surface iron](surface-iron.md#gear-class); nothing here
resolves it.

## Gear class

**[Jig-stick class](../tackle/gear-classes.md), 9–10 ft** — the length is
what buys casting distance and loads the pendulum swing described in
[Mechanics](#mechanics); a shorter rod can't generate the same arc.

A 9–10 ft stick is "not the most fun to fight a fish on," but the stated
priority is getting the cast out to the fish at all — "the main thing is
you've got to get hooked up" (`BLbUu_mfMJY`). Casting reach is ranked above
fight comfort: the rod-length choice is about converting shots into hookups,
not about landing the fish once it's on.

[The baitcaster path in](#the-baitcaster-path-in--a-route-and-its-limit)
covers a lighter, more forgiving starting setup, limited to sub-2 oz, 5–6 in
irons — full-size irons still need the jig-stick class above. Line is
65–80 lb braid on the demonstrated distance casts; see [line
choice](#line-choice-as-it-affects-the-cast) for the mono-vs-braid
casting-latitude trade-off, held against [surface
iron](surface-iron.md#gear-class).

## Common failures

- **Whipping the cast fast instead of loading the blank.** A soft cast
  reaches the same distance as a hard one; whipping a parabolic blank fast is
  when backlashes happen (`IhBY5RHFvuk`) — see
  [Effort](#effort--softer-casts-go-as-far).
- **Pushing/shoving the lure out with the arms** instead of loading the
  rod — the load-and-unload pendulum swing in [Mechanics](#mechanics) is what
  generates distance; muscling it kills both distance and accuracy.
- **A loosely wound spool at the first cast of a stop.** Reel in under
  tension at trolling speed before arriving so the line lays tight on the
  spool; a loose wind is what unloads into an overrun on that first real cast
  (`0DKmYId1NN4`) — see [Getting the line right before you need the
  cast](#getting-the-line-right-before-you-need-the-cast).
- **Lobbing a headwind cast instead of driving it under the wind.** A high
  arc into the wind gets blown back at the caster; the headwind is the one
  place hard-and-fast casting is correct (`IhBY5RHFvuk`) — see
  [Trajectory](#trajectory--the-wind-decides-it).
- **Casting for maximum distance instead of onto the fish.** Overshooting a
  school sitting only 40 yd out with a 75 yd cast wastes the shot; casting
  ahead of a moving school's line of travel keeps fish closing on the jig
  instead of chasing its wake (`IhBY5RHFvuk`) — see
  [Aim](#aim--the-follow-through-not-your-feet).
- **Casting on top of a rail-mate's hookup** — a fresh lure landing in a
  hooked fish's water tangles lines and can cost that angler the fish; watch
  the direction of the cast, not just behind you (`VpW91AKOFVQ`,
  `eL1Qm33-Mj0`).
