# Tube-Bait Fishing

Execution for [tube baits](../lures/tube-baits.md) — the soft-plastic,
internal-lead-head class HookUp Baits made a SoCal staple — fished from a
private boat, party boat, or bay bank for calico and sand bass, yellowtail,
rockfish, halibut, and (occasionally) tuna. The bait class, construction,
colors, and scent system live in [tube baits](../lures/tube-baits.md); this
note owns how to fish them.

**Classification note (Cameron, 2026-08-23):** this content previously lived
in a note titled "glide baits" — a misclassification; a glide bait is a
jointed hard bait ([glide baits](glide-baits.md)), while HookUp Baits are
tube baits. **Confidence:** both principal sources are HookUp Baits' own
principals presenting at trade shows, so product and performance claims stay
**low** per the sponsored-claim rule throughout; mechanism claims are marked
where independently corroborated.

## Reach for this when

- Working the same kelp/reef edges and bay structure as
  [swimbaits](swimbaits.md), and you want a bait that glides on the pause
  over a rock pile or structure edge rather than tracking straight back.
- Fishing a **drifting** party boat rock-cod trip and want a cast-and-drift
  presentation instead of straight-down bait — see the drift technique
  below.
- Deep structure to roughly 200 ft where you want to A/B two colors, or fish
  two depths/weights at once, off one dropper rig.
- Prospecting a kelp paddy for tuna/dorado without spending live bait, or
  punching a bait through a surface skipjack layer to reach yellowfin
  holding below it — see the tuna section below.
- Red-crab-holding structure for [sheephead](../species/sheephead.md) and
  resident bass, or sizing a bait for [California
  halibut](../species/california-halibut.md) — see the color and species
  notes below.


## Rod: parabolic bend, not a tip-only bend

- The most repeated point in the seminar: these baits need a rod that
  **bends all the way through the blank ("parabolic")**, not one that
  flexes at the tip and stiffens through the mid-section. Stated mechanism:
  a hooked fish gets **leverage against the extra weight of a 3–4 oz glide
  bait** hanging beside its face, and a stiff mid-section gives that
  leverage something to work against — the hook pulls "almost every time."
  A full-length soft bend absorbs the leverage and keeps the hook planted.
- This scales with bait size: the effect is worst on the **3–4 oz** glide
  baits; smaller (¼ oz) baits are more forgiving of rod choice.
- Line-class-by-bait-size, his own picks (brand names not carried — mostly
  ASR-uncertain product names in a promotional context): a **¼ oz** bait
  pairs with a rod rated roughly **8–17 lb**; **¼–⅝ oz** baits step up to a
  **10–25 lb**-rated rod; **3–4 oz** tube baits go on **40 lb-class or
  heavier** — he states he does not fish the big tube baits under 40 lb
  class after losing fish on 25 lb gear.
- Length: **8 ft** is his standing preference, valued for casting distance
  and for keeping the tip working through a long parabolic bend.

**A second, independent 40 lb-floor data point (Chad, HookUp Baits
co-owner, `5Oh3IPIC5g4`, 2020):** fishing a **3 oz** bait on a 9 ft
medium-heavy spinning rod, he states directly that **40 lb is the lightest
he'll go with the big baits** ("you don't want... to throw the big baits on
less than 40 pounds, you're gonna get your heart broken") — corroborating,
from a separate seminar two years apart, the 2018 source's "does not fish
the big tube baits under 40 lb class" floor above. Line was **50 lb "Max
Quatro"**-brand braid (a thin, strong braid — product name as heard on
camera, not independently verified) to a **40 lb fluorocarbon** leader.
- **"Big bait, big fish" as a deliberate size filter — the decision and the
  reason (Chad, `5Oh3IPIC5g4`):** he fishes the 3 oz bait all day, every day,
  specifically because it **selects for bigger fish** — "anything with bass
  in it, especially sand bass, calico bass... you eliminate catching anything
  under about 3 pounds," where the average SoCal calico/sand bass is well
  under that. Stated outcome, not just a claim: fishing alongside anglers
  throwing smaller baits who out-number his bite count, his fish "are usually
  double the size every time." Applied deliberately in tournament fishing —
  he throws the big bait all day to upgrade the biggest fish in the limit
  while a partner runs the double rig (below) to fill out the smaller fish
  in the limit faster.

## Line, leader, and reading slack on the water

- **Braid** on every reel except when casting bare iron — roughly **50 lb**
  for general use, stepped down to **20–30 lb** for lighter bay
  presentations. He runs his braid dyed **yellow** on purpose (see below).
- **Fluorocarbon leader, always** — never fish braid straight to the bait.
  Stated reasons: fluoro is less visible and more abrasion-resistant on
  structure than braid or mono. His own back-to-back test — one bait on a
  double rig rigged with **25 lb mono**, the other with **25 lb
  fluorocarbon**, fished simultaneously — drew more bites on the
  fluorocarbon side.
- **Retire fluoro once it "goes furry"** rather than on a fixed interval —
  once the leader scuffs from fish or structure contact it stops passing
  light cleanly and loses the low-visibility advantage; cut back to fresh
  line whenever a fish roughs it up.
- **Read the slack line lying on the water, not just the rod tip.** Spooled
  to the lip on a spinning reel, the retrieve leaves a length of line lying
  flat on the surface between rod tip and bait; how that slack lies tells
  you the bait's state (dragging bottom, swimming clean, getting bit)
  before you feel anything through the rod. He fishes with a hand on the
  trigger/spool, ready to strike, and describes the tell often showing
  first as the slack jumping or snapping tight as a fish sucks the bait in
  from as much as three inches away — before there's a felt bite.
- **Yellow braid is a deliberate choice** to make that on-the-water slack
  read easy to see.

**A second, more detailed source on reading the sink (Chad, HookUp Baits
co-owner, `5Oh3IPIC5g4`, 2020):** independently teaches the same slack-on-
the-water read, with more mechanism:
- **~80% of bites come on the initial sink**, not the retrieve — "don't
  blink on the sink" is the stated rule; a bait that comes back chewed up
  with no felt bite is proof a fish ate and spit it on a sink you weren't
  watching (local slang for this: getting **"folded"** or **"faulted"**).
  Let the bait sink as freely as possible — more free sink means more of
  the glide/roll action that draws the bite.
- **Line-pop vocabulary:** as slack peels off a spinning spool on the sink,
  friction against the guides/spool produces small, ordinary pops. A **fish
  eating the bait produces a different pop** — described as coming from the
  opposite side/direction of a normal friction pop — that's the tell to
  engage the reel, come tight, and set. Distinguishing this "different pop"
  from ordinary line noise is presented as a learned skill that
  meaningfully raises hookup rate once an angler has it.
- Matches this note's yellow-braid/slack-reading doctrine above rather than
  adding a new claim — kept as a second, more mechanistic source for the
  same read.


## Weedless rig for grass and heavy kelp (Owner "sled" system)

- The stock hook on these tube baits is not weedless and fouls in grass or
  heavy kelp. Fix: an **Owner-brand weighted "sled" head**, slid into a
  small slit cut in the bait's belly at the point the hook would normally
  exit, so the hook rides flush with the bait's topline instead of hanging
  free — it comes through grass and kelp clean.
- Trade-off, stated directly: the sled/weedless rig **does not glide as
  well** as the stock rigging (less side-to-side action), but is worth it
  whenever grass or kelp fouls the standard rig. Credited for a personal-best
  spotted bay bass on camera.


## Double-bait dropper rig (bass, yellowtail, and rockfish to ~200 ft)

- A **3-way ball-bearing swivel** with a **wide-gap split ring** carries two
  tube baits at once — built originally for rock/structure fishing, also
  fished for yellowtail and bass.
- **Matched-weight pair, sized to the depth wanted** — most often two
  **1.5 oz** baits for the standard rock rig; he notes two **1 oz** baits get
  down easier than two 1.5 oz when 200 ft is hard to reach, so weight is
  picked for depth, not paired as two different weights on one rig; fished
  down to **200 ft** for rockfish.
- **Two different colors on the same drop, deliberately, as an A/B test** —
  on tournament days he and his partner run two colors on the double rig to
  read which the fish prefer that day, then both switch the boat over to
  the winner.
- **Minimum ~3 in offset between the two leader lengths** is the load-bearing
  spec: same-length leaders let the baits swim side by side and crash/tangle
  on the retrieve; staggering the lengths keeps them apart. (Distinct from
  the soft-plastic double rig in
  [swimbaits](swimbaits.md#double-swimbait-rig-nick-california-current),
  which stages a **12 in / 24 in** split for a small lead bait ahead of a
  bigger trailing bait — same anti-tangle principle, a different
  application: tube-bait rockfish/color-testing vs. soft-plastic
  follower-bait.)
- **Sacrificial short dropper below the main leader:** roughly **40 lb**
  fluorocarbon leader above the swivel, then a separate, shorter, lighter
  (**~25 lb**) dropper down to each bait — a bottom snag costs the one
  hung-up bait and its short dropper, not the whole rig.
- For rockfish specifically, paired with **3–4 oz** tube baits on rock
  structure; for yellowtail, hooking two fish at once on the double rig is
  described as "a handful" but a real, repeatable occurrence.

**A second, independently attributed double-rig source, with a different
line/weight breakdown (Chad, HookUp Baits co-owner, `5Oh3IPIC5g4`, 2020):**
his standing rock-fishing rig, unchanged trip to trip except for re-baiting
worn-out plastics — **80 lb braid main line → 60 lb fluorocarbon leader
above the swivel → two more fluorocarbon droppers off the swivel, 25 lb to
the smaller bait and 40 lb to the bigger bait.** A distinct three-tier line
breakdown from the "40 lb leader + ~25 lb sacrificial dropper" spec above —
kept side by side, not reconciled, as a different angler's own build for the
same rig style.
- **Same size for bass, size doesn't matter as much for deeper fish — a
  nuance not in the source above.** For shallower bass, he matches both
  baits to the **same size** so they sink and glide at the same rate side by
  side; for deeper structure/rockfish he says size matters less — "it just
  needs to be in their face" — and uses a bigger bait mainly to get down to
  the fish faster, contrasted with the matched-weight-pair framing logged
  above.
- **Leader tied above the swivel, not braid straight to it — the reason
  given:** braid tied directly to the swivel is too limp; with two baits
  sinking at different rates one bait's line can drift up and wrap around
  the rig above the swivel. A stiffer **40–60 lb fluorocarbon or mono**
  leader run above the swivel "bounces off" instead of wrapping, which he
  reports as far fewer foul-ups.
- **Swivel choice matters under load** — he describes losing rigs to
  swivels that bend or pull the barrel out of the frame under a 5–6 lb bass
  or a big red rockfish (brand not identified on camera); the swivel he
  settled on holds up. The two arms are also designed to **spin apart by
  hand** so both baits can be pulled off, re-baited, and dropped back down
  quickly without re-tying — "just like using one bait."
- **A third attributed variant — deliberate asymmetric weight for a
  "dancing" fall and jig action (Tackle Express, `tugP0UsrzRI`, "The Secret
  'Dancing' Lure Trick to Catch More Rockfish!," 2026-03-29; unregistered
  channel, single mention — medium confidence):** built on the same
  three-way-swivel double rig, but sized **unequal on purpose** rather than
  matched — a **3–6 oz** hookup bait on the bottom paired with a smaller
  **1–3 oz** hookup bait on top. Framed on camera as a step up in
  difficulty ("a little bit more advanced... step outside your comfort
  zone"), not the default build. Stated effect: the size mismatch gives the
  rig a **dancing action on the fall** to depth; once at the desired depth
  near bottom, popping the rod makes the two baits **dance, twitch, and go
  in between** each other — a different mechanism from, and not reconciled
  with, the matched-weight-pair-by-depth guidance above (that spec pairs
  equal weights to control sink rate/depth for two colors or two depths;
  this one deliberately mismatches weight to drive an action). No line
  class or leader spec given; the only species reference is "rockfish" in
  the video's title, not spoken in the 40-second clip itself.
- **Scope, stated as a decision with a reason, not a blanket rig:** built
  for **rockfish and structure/bass fishing**; explicitly **not** used for
  white seabass; yellowtail hookups on it "usually don't work out well"; and
  two large bonita on it at once is "too much... not worth it" — for
  yellowtail and tuna he fishes a single bait instead (see the yellowtail
  and skipjack/tuna sections below).


## Party boat rock-fishing technique (cast–drift–bounce)

- Framed as the technique for a **drifting** rock/structure party boat trip
  (as opposed to an anchored trip): fish from the **bow**. Treat it like
  fishing a river — **cast up-current/up-drift** ahead of the boat's drift,
  let the bait **bounce down** as the boat comes over the spot, then reel
  up and re-cast before the boat passes it.
- **Cast away from the boat's drift line, not down it** — casting where the
  drift line will end up puts the presentation under the hull; cast out and
  slightly ahead so the bait clears the boat as it bounces down.
- **Detect bottom without over-fishing the touch:** once the bait is
  bouncing down-current, open the bail (or free-spool) rather than holding
  it tight to bottom, then flip back into gear and take up slack — this
  keeps the bait bouncing along instead of planting and dragging flat,
  which is described as much harder to get bit than an actively bouncing
  bait.
- **Observed** (0HwYl0HO2tc, referencing an earlier trip aboard a boat named
  on camera as "Alvarado," an Anchor Chronicle-sponsored trip out of a
  landing captioned "Turner's" — landing name asr-uncertain, not
  independently confirmed): fishing this cast-drift-bounce technique with
  tube baits against a boat-wide field fishing live squid and dropper
  loops, the speaker's group reported **out-fishing the rest of the boat in
  numbers**, plus what the transcript states as "the sixth biggest fish on
  the boat" (ASR-garbled — exact meaning unclear, not read here as "the
  day's biggest fish"). A single self-reported data point from the
  product's own designer — sponsor/promotional context, not independently
  corroborated elsewhere in this corpus.


## Yellowtail: surface pop-retrieve and a half-yo-yo variant

- **On top:** cast beyond a visible push/boil, rod tip low, retrieve on a
  **crank-pop-crank-pop** cadence. Stated as **easier on a spinning reel
  than a baitcaster** — a spinning reel lets you pop the tip and take up
  slack in the same motion, where baitcast anglers tend to fall into a
  choppier pop-pop-pop-crank pattern.
- **Down, half-yo-yo variant:** when fish aren't showing on top, sink the
  tube bait to bottom, retrieve **about halfway** back up, open the bail
  and let it fall back to bottom a second time, then retrieve **the rest of
  the way** to the boat — roughly **half the speed** of working a bare iron
  yo-yo. Illustrated on a group trip to Cedros Island, Baja, fishing this
  cadence into a yellowtail push moving through. A distinct cadence from
  the crank-then-freespool and continuous-wind-to-the-top cadences already
  documented in [yo-yo iron](yo-yo-iron.md#mechanics) — kept here as a
  tube-bait-specific variant, not merged into that note.

**A second, corroborating pop-retrieve source (Chad, HookUp Baits co-owner,
`5Oh3IPIC5g4`, 2020), called "absolutely deadly" on yellowtail after 30
years fishing them:** cast, let the bait sink on roughly a **10 count**,
then retrieve rod-tip-down (tucked under the arm) on a **medium-speed
crank**, popping the rod tip through the retrieve ("bop bop") so the bait
kicks left-right at about the speed of a swimming baitfish — not a fast burn.
Matches the crank-pop cadence above from an independent source.
- **A follows-but-won't-commit fix, decision and reason stated on camera:**
  at the Coronado Islands, a crew getting repeated **follows with no takes**
  on the standard **1–1.5 oz "Big Game"/"Bullet"** bait switched to a
  smaller-profile **5/8 oz** bait on lighter gear and got immediate,
  committed eats. Read as bait profile/size overcoming the same follow-
  refuse problem also documented for [surface iron](surface-iron.md) —
  bait choice by cast-distance-vs-commitment trade-off: go **1–1.5 oz** for
  distance when fish are boat-shy or you need to cover water; drop to
  **5/8 oz** for a smaller profile once fish are showing but refusing.


## Trolling to locate yellowtail (Chad, HookUp Baits co-owner, `5Oh3IPIC5g4`)

- On a private boat, trolling the **1–1.5 oz "bullet"** bait tracks very
  straight and true with a **tight little wiggle** — described as **3–4 mph**
  as "the magic speed" for **locating** yellowtail (not the presentation once
  fish are found, which switches to casting). Positioned as a middle speed
  between two other trolled-search methods named on camera: slow-trolling
  live sardines at roughly **1 mph** (thorough but covers little ground) and
  a faster trolled lure at roughly **5 mph** (a different, unnamed lure —
  asr-uncertain, not confidently identified). See
  [trolling](trolling.md#socal-local-coast--trolling-hard-baits-to-prospect-a-zone-for-yellowtail)
  for the general prospect-a-zone method this sits alongside as a third,
  HookUp-bait-specific speed data point.
- **Single-hookup workflow, stated as a reason to skip the double rig for
  yellowtail:** because a hookup is one fish at a time on a single-bait
  troll, the other rod is kept rigged and in hand (not stowed) while the
  first fish is fought, so it can be **fired right back out immediately** to
  keep working the strike zone — read as staying "in the zone quicker"
  rather than losing search time re-rigging.


## Tuna: patties, boils, and punching through a skipjack layer (Chad, HookUp Baits co-owner, `5Oh3IPIC5g4`)

- **Checking a kelp paddy without spending live bait:** fire a **1 oz
  bullet** (mint or sardine) at the paddy, run along it a couple of times,
  and let a couple of casts **sink 20–30 ft**. A bite confirms fish; no
  bite on a couple of well-worked casts reads as "probably nothing on it" —
  move on and save the livewell for a confirmed bite. Complements, rather
  than replaces, the wake-up tricks (slow-trolled sardine, deck-hose spray,
  chunking) already documented for a quiet-but-loaded paddy in
  [dorado](../species/dorado.md).
- **Boil/foamer:** fire into the boil, give it a full **50 count** before
  the retrieve — the same "don't blink on the sink" principle as the
  bass/rockfish read above, extended to tuna: "even the tuna love to bite
  it on the sink."
- **Punching through a skipjack layer to reach yellowfin below — decision
  and reason stated on camera:** when skipjack blanket the surface over
  yellowfin holding underneath, live bait gets **intercepted by the
  skipjack** first; a **1 oz bullet worked fast** punches through to the
  yellowfin instead, reported as producing a hookup "every time" against
  livebait anglers who kept getting stopped by skipjack. See
  [skipjack tuna](../species/skipjack-tuna.md) and
  [yellowfin tuna](../species/yellowfin-tuna.md).
- Chad separately notes he doesn't fish tuna often, finding them far less
  predictable than bass/yellowtail/sheephead ("tuna are such a weird
  fish") — his tuna comments above are occasional, not a primary program.


## Common failures

- **Stiff (non-parabolic) rod on a big tube bait** — the failure mode
  called out repeatedly: the fish gets leverage on a stiff mid-section and
  throws the hook.
- **Same-length leaders on the double-bait dropper rig** — the baits swim
  together and tangle; stagger the lengths by several inches.
- **Letting the drift-bounce bait plant dead on the bottom** — stops
  looking like a fleeing/feeding baitfish and stops getting bit; keep it
  bouncing.
