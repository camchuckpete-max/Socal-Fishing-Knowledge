# Tube-Bait Fishing

Execution for [tube baits](../lures/tube-baits.md) — the soft-plastic,
internal-lead-head class HookUp Baits made a SoCal staple — fished from a
private boat, party boat, or bay bank for calico and sand bass, yellowtail,
rockfish, halibut, and (occasionally) tuna. Tube baits are a distinct
soft-plastic class from [glide baits](glide-baits.md), a jointed hard-bait
class this note's content previously sat under in error (cameron). The bait
class, construction, colors, and scent system live in
[tube baits](../lures/tube-baits.md); this note owns how to fish them.

## Reach for this when

- Working the same kelp/reef edges and bay structure as
  [swimbaits](swimbaits.md), and a bait that glides on the pause over a rock
  pile or structure edge (rather than tracking straight back) is wanted.
- Fishing a **drifting** party-boat rock-cod trip and a cast-and-drift
  presentation is wanted instead of straight-down bait — see the drift
  technique below.
- Deep structure to roughly 200 ft, to A/B two colors or fish two
  depths/weights at once off one dropper rig.
- Prospecting a kelp paddy for tuna/dorado without spending live bait, or
  punching a bait through a surface skipjack layer to reach yellowfin
  holding below it — see the tuna section below.
- Red-crab-holding structure for [sheephead](../species/sheephead.md) and
  resident bass, or sizing a bait for
  [California halibut](../species/california-halibut.md) — color and
  species detail live in [tube baits](../lures/tube-baits.md).

## Rod: parabolic bend for big baits

The rod needs to **bend all the way through the blank ("parabolic")**, not
flex at the tip and stiffen through the mid-section (`0HwYl0HO2tc`). A hooked
fish gets leverage against the extra weight of a 3–4 oz bait hanging beside
its face; a stiff mid-section gives that leverage something to work against
and the hook pulls free almost every time, where a full-length soft bend
absorbs it and keeps the hook planted (`0HwYl0HO2tc`). The effect scales with
bait size — worst on **3–4 oz** baits, more forgiving on **¼ oz** baits
(`0HwYl0HO2tc`).

Line class by bait size: **¼ oz** pairs with a rod rated roughly **8–17 lb**;
**¼–⅝ oz** steps up to **10–25 lb**; **3–4 oz** tube baits go on **40 lb-class
or heavier** — do not fish the big tube baits under 40 lb class, after losing
fish on 25 lb gear (`0HwYl0HO2tc`). Length: **8 ft** is preferred, valued for
casting distance and for keeping the tip working through a long parabolic
bend (`0HwYl0HO2tc`). ⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-by-source): `0HwYl0HO2tc`
states the 8 ft preference but gives no reason there; the casting-distance/
tip-loading rationale is stated in `5Oh3IPIC5g4` instead.

A second, independent source corroborates the 40 lb floor two years later:
fishing a **3 oz** bait on a 9 ft medium-heavy spinning rod, "you don't
want... to throw the big baits on less than 40 pounds, you're gonna get your
heart broken" (`5Oh3IPIC5g4`) — line was **50 lb "Max Quatro"**-brand braid
(product name as heard on camera, not independently verified) to a **40 lb
fluorocarbon** leader.

**"Big bait, big fish" is a deliberate size filter, not just a leverage
choice.** Fishing the 3 oz bait all day selects for bigger fish — sand bass
and calico under about 3 lb (below the SoCal average) largely stop biting it
— and produces roughly double the average size against anglers throwing
smaller baits nearby (`5Oh3IPIC5g4`). ⚠ Fact-check (single-source): the
"roughly double the average size" figure rests on this one speaker's own
comparison, uncorroborated elsewhere in the note's citations. Applied in
tournament fishing: throw
the big bait all day to upgrade the biggest fish in the limit while a
partner runs the
[double rig](#double-bait-dropper-rig-bass-yellowtail-and-rockfish-to-200-ft)
to fill out the smaller fish in the limit faster (`5Oh3IPIC5g4`).

## Line, leader, and reading the sink

Braid on every reel except when casting bare iron — roughly **50 lb** for
general use, stepped down to **20–30 lb** for lighter bay presentations, run
dyed **yellow** on purpose to make the on-water slack read easy to see
(`0HwYl0HO2tc`). Fluorocarbon leader always — never fish braid straight to
the bait: fluoro is less visible and more abrasion-resistant against
structure than braid or mono. A back-to-back test — one bait on a double rig
with 25 lb mono, the other with 25 lb fluorocarbon, fished simultaneously —
drew more bites on the fluorocarbon side (`0HwYl0HO2tc`). Retire fluoro once
it "goes furry" from fish or structure contact rather than on a fixed
interval — a scuffed leader stops passing light cleanly and loses the
low-visibility advantage (`0HwYl0HO2tc`).

**Read the slack line lying on the water, not just the rod tip.** Spooled to
the lip on a spinning reel, the retrieve leaves slack lying flat on the
surface between rod tip and bait; how it lies tells the bait's state
(dragging bottom, swimming clean, getting bit) before anything is felt
through the rod. Fish with a hand on the trigger/spool, ready to strike — the
tell often shows first as the slack jumping or snapping tight as a fish
sucks the bait in from as much as three inches away, before there's a felt
bite (`0HwYl0HO2tc`).

A second, independently corroborating source teaches the same slack-reading
skill with more mechanism (`5Oh3IPIC5g4`): roughly **80% of bites come on the
initial sink**, not the retrieve — "don't blink on the sink." ⚠ Fact-check
(single-source): the specific **80%** figure rests on this one speaker's own
estimate; the corroboration from `0HwYl0HO2tc` supports sink-watching as a
technique but gives no percentage. A bait that
comes back chewed up with no felt bite means a fish ate and spit it on a sink
that wasn't watched (local slang: getting "folded" or "faulted"). Let the
bait sink as freely as possible — more free sink means more of the
glide/roll action that draws the bite. As slack peels off a spinning spool on
the sink, friction against the guides/spool produces small, ordinary pops; a
fish eating the bait produces a distinguishable pop from the opposite
side/direction — the tell to engage the reel, come tight, and set.
Distinguishing that pop from ordinary line noise is a learned skill that
meaningfully raises hookup rate once it's acquired.

## Weedless rig for grass and heavy kelp (Owner "sled" system)

The stock hook on these tube baits is not weedless and fouls in grass or
heavy kelp. Fix: an **Owner-brand weighted "sled" head**, slid into a small
slit cut in the bait's belly at the point the hook would normally exit, so
the hook rides flush with the bait's topline instead of hanging free — it
comes through grass and kelp clean (`0HwYl0HO2tc`). Trade-off: the
sled/weedless rig **does not glide as well** as the stock rigging (less
side-to-side action), but is worth it whenever grass or kelp fouls the
standard rig (`0HwYl0HO2tc`).

⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-internal): [calico
bass](../species/calico-bass.md#situations--techniques) routes to this section
off the situation "grass or heavy kelp fouls a standard **weedless** rig,"
which inverts the premise stated here — the rig that fouls is this bait's
stock, *non*-weedless hook, and the sled is the fix that makes the tube bait
weedless in the first place, at a stated cost in glide.
[Swimbaits](swimbaits.md), the note that owns the weedless keel-weighted rig,
records it running clean through heavy kelp with no fouling (`_r_qKX_7080`),
so the router's trigger condition is documented in neither note.

## Double-bait dropper rig (bass, yellowtail, and rockfish to ~200 ft)

A **3-way ball-bearing swivel** with a **wide-gap split ring** carries two
tube baits at once — built for rock/structure fishing, also fished for
yellowtail and bass (`0HwYl0HO2tc`). Weight is a matched pair, sized to the
depth wanted rather than paired as two different weights on one rig: most
often two **1.5 oz** baits for the standard rock rig, stepping to two **1
oz** baits when 200 ft is hard to reach — fished down to **200 ft** for
rockfish (`0HwYl0HO2tc`). Two different colors on the same drop, deliberately,
as an A/B test: on tournament days, run two colors on the double rig to read
which the fish prefer, then switch the boat to the winner (`0HwYl0HO2tc`).
**Minimum ~3 in offset between the two leader lengths** is the load-bearing
spec — same-length leaders let the baits swim side by side and crash/tangle
on the retrieve (`0HwYl0HO2tc`); this is a distinct anti-tangle spacing from
the **12 in / 24 in** split used in the [soft-plastic double swimbait
rig](swimbaits.md#double-swimbait-rig-nick-california-current), a different
application (tube-bait rockfish/color-testing vs. soft-plastic
follower-bait). A sacrificial short dropper protects the rig: roughly **40
lb** fluorocarbon leader above the swivel, then a separate, shorter, lighter
(**~25 lb**) dropper down to each bait, so a bottom snag costs only the one
hung-up bait and its short dropper (`0HwYl0HO2tc`). For rockfish, pair **3–4
oz** baits on rock structure; for yellowtail, a double hookup on this rig is
"a handful" but real and repeatable (`0HwYl0HO2tc`). ⚠ Fact-check
(contradicted-by-source): `0HwYl0HO2tc`'s own double-dropper-rig spec for
rockfish (above) pairs two 1.5 oz baits, stepping to two 1 oz when 200 ft is
hard to reach — not 3–4 oz; the 3–4 oz figures in that transcript describe
single baits fished on 40 lb-class gear (including an out-of-region British
Columbia lingcod/yelloweye-rockfish trip), not this double rig.

A second, independently attributed build runs a different three-tier line
breakdown, kept side by side rather than reconciled: **80 lb braid main line
→ 60 lb fluorocarbon leader above the swivel → two more fluorocarbon droppers
off the swivel, 25 lb to the smaller bait and 40 lb to the bigger bait**,
unchanged trip to trip except for re-baiting worn-out plastics (`5Oh3IPIC5g4`).
For shallower bass, both baits match the **same size** so they sink and
glide at the same rate; for deeper structure/rockfish, size matters less —
"it just needs to be in their face" — and a bigger bait is used mainly to
reach the fish faster (`5Oh3IPIC5g4`). Leader is tied above the swivel
rather than running braid straight to it, because limp braid lets one
bait's line drift up and wrap around the rig as the two baits sink at
different rates; a stiffer **40–60 lb fluorocarbon or mono** leader "bounces
off" instead (`5Oh3IPIC5g4`). Swivel choice matters under load — a swivel
that bends or pulls its barrel out of the frame loses rigs to a 5–6 lb bass
or a big red rockfish; the arms should also spin apart by hand so both baits
can be re-baited and dropped back down without re-tying — "just like using
one bait" (`5Oh3IPIC5g4`).

A third, unequal-weight variant drives a different fall action rather than
testing colors or depths: the same three-way-swivel double rig sized
**unequal on purpose** — a **3–6 oz** bait on the bottom paired with a
smaller **1–3 oz** bait on top — gives a **dancing action on the fall**, and
popping the rod once at depth makes the two baits dance, twitch, and go in
between each other. Framed on camera as a step up in difficulty ("a little
bit more advanced... step outside your comfort zone"), not the default build
(Tackle Express, `tugP0UsrzRI`; unregistered channel, single mention — medium
confidence). No line class or leader spec is given, and the only species
reference is "rockfish" in the video's title.

**Scope, stated as a decision with a reason, not a blanket rig:** built for
rockfish and structure/bass fishing; explicitly not used for white seabass;
yellowtail hookups on it "usually don't work out well"; two large bonita on
it at once is "too much" — for yellowtail and tuna, a single bait is fished
instead (`5Oh3IPIC5g4`).

## Party boat rock-fishing (cast–drift–bounce)

The technique for a **drifting** rock/structure party-boat trip (as opposed
to an anchored trip): fish from the **bow**, treating it like fishing a
river — **cast up-current/up-drift** ahead of the boat's drift, let the bait
**bounce down** as the boat comes over the spot, then reel up and re-cast
before the boat passes it (`0HwYl0HO2tc`). Cast away from the boat's drift
line, not down it — a cast placed where the drift line will end up puts the
presentation under the hull; cast out and slightly ahead so the bait clears
the boat as it bounces down (`0HwYl0HO2tc`). Detect bottom without
over-fishing the touch: once the bait is bouncing down-current, open the
bail (or free-spool) rather than holding it tight to bottom, then flip back
into gear and take up slack — this keeps the bait bouncing instead of
planting and dragging flat, which is much harder to get bit than an actively
bouncing bait (`0HwYl0HO2tc`).

One party-boat session fishing this technique reported out-fishing a
boat-wide field of live-squid anglers; full account in the
[evidence file](evidence/tube-bait-fishing.md) (`0HwYl0HO2tc`).

## Tuna: patties, boils, and punching through a skipjack layer

**Checking a kelp paddy without spending live bait:** fire a **1 oz bullet**
(mint or sardine) at the paddy, run along it a couple of times, and let a
couple of casts **sink 20–30 ft** (`5Oh3IPIC5g4`). A bite confirms fish; no
bite on a couple of well-worked casts reads as "probably nothing on it" —
move on and save the livewell for a confirmed bite (`5Oh3IPIC5g4`). This
complements, rather than replaces, the wake-up tricks (slow-trolled sardine,
deck-hose spray, chunking) already documented for a quiet-but-loaded paddy
in [dorado](../species/dorado.md).
⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-internal):
[dorado](../species/dorado.md#situations--techniques) routes the same
check-cast to this section off the same cite and the same 1 oz size, but names
the colour as **pink** — "a pink ('Dorado') colour, 1 oz or 5/8 oz… a named
dorado favourite at paddies" (`5Oh3IPIC5g4`) — against **mint or sardine**
here. The two notes hand a reader different colours for one action. The router
side already carries a contradicted-by-source flag establishing that "Dorado"
in that transcript is the fish, not a colour name, and that the speaker's
named colours include mint and sardine as well as pink; this reciprocal flag
makes the split visible from this side too.

**Boil/foamer:** fire into the boil and give it a full **50 count** before
the retrieve — the same "don't blink on the sink" principle as the
bass/rockfish read above, extended to tuna: "even the tuna love to bite it
on the sink" (`5Oh3IPIC5g4`).

**Punching through a skipjack layer to reach yellowfin below:** when
skipjack blanket the surface over yellowfin holding underneath, live bait
gets intercepted by the skipjack first; a **1 oz bullet worked fast** punches
through to the yellowfin instead, reportedly producing a hookup "every time"
against livebait anglers who kept getting stopped by skipjack (`5Oh3IPIC5g4`).
See [skipjack tuna](../species/skipjack-tuna.md) and
[yellowfin tuna](../species/yellowfin-tuna.md).

Tuna are fished occasionally rather than as a primary program by this
source, who finds them far less predictable than bass/yellowtail/sheephead —
"tuna are such a weird fish" (`5Oh3IPIC5g4`).

## Gear class

**Bass casting / swimbait class**, stepping up to a heavy **40 lb-class**

## Common failures

- **Stiff (non-parabolic) rod on a big tube bait** — the fish gets leverage
  on a stiff mid-section and throws the hook.
- **Same-length leaders on the double-bait dropper rig** — the baits swim
  together and tangle; stagger the lengths by several inches.
- **Letting the drift-bounce bait plant dead on the bottom** — stops looking
  like a fleeing/feeding baitfish and stops getting bit; keep it bouncing.
