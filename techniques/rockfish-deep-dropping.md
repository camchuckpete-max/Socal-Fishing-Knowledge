# Rockfish Deep-Dropping

Put a bait or jig on hard bottom in deep water, hold the zone against the drift,
and keep off the snag. This note owns the execution — bait rigs, jig cadence,
weight, and reading the meter; the reverse map of species is in **Linked from**.
For setting up and managing the drift itself at depths too deep to anchor
(reading wind vs. current, motor-as-rudder, drift socks), see
[search and glassing — too deep to anchor](../planning/search-and-glassing.md#anchoring-geometry).

## Reach for this when

- [Rockfish and lingcod](../species/rockfish-lingcod.md) on deep structure —
  reds, bocaccio (salmon grouper), coppers, lings, whitefish, and the Channel
  Islands variety (blue bass, johnny bass, sheephead) that come with them.
- You want harvest fish off a marked hard-bottom spot without carrying bait —
  a rolled-up gangion and a few sinkers live in the rod locker, ready any trip.
- For a sportier presentation on the same fish, see
  [slow-pitch jigging](slow-pitch-jigging.md).

## Rod, reel & line for the feel

Fishing 180–200 ft (a 35-fathom/210 ft spot in one worked example), the whole
setup is built around **feeling the bite from depth** (`_ZThckj2TIM`):

- **Rod material — graphite or graphite-composite, not all-glass.** A
  graphite/composite blank transmits a deep bite; an all-glass rod bends
  along its whole length and you can't feel it (`_ZThckj2TIM`). This is the
  opposite preference from [flylining a live
  bait](../tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md), where a soft glass/composite
  tip is wanted for a gentle cast — a different job, not a contradiction.
- **Reel — bigger body beats a smaller reel at the same gear ratio.** A
  larger-bodied reel carries more cranking torque than a smaller reel geared
  the same (both 6:1 in the comparison); the smaller reel is noticeably
  harder to crank a heavy rockfish up from 200 ft (`_ZThckj2TIM`).
- **A two-speed reel earns its keep specifically for fatigue, not torque
  alone.** Cranking a heavy fish up from 200+ ft in high gear all day is
  tiring on the hands and back; shifting to low gear later in the day winches
  the fish up slower but with much less effort (`OdRZIDBO6Mg`). Pair it with
  a **shorter rod, 7–7'6", medium-heavy or heavy** — a longer rod and a
  two-speed reel "doesn't make a whole lot of sense" together (`OdRZIDBO6Mg`).
  General selection logic for when to reach for a two-speed at all is in
  [star drag vs. lever
  drag](../tackle/star-drag-vs-lever-drag.md#when-to-reach-for-a-two-speed-at-all--deep-bottom-fishing-vertical-work-and-rod-length-tackle-express).
- **Rod length — leverage and rail-hauling, not just fatigue.** Framed for
  rock fishing generally (heavy weights, large sinkers, fishing deep), the
  same **7 ft–7 ft 6 in range, generally nothing longer**, hedging up to an
  8 ft ceiling at most, for two more reasons: (1) **leverage** — a longer rod
  gives a fish more leverage over the angler fighting it; (2) **rail-hauling
  reach** — once the fish is fought alongside the boat, a longer rod holds it
  farther from the rail, so the angler has to reach farther out to grab and
  haul it over the side (`eLwier3zVdo`).
- **A named product just past that range:** the [Phenix Axis, 7 ft 8 in heavy
  action rated 25–60 lb
  test](../tackle/tackle-express-phenix-axis-rockfish-rod.md), pitched as a
  mid-tier rockfishing rod (`rBt096uru3U`).
- **Conventional beats spinning for deep vertical work — a guide-position
  mechanism.** On a conventional rod the reel sits on top and the line runs
  off the top of the blank, so cranking up from depth (an example of 700
  vertical ft) means resting the reel handle on the rail and winding. On a
  spinning rod the reel hangs underneath, so the guides sit on the bottom of
  the blank and the **line** is what touches the rail first on a long
  vertical retrieve — line dragging against the rail over hundreds of feet of
  cranking is much more likely to abrade, damage, or break, costing the fish
  (`qh3hL2Dt3HY`, `zLN0v-gWpeI`).
- **Line — mostly braid, short mono top shot (~25 yd or less).** Braid
  doesn't stretch, so the rig stays taut to the bottom through wind and
  drift; an all-mono line stretches/bellies in the current and drags the
  sinker off bottom. Keep a short mono top shot anyway because mono is
  easier to cinch tight when tying [dropper loops](dropper-loop.md) — the
  point is short top shot, not no top shot (`_ZThckj2TIM`).
- **Line class by reel size, for a rock-cod quick gangion at 400–600 ft:**
  **40 lb braid** on a smaller reel, **65 lb braid** on a bigger reel — a
  heavier pairing than the 30 lb figure below in [Weight & staying off the
  snag](#weight--staying-off-the-snag), since it's a different rig style
  (bait gangion at a deeper band, not a light jig). Fill the reel to the rim
  with braid — a reel that isn't spooled full reads "out of line" once you're
  fishing, and every inch of capacity matters for the drop and the fight. 40
  lb braid is already hard to break by hand, so a break-off on 40 lb reads as
  a genuine snag, not an undersized-line problem (`zVIfArUrpDI`).
- **Why braid over mono at all at these depths (mechanism):** monofilament
  "has a tendency to want to float," so at 400–600 ft it won't get a bait
  straight down to the fish fast enough — and the spots fished are often very
  small, so a slow, scoped drop can miss the zone on the drift entirely
  (`zVIfArUrpDI`). See [line & leader](../tackle/line-and-leader.md#when-braid--and-how-heavy)
  for the general braid-vs-mono buoyancy breakdown this sits alongside.

## Rigs — three ways to fish the bottom

- **Dropper-loop gangion** (bait/Gulp): 40 lb main line, **two dropper loops
  ~1.5 ft apart**, a small swivel at each end so it rolls up clean. Add **6/0
  Gamakatsu octopus circle** hooks looped on, then thread a **5 in Gulp grub**
  (nuclear chicken / gold) onto each like a swimbait — the tail wafts and the
  scent fishes as well as live bait even after the tail's chewed off. See the
  [dropper-loop](dropper-loop.md) note for tying the loops. Below the bottom
  hook, tie **~1 ft of lighter 12–15 lb line** to the sinker as a breakaway
  (`O5aQkex0qGg`).
- **Shrimp fly / bait gangion:** same idea with tied flies over squid or Gulp
  when you want to sort species or the bite wants a smaller offering
  (`IxfTRWmMqQU`). ⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-by-source): the cited clip only
  compares jig-head weight against torpedo-sinker weight and braid diameter;
  it never mentions squid, Gulp, tied flies, or sorting species
  (`IxfTRWmMqQU`).
- **Leadhead + plastic (jigging):** a 3 oz leadhead with a swimbait/grub for
  shallower deep-drops (100–300 ft, `O5aQkex0qGg`), or an 8 oz jig head + 8 in
  Gulp grub for the bigger reds (`AqW_Z9pFcHU`). ⚠ Fact-check
  (contradicted-by-source): the cited clip credits this combo with catching
  "the bigger rock fish" generally, not reds specifically (`AqW_Z9pFcHU`). A
  free-swinging head fishes
  suspended fish; a fixed head lets you drop through a red already limited-out
  without hooking it, then sink the rest of the way to a copper or ling on the
  bottom (`O5aQkex0qGg`).

## Weight & staying off the snag

- **Thin braid, not heavy** — **30 lb braid** (a fathom-400 / squall-20
  level-wind holds plenty for 600–900 ft) sinks straight with far less scope
  than the old 80 lb Dacron; you can hold bottom in 300 ft with a 3 oz jig
  head. A **24 oz torpedo sinker** gets a bait gangion down and holds it — no
  need to carry a range of weights (`O5aQkex0qGg`). The same mechanism scales
  a whole outfit down: a jigging setup running **200 g (~7 oz) or less in
  waters up to 600–700 ft** needs only 30 lb or less braid diameter, because
  the thicker the braid, the more drag in the water and the more weight it
  takes to reach depth — against a traditional bait rig for the same fish,
  which runs a 16 oz torpedo sinker, two hooks, and shrimp flies on roughly
  65 lb braid, with a jig roughly a third that sinker's weight (~5.3 oz) on a
  reel half the size (`IxfTRWmMqQU`).
- **Breakaway sinker** (the light dropper above) means a snagged sinker
  breaks off alone and you keep the gangion, hooks, and fish (`O5aQkex0qGg`).
- On a jig, keep **light thumb tension on the spool** during the drop so the
  jig falls straight and fast rather than scoping away; ease off near the
  bottom to let it flutter into the zone (`O5aQkex0qGg`).

**Sinker weight scales with depth.** SoCal rockfishing covers a wide range,
on average roughly 300–600 ft (captioned "3–600 ft," read as a dropped-digit
rendering — asr-uncertain), shallower or deeper depending on conditions
(`S9sQ8Vg8-5o`):

The lighter jig-head figures (3 oz at 300 ft) assume a light-thumb-tension jig
on thin braid, not a torpedo-sinker gangion — see Rigs above for the two rig

**28 oz** torpedo (captioned "size four" for the smallest — rendered here as
oz to match the surrounding 6–8/16–20/28 oz figures from the same clip;
asr-uncertain) (`S9sQ8Vg8-5o`). Heavy current or wind on a 300–600+ ft
two-hook rig means cranking up two open-mouthed fish (a worked example: 750
ft of line) against the added "water tension" of both mouths dragging through
the column — in high gear that's an estimated **nearly 3 ft retrieved per
crank**, enough to tire out even a high-energy angler over a day of repeated
drops; shifting to low gear turns the reel into a winch — less line per
crank, more torque, less effort, which is what lets repeated drops continue
through the day rather than a fatigue-forced break (`S9sQ8Vg8-5o`,
`Qj6brwOJk9E`).

- **Weak link at the dropper-loop knot, not just a breakaway leader** — a
  second, contrasting way to sacrifice minimum gear on a snag: fish **30–40
  lb mainline** (25–30 lb if you're not strong enough to break 40 lb by hand)
  straight through the [double dropper
  loop](dropper-loop.md#weak-link-at-the-bottom-dropper-loop-scotty), no
  separate light breakaway section. When the sinker hangs up, lock the reel
  down (not free-spool) and let the boat's drift load the line until it
  parts — it normally **breaks at the bottom dropper loop's tag point**
  (nearest the sinker), not at the sinker knot or up the mainline, because the
  long loaded section stretches (mono stretch) while the short tag at the
  loop takes the shock. Recovers the gangion and hooks above the break; only
  the sinker (and sometimes not even that) stays on the bottom (`_ZThckj2TIM`).
- **Twist swivel above the sinker, tied light on purpose.** Run a small twist
  swivel just above the sinker via a lightly-tied **San Diego jam knot — see
  the [deliberately weak
  variant](../rigging/san-diego-jam-knot.md#deliberately-weak-variant-for-a-weight-connection-scotty)**
  — it won't stop all line twist but cuts it down. **Clearing twist after a
  fish:** a fish cranked up from ~200 ft spirals the whole way up and loads
  the line with twist; hold the rig at the knot (above the swivel) and let
  the weight hang free below — the swivel spins the twist back out before you
  fish it again (`_ZThckj2TIM`).
- **Moving spot to spot:** wrap the sinker's tag line **4–5 tight wraps**
  around the rubber of the reel handle (hook left off) to stow the rig
  without a hook swinging loose while running between spots (`_ZThckj2TIM`).

## Jig cadence

- Drop to the bottom, put the reel in gear, **wind 1–3 turns up out of the
  rocks**, then work **big sweeps of the rod, letting the jig flutter back
  down**, keeping bottom contact. On a slack-line drop, most bites come **on
  the first fall or right when you kick it back into gear** off the bottom
  (`O5aQkex0qGg`). ⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-by-source): the cited clip gives
  the jig cadence as "a wind or two" (one to two turns); the two-to-three-turn
  figure in the same clip is stated for the bait-gangion cadence, not the jig
  (`O5aQkex0qGg`).
- These fish chase up — reds and lings will come **30 ft off the bottom** to
  eat. Bring the jig as far as ~30 ft up, then flutter it back down. On a
  short-biting ling, drop straight back to the bottom and start hopping
  again — they'll chase it back down and re-eat (`O5aQkex0qGg`).
- The same lift-then-flutter rhythm is named on camera as **"Lift & Drop"**:
  drop to the bottom, take one or two turns up, then lift the rod and let the
  jig sink all the way back down, repeating — the flutter-and-dart on the
  fall is the trigger. Pick this for **isolated rocks**, to draw a big
  rockfish up out of the structure (`S9sQ8Vg8-5o`, `p9YYIb4GKHM`).

## Wind-retrieve skirted bait — a fourth presentation

A presentation distinct from the three rigs above: a skirted bait wound on a
**slow, steady retrieve** instead of jigged or soaked on a dropper loop, an
idea carried over from bass fishing that has reportedly been fished in Japan
and Australia for 20+ years (`iAr6sbRC384`). The bait's product name renders
inconsistently in auto-captions (roughly "wrap"/"world"/"walkover," plausibly
a Daiwa product) and is not carried as a named product (asr-uncertain). ⚠
Fact-check (single-source): this entire presentation — the rig, the rod/reel/
line system below, the retrieve, the colors, and the bite read — rests on one
2020 seminar clip, with no independent corpus corroboration
(`iAr6sbRC384`).

- **Tie point changes the action.** Tying the leader to the bait at the
  nose/tip gives a straighter winding action; tying mid-body gives a jerkier
  action. Tying from the tip on a slow, steady retrieve works best, on both
  sport boats and private boats (`iAr6sbRC384`).
- **Retrieve and the "steal it" trigger.** Drop to bottom, then wind slowly
  and steadily — roughly 10 cranks as a working cadence. A light tap-tap on
  the retrieve is usually a small fish nipping the skirt without being able
  to eat the whole bait; a **bigger predator fish moves in to take the bait
  away from the smaller fish**, and that's when the eat happens — so the
  bait is kept working through a light tap rather than reeled away from it.
  This presentation covers both bottom fish and fish suspended off the
  bottom on the same retrieve (`iAr6sbRC384`).
- **Sport-boat drop etiquette.** When the whole rail drops together on an
  anchored stop, the fish right under the boat get picked off fast by
  whoever's gear lands first; working the bait **20–30–50 ft** out from the
  boat on the slow retrieve avoids competing for the same fish everyone else
  is already on. ⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-by-source): the cited clip's
  "away from everybody" line is given for walking to the bow to fish, and the
  20–30–50 ft distance is given separately, in the context of covering
  suspended fish over red crab — the note combines two distinct statements
  from the same clip into one avoid-competition rationale (`iAr6sbRC384`).
  Rockfish suspending higher over structure than in past years
  is tied on camera to **red crab** concentrations in the water column above
  these spots, as observed in the years before 2020-03-16 (`iAr6sbRC384`).
- **Colors.** Three patterns used most: a **red-crab pattern** (the pick when
  red crab is present, called a strong producer), a **dark pattern**, and a
  **baitfish pattern** — the last flagged as almost too effective, drawing
  bycatch (mackerel, bonito) along with target rockfish and bocaccio ("salmon
  grouper") on the way through the column (`iAr6sbRC384`).
- **Bite/hookset — don't swing.** A tap-tap on this retrieve should not be
  met with a hookset; keep winding steadily. The bait's **long-shank hook**
  is meant to pin the fish in the corner of the mouth as it works rather than
  needing an active strike (`iAr6sbRC384`).
- **Trailer fix for a chewed bait.** When the skirt gets cut or chewed up by
  small fish, a soft-plastic trailer threaded onto the hook shank (slid into
  place and re-seated) is a field fix to keep the bait fishing instead of
  shelving it (`iAr6sbRC384`).

### Rod, weight, and reel for this system

- **Rod — a purpose-built soft/parabolic blank ("Elevator" rod, Daiwa).**
  Composite blank, fast tip but very limber through the rest of the blank —
  the deep, even bend acts as a shock absorber: even a 20–30 lb lingcod loads
  the blank rather than transmitting a hard shock to the hook, which reduces
  pulled hooks on big fish. Comes in three power classes (medium,
  medium-heavy, heavy); medium-heavy and heavy fish deepest, to **450 ft**
  (`iAr6sbRC384`).
- **Weight — deliberately light for the depth.** As light as **4 oz even in
  ~300 ft** of water, accepting a slower drop (roughly 10 seconds slower to
  reach bottom) in exchange for more bites. The system's weight options top
  out around **7 oz** for heavier conditions/deeper water (`iAr6sbRC384`).
- **Reel — baitcaster, deliberately low/slow gear ratio.** Paired with a
  level-wind baitcasting reel; modern braid capacity removes the need for a
  large 30/40/50-size conventional reel at this depth. A **lower gear
  ratio — on the order of 6:1 to 6.5:1** — matters because a hooked rockfish
  cranked up fast on this retrieve spins in a circle on the way up, and
  winding too fast can spin the fish right off the hook. This is a different
  reason for a low gear ratio than [flylining live
  bait](../tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md#flyline-reel--rod-selection),
  where the low ratio is for feeling a finicky bite rather than stopping a
  spinning fish from throwing the hook (`iAr6sbRC384`).
- **Line/leader.** 40–50 lb braid (40 lb favored) main line; 2.5–3 ft leader
  in 30–40 lb mono — no fluorocarbon needed for this presentation
  (`iAr6sbRC384`).

### A second example of the same nip-triggers-the-bigger-fish pattern

A small **shrimp-pattern fly or bucktail teaser** run above a soft-plastic
jig on a dropper-style rig works the same idea: the teaser mimics a shrimp or
small baitfish that a predator fish is trying to eat, and the visual of
something already being chased draws bigger strikes than the plain jig
fished alone — the same underlying trigger as the tap-tap/steal-it read
above, applied to a jig rig instead of the wind-retrieve bait (`iAr6sbRC384`).

## Reading the meter (bottom discrimination)

- Approach the spot on the chartplotter, flip on the sonar, and idle onto the
  structure — many spots are a **distinct hump coming up 20–30 ft** off the
  surrounding bottom. Drop your autopilot on it and mark a **waypoint**, then
  work the **track line** back and forth (`11npFUPOJKU`). ⚠ Fact-check
  (contradicted-by-source): the cited clip describes the 20–30 ft hump height
  for the one example spot being worked on camera, not a "many spots"
  generalization (`11npFUPOJKU`).
- Run a **dual-frequency split screen.** **Low frequency** paints everything
  and runs gainy — good for the bait cloud sitting up over the hump. **High
  frequency** distinguishes the game fish: **arches over hard bottom** are
  rockfish. Fish the hard-bottom "highways" where you see arches, not the
  open bait cloud (`11npFUPOJKU`). See [electronics and
  sounder](../planning/electronics-and-sounder.md) for the general method.
  **At depth, make sure the low-frequency channel is on** — **50 kHz**
  specifically at 180–200 ft, because low frequency penetrates to depth
  better than high frequency, which is exactly why the dual-frequency split
  pairs a low-frequency channel for reach with a high-frequency channel for
  fish-arch detail (`_ZThckj2TIM`).
- Islands note: current matters as much as bait — a spot with squid but no
  [current](../conditions/current-diagnostics.md) fishes soft; combine a
  dropper loop with a [sliding sinker](sliding-sinker.md) to cover the whole
  column when marks are scattered (`6-8KfjEg0x8`).
- **Driving onto a tight numbered spot.** Run to the waypoint on the chart
  plotter and watch the plotter's **distance-to-go** readout count down on
  the approach — shut the sounder off on the run in and fish the plotter
  number instead. When distance-to-go reads **0.00**, kill forward way (stop
  the boat, put it in reverse) and drop immediately: many of the numbered
  rock spots are small — "not even as big as a stage" — so a late drop misses
  the structure entirely (`AH4Eiya1Hn0`).
- **Diagnose a miss off the track line.** If the gear wasn't ready and the
  line went in late (an example: already reading 0.01 by the time the sinker
  hit the water), that overshoot is enough to land off the spot and produce
  sand dabs instead of rockfish; comparing the boat's recorded track line
  against the waypoint after the drift shows the overshoot so it can be
  corrected on the next pass (`AH4Eiya1Hn0`).
- **At extreme depth, read "fuzz," not individual arches.** At 300–500+ ft,
  running the contour looking for rock, the target picture is a hard-bottom
  return coming up topped with "fuzz," not the clean boomerang-shaped arches
  read at shallower depth — individual fish stop resolving as distinct arches
  at that range, they paint as a fuzzy mass over the hard spot, and that fuzz
  is what to drop on (`AH4Eiya1Hn0`).

## Releasing fish from depth (descender)

Rockfish brought up fast from depth suffer barotrauma and can't swim back
down on their own — a weighted release device gets them back to depth alive
(`_ZThckj2TIM`). ⚠ Fact-check (single-source): the barotrauma mechanism and
its no-swim-back consequence rest on this one clip alone in this note. Clip
the fish into the descender (a simple wire release
device, sold online — worked in carefully so it doesn't puncture the fish),
get enough weight on the device to actually reach bottom depth rather than
stalling partway up (a fish released too shallow slides free and floats back
to the surface), then free-spool it down to depth and give the rod a sharp
snap ("hit the air brakes") to pop the fish free of the release clip so it
can swim back to structure (`_ZThckj2TIM`).

## Detecting the bite and setting the hook

For the soaked dropper-loop gangion above (rod dangling off the side of the
boat, reel in gear, wound up **1–3 turns off the bottom**, then left to sit):
the tell is the **rod tip bouncing, jiggling, and dancing** — that motion is
the bite, read before you feel anything through the blank (`WHLz0kR___I`).

- **Hookset — a small swing plus the handle, at the same time.** Give the
  rod a little upward "hump" while starting to turn the handle in the same
  motion, not one then the other. The swing drives the hook; turning the
  handle at the same moment picks up the slack the swing creates, so the
  hookset and the slack pickup happen together rather than the swing
  outrunning the retrieve (`WHLz0kR___I`).
- **A contrasting preference, same source:** some anglers skip the swing
  entirely and just turn the handle straight through the bite — framed on
  camera as preference, not a right/wrong call (`WHLz0kR___I`).
- **On a self-hooking circle hook** (the rock-cod quick gangion in [Rod,
  reel & line for the feel](#rod-reel--line-for-the-feel) above), skip the
  hookset altogether — turn the handle and wind up the fish, consistent with
  the slow-steady-wind retrieve above (`zVIfArUrpDI`, `U-dGRQ0X-Mc`). ⚠
  Fact-check (contradicted-by-source): `U-dGRQ0X-Mc` never discusses circle
  hooks or skipping the hookset — it only covers winding after a fish is
  already hooked; the matching skip-the-hookset content is in `qh3hL2Dt3HY`
  instead ("when getting bit with a circle hook ... you're just going to turn
  the handle and lift your rod").
- Contrast with the wind-retrieve skirted-bait system above, where the rule
  runs the opposite way — **don't swing** on a tap-tap, keep winding steadily
  and let the long-shank hook self-set. A different rig (self-hooking
  long-shank hook on a steady retrieve) drives a different answer; it isn't a
  conflict with the dangling-gangion bite/hookset above.

## Gear class

No dedicated class exists yet in [gear classes](../tackle/gear-classes.md);
resolve from the parameters above: a **7–7'6" medium-heavy to heavy
graphite/composite conventional rod** (never all-glass — you can't feel a
deep bite through one), a **bigger-bodied reel**, ideally **two-speed** for
fatigue on repeated drops, and **30–65 lb braid** depending on rig and depth
(see [Rod, reel & line for the feel](#rod-reel--line-for-the-feel)) with a
short mono top shot. Conventional, never spinning, at real depth — a
spinning reel's line, not its guides, drags the rail on a long vertical
retrieve and is more likely to abrade or break. For a sportier presentation
on the same fish with a dedicated class, see [slow-pitch
jigging](slow-pitch-jigging.md).

## Common failures

- **Heavy braid / too much sinker** — scopes out, drags off the spot, snags
  more. Go thin and just heavy enough to hold bottom (`O5aQkex0qGg`).
- **No breakaway** — one snag costs the whole rig instead of a sinker
  (`O5aQkex0qGg`).
- **Fishing the bait cloud instead of the hard bottom** — stop and drop only
  on marks you can grade as fish over structure (`11npFUPOJKU`).
- **Hook tied into the loop backwards** — see [hook orientation on the
  loop](dropper-loop.md#hook-orientation-on-the-loop-scotty) in the
  dropper-loop note; the wrong orientation still catches fish but pulls the
  hook out under load instead of setting it deeper, costing more fish on a
  long crank up from depth (`_ZThckj2TIM`).
- **Winding the sinker or hook all the way to the rod tip** — the single most
  common mistake called out; it can compromise or break the tip-top insert
  (or the tip itself), ending the trip (`S9sQ8Vg8-5o`, `lYnD_MiALL8`).
- **Turning the handle as fast as possible on a hooked fish.** Rockfish
  "don't pull drag" — they don't fight hard enough to need speed — and a fast
  crank spins the fish, which spits the hook more often before it reaches the
  boat. Wind **steady**, not fast; this is the stated reason a two-speed
  reel's low gear (deliberately slower line intake) earns its keep beyond
  fatigue alone (`S9sQ8Vg8-5o`, `lYnD_MiALL8`).
- **Pumping the rod.** A worked example: lift 6 ft, drop 4 ft — that 4 ft of
  drop hands the fish 4 ft of slack to throw the hook on. Lay the rod on the
  rail, hold it steady in your arm, and just turn the handle (`S9sQ8Vg8-5o`,
  `lYnD_MiALL8`).
