# Surface Iron

Long-cast a heavy chrome/painted iron on a jig-stick and swim it back near the
surface with a steady grind, varying speed to trigger the bite. The kick — the
iron's periodic side-to-side kick-out — is the presentation, not a side effect
of a good swim. This note owns the cast and the retrieve; which fish and
situations call for it is the reverse map in the generated **Linked from**
section below.

## Reach for this when

- [Yellowtail](../species/yellowtail.md) boiling on the surface or working a
  [kelp paddy](../conditions/kelp-paddies.md) — the long cast reaches fish that
  spook off the boat.
- Surface-feeding [calico bass](../species/calico-bass.md) and
  [sand bass](../species/sand-bass.md) over kelp/structure.
- [Barracuda](../species/barracuda.md) and [bonito](../species/bonito.md) on top.
- [Bluefin](../species/bluefin-tuna.md) foamers when you want a bigger, faster
  presentation than a plug — but for the run-and-gun foamer game itself see
  [foamer casting](foamer-casting.md).
- The bait tank is anchovy, not sardine or mackerel: a fly-lined anchovy draws
  bycatch as readily as the target species, so switch to the iron
  (`8TO-tuYjwWk`) — see [flyline — anchovy
  bycatch](flyline.md#anchovy-bycatch-when-to-switch-off-flyline).

## Retrieve & cadence

### The kick is the presentation

A swimming iron traces a "serpentine action" like a snake, and "every third or
fourth time it's going to kick out to the side, one direction or another. That
kick-out is what triggers a bite" (`yn1suHH6b1Y`, `Ec2GaNpiOHI`). The reason a
fish reads the kick as food: a bait school circulates inside-outside — the
outside fish trying to get in to be safe, the inside fish being pushed out —
and the ones the bonito and the yellowtail bite aren't the ones in the middle;
they're the one that "goes around wrong" and separates from the ball
(`Ec2GaNpiOHI`). The jig is imitating the bait that has separated. A lure
swimming flat gets helped: "speed it up, slow it down, a little perky-jerky
every now and then… you gotta make them bite it. Rarely do they just come up
and inhale it" (`Ec2GaNpiOHI`).

There is no species-specific retrieve: "I was using a yellowtail retrieve, a
calico bass retrieve, a barracuda retrieve — no. When the lure swims right,
fish bite it," fast or slow, as long as the action is right (`Ec2GaNpiOHI`) —
with the corollary that you cannot out-wind a fish: "calico bass can chase down
the lure faster than you can [reel]" (`Ec2GaNpiOHI`). This sits beside — not
merged into — the steady-grind cadence doctrine below; see
**Doctrine & conflicts** for why the two are compatible readings.

### Working the retrieve

- Start a **nice steady grind** ("steady whine") the instant the iron hits
  the water — not too fast; the baseline for calico and picky fish. Wind from
  the splash so the jig stays kicking on top, **slow-to-medium** for calico
  specifically — cranking fast makes the lure skip out of the water instead
  of swimming through it (`3dVc-2rsYII`).
- **Speed up for yellows** to really get the jig moving and swimming.
- Read the **kick** off the rod tip: a heavy iron on a slow, wide, drawn-out
  kick is the change-up for picky bites and slack current; a smaller/faster
  iron worked medium-to-high speed is the searching retrieve. Match rod-tip
  feel to jig — you should feel the iron working, not just dead weight.
- **Fish the retrieve tip-down.** The rod tip pointed down toward the water
  lets the iron kick side to side properly; tip-up pulls it up onto the
  surface where it stops swimming right (`VpW91AKOFVQ`, `3dVc-2rsYII`).
  How far this bite zone extends is species-specific — see
  [yellowtail — surface iron](../species/yellowtail-surface-iron.md).
- **Match crank speed to reel gear ratio, not a fixed cadence.** A high-gear
  jig-stick reel (~**42 in per crank** on a Tranx-500-class HG) covers slow
  grind to fast yellow speed on crank cadence alone. A high-speed reel
  (six-point-something-to-one) has to be cranked deliberately slower to keep
  the kick correct, while an older **4:1–5:1** reel (period conventionals like
  a Newell or Penn Senator) does that slow-down for you and suits a picky bite
  (`3dVc-2rsYII`). Reeling too fast is the single most common mistake — when
  fish you can see, and have caught on that same jig before, won't eat,
  suspect retrieve speed before changing jigs (`3dVc-2rsYII`).
- **Set retrieve speed by keeping the jig visible on top.** The speed is not a
  number: it is the slowest wind that still keeps the jig gliding up where you
  can see it, and **raising the rod tip** is the lever for bringing it up
  without winding faster (`ejUTAnONf7o`) — a practical read of the
  no-species-specific-retrieve doctrine above.
- **Finicky bite scales with school size and location, same species and
  gear.** A big school at the **Coronado Islands** often eats on a faster,
  less exacting retrieve; the smaller schools along the mainland coast
  (**Del Mar down to Imperial Beach**) run markedly more finicky, especially
  on bigger-grade fish, and need the retrieve dialed in precisely
  (`VpW91AKOFVQ`). A refinement of "speed up for yellows" by location and
  school size, not a conflict with it.
- **Burn and return to pace when fish are showing** — pick up "a little bit,
  giving it like a burn," then go back to the pace (`0DKmYId1NN4`). Not a new
  cadence — an injected acceleration inside the steady grind.
- **A tracking fish gets speed, not action**: "faster faster faster, keep
  going — don't twitch, just keep swimming" (`2cxeg_AE418`). Adding a twitch
  to a committed follower is the failure; adding speed is the fix. Sits beside
  — not over — the "little perky-jerky every now and then" help for fish that
  are **not** already tracking (`Ec2GaNpiOHI`).
- **Never stop winding on a miss.** When a fish blows up on the jig and
  doesn't stick, "keep winding, keep on" — the fish comes back and eats it
  (`u80AI37soFI`). Stopping the jig ends the presentation; the swim is the
  trigger, so a missed strike is a reason to keep swimming, not to pause.
- **The pick-up-to-recast is itself a strike trigger.** Speeding up to wind in
  for another cast draws bites on the acceleration (`_MygUHw19q8`) — worth
  knowing before you wind a "finished" retrieve in casually.
- **Two named finicky signatures, both read as fish state:** "grabbing one
  and going back down" — feeding but not committing to the jig
  (`0DKmYId1NN4`); and fish that "attack it and then swipe back down… they
  weren't pushing through and swallowing it" — strikes that never load the
  hook (`ejUTAnONf7o`), read that day as the wind turning the bite, not the
  jig being wrong. See [common failures](#common-failures).
- **Sink it out to cover the column.** Periodically let the iron sink out
  almost like a [yo-yo iron](yo-yo-iron.md) drop, then resume the normal
  surface retrieve — it covers different depths instead of only fishing the
  top (`ntQXxcH5sjI`). The tip runs through the corpus from 2016 on, on both
  the SoCal and Cedros sides of the fishery: the yellowtail cases — Catalina and Cedros — are in
  [yellowtail — surface iron](../species/yellowtail-surface-iron.md). A different fall-bite context — casts allowed to fall
  immediately at anchor over kelp, bit **on the fall** (`NGxyOlPx3ug`) — is
  kept beside, not merged, in the [evidence file](evidence/surface-iron.md).
- **Hookset — wind through it, don't swing.** On the bite, wind through it,
  cranking a little quicker to drive the hooks home; a swing that misses
  pulls the jig straight out of the bite zone and ends the shot at that fish.
  **Crank as fast as you can once you're bit until the line starts peeling out
  the other way** — that's the tell you're actually hooked up; if the line
  isn't going out, the fish likely just missed it and there's a good chance
  for a repeat bite (`3dVc-2rsYII`). The mechanism: most strikes (roughly
  **9 of 10**) come as the fish grabs the swimming iron amidships and shakes
  it — "dog boning" — before turning to swim off; a swing in that instant
  jerks the jig straight out of the fish's mouth, while continued winding
  drives the hooks from the middle of the jig into the fish's face as it
  turns — what actually seats the hook (`KLoEJInlmZo`). The swing-to-set
  doctrine for vertical jigging is the counter-case — held in
  **Doctrine & conflicts** below, not reconciled here.

A distinct alternate technique, not a variant of this retrieve:
[skip jigging](skip-jigging.md) — rod pointed straight up, cranked as fast as
possible so the iron skitters ACROSS the surface instead of swimming through
it (`OL8D1l73RVg`); the tension with this note's tip-down doctrine is held in
**Doctrine & conflicts**, the mechanics in that note.

## When the current dies — wire the jig

The retrieve above assumes water moving past the jig. When the **current goes
slack or slow** and the jig's swing softens — fish tracking it in and not
committing — the rigging change-up is a **short single-strand wire between the
ring and the nose of the iron**, which frees the jig to swing sharply at very
slow speeds. Condition triggers, jig selection, wire spec, the cut-it-off
maintenance rule, and the failure modes are in
**[wiring a surface iron](../rigging/wiring-a-surface-iron.md)**; the
connection is the [haywire twist](../rigging/haywire-twist.md). The stated
counter-case matters as much as the case: if the jig is already swinging
right, leave it alone.

## Chumming the school up, then casting the iron

The sequence: **meter the school → throw live chum → let them boil → cast the
iron into the boil** — the fallback when fish are metered but not showing,
ranked below casting to natural surface activity: "unless we see them boiling
and we could cast on them, then that's going to be our go-to. But this is our
way to get them going: we throw some bait out and then we're going to cast on
them" (`4pT0efBD1j0`). The stated objection travels with the method ("a lot of
people say this is not strictly irons") and the stated answer is that some
days it is the only way to get them going (`4pT0efBD1j0`). The mechanism
repeats a season later — "they found our [chum] — they'll come find us" —
alongside a boat-shy read, "they would not let me drive them over the top of
them," the practical reason the long cast exists (`dS9gd9k7A7Q`). Same-day
corroboration of the sink-out change-up above: the jig got bit after being
allowed to sink about 10 seconds before the retrieve started (`4pT0efBD1j0`).

**Hold the stop once someone's bent**: "every time he hooks up I try to get
bit back right away — that way we could get the most out of our stop"
(`gdqI2dNd5_U`). A hooked fish holds the school near the boat; the window is
while somebody is still connected.

## Size / weight selection

- **Class weight benchmarks.** A surface iron generally runs **2–3 oz**,
  distinctly lighter in the hand than a **5–6 oz** [yo-yo iron](yo-yo-iron.md)
  — pick the two up and you can feel the difference (`VpW91AKOFVQ`). A
  yo-yo-weight iron can still be cast and swum back like a surface iron as
  long as it has the right action; some anglers instead cast it out, let it
  sink on a slack line, then wind it back at roughly a **45° angle**,
  spinning/jigging it in fast — a third cadence, distinct from both the
  straight-up-flutter-down yo-yo drop and this note's steady surface swim
  (`VpW91AKOFVQ`).
- **Wind up → heavier iron.** When it's windy, step up to a heavier surface
  iron (Salas 7X class) — the extra weight "makes casting out a lot easier …
  giving me a little boost in my cast" to punch through the wind
  (`D5DR7Kx42_A`).
- **Anchovy / "rice" bait → downsize and match the hatch.** The big irons —
  Tady 45, Salas 7X — have been the standard since the sardine cycle started
  in the early 1990s (~1993). When the forage shrinks to small anchovy
  ("rice"), drop to a light iron (Tady AA-light / A1-light class — roughly a
  **third the weight** of a Tady 45): on small bait the small jig out-bites
  the big one **two or three to one** (`pcwcRdmWmLc`).
- **The lighter iron drags the tackle down with it.** An AA-light doesn't
  throw on the 80 lb jig stick — the downsized setup is a medium-action 9 ft
  graphite-composite stick, a smaller narrow reel, ~**50 lb braid** to a short
  fluorocarbon leader (`pcwcRdmWmLc`).
- **Small jig on small anchovy, corroborated — with a bigger-fish nuance.** In
  the 2026 season small surface irons were out-fishing bigger ones locally — a
  small iron by **Killer**, and a small Tady named on camera with the
  speaker's own stated uncertainty as "a A1 or AA or something" (kept as said,
  not resolved to a model) — framed explicitly for **small anchovy** bait
  (`JgXMpjxRxmo`). That bait-size-over-fish-size reading is worked out for
  yellowtail in
  [yellowtail — surface iron](../species/yellowtail-surface-iron.md).
- **The Tady 45 / Salas 7X pairing as "the number one" surface iron dates to
  at least 2009.** Asked for his favorite lure, a Searcher captain named the
  surface iron fished on a long rod for the distance it casts, reaching for a
  **Tady 45** or a **Salas 7X** — "surface jigs that swim really well with a
  slow retrieve" — and singled out [Cedros Island](../locations/cedros-island.md)
  and the San Benito Islands for yellowtail (`lYyi_Yh3S5g`). This predates the
  pairing's next-earliest corpus citations (`GXXvT7pS2fM`, 2019-01-22;
  `BLbUu_mfMJY`, 2018-10-18 — both logged in
  [iron jigs](../lures/iron-jigs.md)) by roughly nine years — a further data
  point for the early-1990s (~1993) dating of the pairing as the sardine-cycle
  standard.
- **A three-iron box framework.** Carry **at least three** surface irons
  covering three different jobs rather than one do-everything jig: 1)
  something **small and erratic for a reaction bite** (a **Tady C** — model
  suffix asr-uncertain), swum tight and fast to trigger a following fish, or
  slower for a finesse presentation; 2) something with a **slower retrieve and
  softer action for a finesse bite** (a **Tady 45** — fishable fast or slow,
  called "pretty universal"); 3) something **heavier that casts through
  wind/chop and gets extra distance** (a heavy iron in the Salas 7X class —
  the product name is caption-garbled, not confirmed) (`A70kK2niu2Q`). A
  second data point for the wind-up-means-heavier-iron rule above. The Tady C
  model name gets independent corroboration — shown on camera and described as
  **a little smaller than the Tady 45** (`49joKHD7Umc`) — though the caption
  spelling is still not clean, so the asr-uncertain flag on the model stands.

The short version: **colour is not the variable**, and the same answer holds

## Gear class

### Reel — why high-ratio reels are wrong for this

Reeling too fast is the commonest mistake (see the retrieve section), and the
reason is design intent: "when these lures were designed — the **Penn
Jigmaster** was the go-to reel. And that's **four to one**"; reels at "six and
a half, seven and a half to one — they're too fast" (`Ec2GaNpiOHI`). Spool
diameter is the other half of it: a taller spool (Accurate Valiant class)
picks up more line per turn, so a nominally slower ratio can still be right; a
smaller-diameter spool (Tern class, ~5.5–5.7:1) needs compensating
(`Ec2GaNpiOHI`). A 7:1 counter-doctrine is held in **Doctrine & conflicts**
below. The class call: a narrow conventional (Tranx-500 class),
thumb-controlled, cranked at whatever cadence keeps the kick correct.

### Rod

- **Class:** [jig-stick class](../tackle/gear-classes.md) — 9–10 ft,
  medium-heavy, soft-enough tip to cast a heavy iron but with backbone; a
  10 ft stick throws the biggest irons farthest. Cameron's Torium and
  Trinidad-40 setups fill this role.
  ⚠ misplaced-content: the Cameron-inventory sentence is profile data in a
  universal note — queued for `profiles/cameron/rods.md`.
- **Length and material:** "An **8-ft jig stick is a yo-yo rod** — it's not a
  jig stick. **9-ft is okay. 10-ft is better**" (`yn1suHH6b1Y`). On glass:
  **S-glass does what E-glass does at ~20% less weight** — "when you're on a
  boat 12–14 hours making 300 casts, your back and shoulders don't hurt" — and
  it "shots off a lot sooner, it's got more backbone… think denim versus
  polyester" (`yn1suHH6b1Y`). Stated from a rod manufacturer's stage: the
  S-glass mechanism is doctrine, the brand framing is promotional.
- **Fiberglass vs. composite — two working jig sticks, two jobs.** An
  all-fiberglass rod (Cal Star 90J example) loads up easily and "can really
  swing into pretty far" — the go-to build for surface iron, "the old-school
  classic way to fish," and the same glass rod also throws poppers and stick
  baits (`sjlL5GidM58`). A composite rod (Shimano Terramar 90 example —
  graphite blended with glass) carries a **stiffer action**, the better tool
  for working a popper or stick bait — a stiffer blank gives "a lot more
  response out of your sweep than you would with a jig stick" (`sjlL5GidM58`).
  Two jobs, not one best pick.

### Line, leader, and terminal

- **Line:** ~**80 lb** braid main. Short mono/fluoro leader — **50 lb,
  ~18 in** — to a **100 lb clip** tied straight to the iron (the clip lets
  you swap irons without re-tying).
- **Mono vs braid — the real trade-off is casting latitude, not swim.** "On
  straight spectra you need to make very deliberate casts. You can't just see
  a spot of fish over in the other corner and make that cast — it's a
  backlash" (`Ec2GaNpiOHI`). Braid doesn't kill the action: "a good swimming
  lure is going to swim well no matter what" (`Ec2GaNpiOHI`).
- **Top-shot thickness affects the kick.** A thick top shot dampens an iron's
  swimming action, so on irons specifically run the **lighter/thinner end** of
  your top-shot range; a stickbait or popper tolerates a heavier top shot
  without the same penalty (`F7jLrt2j2X8`; see the reel/top-shot data point in
  [rod & reel selection](../tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md)).
- **Change line during the day.** Carry a bulk spool and re-spool **three or
  four times over a day's fishing** — hundreds of casts, one backlash or nick
  in the line, and "it's not worth losing a lure that you might catch several
  hundred fish on" (`Ec2GaNpiOHI`).
- **Hooks:** Mustad 300-series, chosen **to bend** — rarely on a fish, but
  when the jig is buried in rock or kelp or a sea lion has it, "I'd rather
  have the hook bend and get the lure back" (`Ec2GaNpiOHI`).
- **Iron:** [surface-iron class](../lures/iron-jigs.md); carry a spread of
  profiles/kicks (wide-slow through fast-tight) so you can match the bite.
- **Stow-the-iron tip:** crimp a small loop of **100 lb mono** onto a reel
  post and hang the iron's hook there under tension — it won't rattle loose,
  and it pulls free clean for the cast instead of hooking the spool or nicking
  your line (a Capt. Jimmy Decker tip, `XLVUhV8DW64`).

## Doctrine & conflicts

Positions kept side by side, attributed; **section order is not a ranking.**

- **Hookset: wind through vs. swing.** The wind-through hookset above
  (`3dVc-2rsYII`, `KLoEJInlmZo`) contradicts the swing-to-set doctrine for
  vertical jigging — [knife jigging](knife-jigging.md)'s "grab the spool and
  give three or four hard sideways swings" (`Ul5FLB2dFgQ`). A
  technique-class label, not a conflict to reconcile: the decision frame is
  **how the fish takes the jig** — a vertical jig gets **sucked in** on a
  slack-water take (swing), while a surface iron gets **struck** on a moving
  retrieve (wind through, keeping tension instead of yanking the jig away
  from a fish still closing).
- **Reel ratio: "too fast" vs. the 7:1 middle ground.** Against the
  design-intent doctrine above — 6.5–7.5:1 reels are "too fast" for jigs
  designed around the 4:1 Jigmaster (`Ec2GaNpiOHI`) — a counter clip frames a
  **7:1** baitcaster favorably for surface iron and yo-yo work on big
  yellowtail, as a torque/versatility middle ground rather than too fast
  (`pOFsjDTqaxY`; [baitcaster gear ratio for landing big
  yellowtail](../tackle/tackle-express-baitcaster-gear-ratio-yellowtail.md)).
  The decision frame: what matters is line speed at the jig — set by crank
  discipline and spool diameter as much as ratio. A slower reel does the
  slow-down for you (the same reason a 4:1–5:1 suits a picky bite); a faster
  reel demands deliberately slower cranking.
- **Drag pressure on a hooked fish: pull vs. don't.** "Sometimes you pull too
  hard — it's not hooked that well and it'll just pop off" (`u80AI37soFI`)
  sits in real tension with the lock-the-drag-and-pull doctrine that applies
  over rock at [Cedros](../locations/cedros-island.md) — both stated by the
  same crew, in different water. The decision frame: **where the fish will
  reach structure, pull; in open water on a jig that may be barely stuck,
  don't.**
- **Retrieve doctrine — compatible readings, not a conflict.** "There's no
  normal retrieve… when the lure swims right, fish bite it" (`Ec2GaNpiOHI`)
  and this note's "steady grind, speed up for yellows" cadence are different
  voices describing the same thing: a cadence is one way to produce the right
  action. Kept side by side because the framings differ, not the doctrine.
- **Skip jigging** (`OL8D1l73RVg`) — deliberately tip-up and flat-out fast, in
  tension with this note's tip-down/steady-grind doctrine — is a distinct
  technique with its own note ([skip jigging](skip-jigging.md)), kept side by
  side rather than merged.

## Common failures

- **Backlash on the cast** — kills distance and, on a foamer, kills your one
  shot at that school. Thumb the spool through the cast.
- **Not guiding the line onto the spool while winding.** In the excitement of
  a bite it's easy to stop guiding the retrieved line across the spool with
  your fingers, letting it pile up on one side. The pile doesn't cause a
  problem on the retrieve itself — it sets up a backlash on the **next** cast
  once you reach that piled-up line. Guide the line evenly across the spool on
  every retrieve (`KLoEJInlmZo`).
- **Pushing instead of loading the rod** — short casts, no distance.
- **Wrong retrieve speed** — grinding too fast for finicky calico, or too slow
  to turn a yellowtail. When bit-but-not-hooking, change speed before you
  change jigs.
- **Dropping the rod tip before you're winding.** On the hookset and through
  the fight, **pull the rod up first, then start cranking before the tip
  comes back down** — tip-down-then-wind puts slack in the line right as the
  hook is trying to seat, a documented way to pop a jig out of a fish's mouth
  mid-fight (`VpW91AKOFVQ`). Hold the rod up against the forearm — or braced
  against the hip/gut, with a fighting belt on a bigger fish — rather than
  down low, where you lose leverage. A rod that goes straight/slack mid-fight
  is the tell you're about to lose the fish; keep constant bend and tension
  throughout (`VpW91AKOFVQ`).
- **Over-pulling a lightly hooked fish.** "Sometimes you pull too hard — it's
  not hooked that well and it'll just pop off" (`u80AI37soFI`). The
  pull-vs-don't decision frame is in **Doctrine & conflicts** above.
- **Blaming the jig for a wind-changed bite.** On a 25 kt day at the Coronado
  Islands, fish swiped and dropped off the iron all morning; the crew's
  conclusion was that "the wind kind of threw everything off — they weren't as
  active on the surface as they were the past couple weeks," and other boats
  confirmed the same day had gone mostly [yo-yo](yo-yo-iron.md)
  (`ejUTAnONf7o`). Before cycling through jigs, check whether the surface bite
  is still on at all — and check it against other boats.
