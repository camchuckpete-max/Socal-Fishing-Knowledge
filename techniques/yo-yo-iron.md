# Yo-Yo Iron

Drop a heavy iron straight down to a mark and **crank it back up at full speed**
on a heavy jig-stick — a vertical presentation for fish holding deep or in
current too strong to fish on top. It's cited as the primary way yellowtail
are caught aboard San Diego's long-range fleet, on trips of any length
(`ETw_3AFxEcM`). Execution lives here; where and which fish is the reverse map
in **Linked from** below.

## Reach for this when

- [Yellowtail](../species/yellowtail.md) hold **on or near the bottom** rather
  than boiling on top — the standard Bahía de los Ángeles September pattern,
  fished on dropper loops and yo-yo iron (cameron) — and the default when fish
  are metering deep on structure.
- **Springtime, SoCal coast:** yellowtail suspend right on the bottom over
  squid beds and red crab up and down the coast, and yo-yo iron is the top
  technique for reaching them this time of year (`wzI0lpgKT1U`).
- A hard [current](../conditions/current-diagnostics.md) has shut the surface
  down: when the kelp is **pulled under or laid flat**, surface presentations are
  dead — go vertical. When the kelp resurfaces (current slacking), switch back to
  [surface iron](surface-iron.md).
- [Bluefin tuna](../species/bluefin-tuna.md) marked shallow (~60 ft) around the
  boat while chumming, with no surface foamer yet showing — yo-yo the mark
  instead of waiting on a visual bite (`ntQXxcH5sjI`). ⚠ Fact-check
  (contradicted-by-source): the transcript gives the ~60 ft marks and
  sardines being fished, but never identifies those marks as bluefin
  specifically, never mentions chumming, and never states a foamer was
  absent. Distinct from the 30–50
  fm sounded-school knife-jig scenario in
  [bluefin tuna](../species/bluefin-tuna.md#situations--techniques) — this is a
  much shallower, near-boat mark.
- **Baja, Sea of Cortez — [cabrilla](../species/cabrilla.md) once the sun's up
  and off the jerkbait bite:** late morning to lunch (~9–10 am on), when fish
  have dropped off the shallow jerkbait structure to **60–80 ft**; heavier
  still at midday (~11 am–3 pm) when the bigger fish push to **80–150 ft**
  (`EmZO8QiOfik`). Execution:
  [cabrilla — yo-yo iron](../species/cabrilla-yo-yo-iron.md).

## Mechanics

Position up-current of the mark so the drop sweeps down onto the structure.
Free-spool the iron straight to the mark (or to the bottom), thumbing the
spool so it falls under light tension and tracks straight down rather than
[scoping](../conditions/current-diagnostics.md#line-scope--reading-and-correcting-drift-off-vertical)
away from the boat. A heavy iron is mandatory so it reaches the zone and stays
vertical in current; too light and it never gets down or blows out sideways.

The base retrieve is one motion: **wind as fast as you can, all the way up** —
the fleeing iron's speed is the strike trigger, not a cadence. If nothing
hits, drop back to the mark and rip it again. On the bite, keep cranking
rather than swinging to set the hook — the continuous full-speed wind drives
the hookset itself (`5pbA-wXoric`, `ETw_3AFxEcM`, `hteLeDIy9Qs`, `SAltQjih0ms`).
Fish as heavy a line as you can still crank fast and control the fish: bite
rate is not affected by line diameter, so there is no cost to running the
heaviest class the rod and reel support — 40 lb works, 50 lb is better, 60 lb
better still (`5pbA-wXoric`, `UP_3fBu7VPA`). ⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-by-source):
`UP_3fBu7VPA` states only a personal line-size floor ("I wouldn't do any less
than 40 lb, I do 50 or 60 lb personally") — it never states or implies that
bite rate is unaffected by line diameter; that mechanism is `5pbA-wXoric` only.

Tuck the rod under the arm once the jig hits bottom and the reel is in gear,
then crank back as fast as possible: the jig cannot be cranked too fast, only
too slow (`RNRFrfepiW0`). **Start winding the instant the jig touches and never
let it sit** — the most common yo-yo error is settling into position first, and
a jig left on the bottom gets picked up by
[ocean whitefish](../species/ocean-whitefish.md), small cod and other bottom
critters before a yellowtail finds it (`8XIy7hPsFkQ`).

**A 45° cast beats straight up-and-down for some anglers.** For yellowtail,
casting the jig out (or flipping it away from the boat) so the line sits at
roughly 45° on the wind-back — rather than working it purely vertical — reads
as more natural because of the diagonal retrieve path; fish are taken both
ways, so this is a stated preference with a mechanism, not a correction to the
vertical retrieve (`HTowqnwAMeA`).

**Downsize to match small forage.** When yellowtail are keyed on small bait —
red crabs, small sardines — drop to a smaller/lighter yo-yo jig, including a
brass-finish iron, in place of the standard heavy class; a Tady 4/0 is a
year-round confidence pick otherwise, backed by a Kicker 25 (heavy) and
steel-finish irons for the standard heavy-forage case (`WE643Fue1_A`). A
single SoCal session recorded the same downsize producing a bite with no
forage reason stated — logged in the [evidence file](evidence/yo-yo-iron.md).

A Colt Sniper-class dart jig can be fished two ways off the same cast: cast,
splash, and wind it as a swim retrieve, or cast, splash, let it hit bottom, and
fish it like a yo-yo jig (`HQC-NwW4018`); see also
[dart jig — daytime finesse tuna](dart-jig-tuna.md) for its usual application.

**Baja, Sea of Cortez — cabrilla runs a cast-and-burn, not a bottom hop**
(`EmZO8QiOfik`), on its own cadence count, bite window and depth-keyed iron
picks: see [cabrilla — yo-yo iron](../species/cabrilla-yo-yo-iron.md).

## Cadence variants

A repeated crank-then-freespool cycle that stays down in the strike zone is a
distinct cadence from the continuous full-speed wind above. Sources describe
several variants, kept side by side rather than reconciled to one count:

- **Working water (cast-and-retrieve):** free-spool the iron to the bottom,
  engage gear, crank as fast as possible for 10 cranks, freespool back to the
  bottom, and repeat while walking the iron toward the boat over as much as
  150 yd. Winding continuously the whole retrieve pulls the iron up out of the
  zone where suspended fish hold (`wzI0lpgKT1U`).
- **Straight up-and-down under the boat:** 5–6 quick cranks (roughly 20 ft of
  retrieve), then freespool back down; repeat, cycling through the band where
  fish are marked — e.g. working the 100–120 ft band on marks sitting at
  120 ft in 120–200 ft of water, with fish suspended ~20 ft off the bottom
  (`wzI0lpgKT1U`).
- **Half-depth wind, then re-drop:** free-spool to the bottom, crank as fast as
  possible only about halfway back up — e.g. in 120–150 ft of water, wind up
  60–70 ft off the bottom — then freespool to the bottom again and repeat.
  This saves the angler's arm over a full-depth wind every drop and keeps the
  iron working the band close to the bottom where the fish actually hold
  (`SAltQjih0ms`). **Do not work the iron in short hops right at the
  bottom** — that motion draws rockfish instead of yellowtail (`SAltQjih0ms`).

Strikes often come on the drop, not the crank — the falling iron on freespool
looks like a dying baitfish (`wzI0lpgKT1U`) — but bites also come on the
wind-up itself, so work the whole cycle (`wzI0lpgKT1U`, `ILBl12Jm7-0`). Keep
the ready-position grip through the drop — index finger on the spool, thumb on
the trigger, left hand forward (see
[rod & reel selection](../tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md)) — so the
gear-and-crank on a bite is instant rather than a repositioning; on the drop a
bite often reads as a brief stop before the fish takes off with it
(`wzI0lpgKT1U`). Two further single-session cadence reports — a
two-partial-crank variant and a clicker-on "lazy boy" variant — are logged in
the [evidence file](evidence/yo-yo-iron.md).

## Jig size & depth

Weight scales against a surface iron: roughly **5–6 oz** against a surface
iron's 2–3 oz — heavy enough to sink fast and hold vertical in current
(`VpW91AKOFVQ`). Depth-banded sizing, corroborated across sources:

- **60/80/100 ft:** a smaller jig (Salas 6X Jr class).
- **120–150 ft:** a Salas 6X, roughly 5–6 oz.
- **Real deep water** — named examples: Colonet, "the ridge," Alijos Rocks,
  Guadalupe: a 7X-heavy iron (`hteLeDIy9Qs`, `VpW91AKOFVQ`, `LTCFjqtSI8g`).
  ⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-by-source): only `hteLeDIy9Qs` names Colonet,
  "the ridge," Alijos Rocks, and Guadalupe with a 7X-heavy iron; neither
  `VpW91AKOFVQ` (Coronado Islands and the Del Mar–Imperial Beach coastline
  only) nor `LTCFjqtSI8g` (no matching real-deep-water locations) support
  this "corroborated across sources" framing for the deep tier.

On an overnight/multi-day trip, yo-yo depth commonly runs **120–240 ft**;
check with the crew on what depth and jig size is producing before the drop.
Rule of thumb: **150–250 ft** calls for a full-size 6X-class iron; shallower,
or when getting down fast matters less, a smaller iron (6X Jr, or a Tady
40-class jig) (`UP_3fBu7VPA`). When a school is called over the PA with no
bottom to mark against, get the jig down a little deeper than the depth
called, then wind it up through the school at full speed (`RNRFrfepiW0`).

A school may hold under the boat for only 5–10 seconds during an otherwise
long search — being rigged and ready to drop the instant fish are called
matters as much as jig choice (`UP_3fBu7VPA`).

## Colour

Colour follows forage, not a fixed favourite: **blue and white** when
yellowtail feed on thin baitfish — anchovies, sardines; **scrambled egg** when
they're on invertebrates — squid or crabs (`ETw_3AFxEcM`). **Mint** is a third
go-to with no stated forage reason — a preference, not a mechanism
(`ETw_3AFxEcM`). Red-toned jigs produce specifically during red-crab years,
matching forage colour rather than being a fixed favourite (`UP_3fBu7VPA`,
`RNRFrfepiW0`). Scrambled egg, blue and white, and mint are named repeatedly as
"the classics" (`UP_3fBu7VPA`, `A70kK2niu2Q`, `SAltQjih0ms`); dorado is also a
repeated producer, particularly on Baja trips (`XwwIvPFxRiQ`, `ilINTeknKB4`).
⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-by-source): only `UP_3fBu7VPA` groups scrambled
egg/blue-and-white/mint as "the classics" for yo-yo iron. `SAltQjih0ms` names
only scrambled egg and blue-and-white (calling blue-and-white "the universal
color for yo-yo fishing"), never mint; `A70kK2niu2Q` gives mint as a
confidence color for surface iron, not yo-yo, and calls scrambled egg/blue-
and-white "my two favorite colors" without grouping mint in.
All red is named alongside blue and white and scrambled egg as one of the
three colours reached for most (`HTowqnwAMeA`).
A Tady 4/0 in scrambled egg or blue-and-white is named as a go-to yo-yo jig
for yellowtail from San Diego to Cabo San Lucas (`ETw_3AFxEcM`). Individual
trip colour reports corroborating this range are logged in the
[evidence file](evidence/yo-yo-iron.md).

## Gear class

**Rod:** heavy [jig-stick class](../tackle/gear-classes.md) with real
backbone. A 10 ft stick (Phoenix Axis class) also works for
casting-then-yo-yoing the same iron (cameron).

**Reel:** high-torque conventional wound tight — the appeal of yo-yo is a
hard, fast retrieve, so a strong power grind matters more than top gear ratio.
On a charter with anglers who aren't strong crankers, a ~6.6:1-ratio reel
makes it easier to take line fast enough on the wind-up: retrieve speed
matters more than raw cranking power for someone still learning the technique
(`VpW91AKOFVQ`). A concrete number behind that reasoning — a Penn Torque 40
paired with a rod rated 40–100 lb, picked specifically for its fast retrieve
of 43 in of line per full handle rotation (`7Ljt-DeFVcs`). ⚠ Fact-check
(single-source): the 43 in/rotation retrieve-rate figure rests on this one
transcript only; no other cited source in this note corroborates it. A Tackle Express
counter clip makes the same torque-over-speed case for a baitcaster fishing
both surface iron and yo-yo on big yellowtail (`pOFsjDTqaxY`) — see
[baitcaster gear ratio for landing big
yellowtail](../tackle/tackle-express-baitcaster-gear-ratio-yellowtail.md).

**Leader:** run a short piece of mono between the braided main line and a
short fluorocarbon leader for stretch. Going straight from spectra to a short
fluoro leader with no buffer bends hooks and pulls fish — the mono cushion
stops that specific failure mode (`hteLeDIy9Qs`).

**Iron:** heavy [yo-yo iron class](../lures/iron-jigs.md) — a Salas 6X / Salas
CP 105 type: drop to the mark, full-speed crank. Light enough that it can
still be cast and swum back like a surface iron if needed (see
[surface iron](surface-iron.md)).

**Attributed builds, kept side by side** — none replaces the class build
above, and listing order is not a ranking:

- **Base case, 12–30 lb yellowtail near an island or bank in 120–180 ft:**
  two-speed Penn Fathom 30, 65 lb braid, a 50-yard 40–50 lb monofilament top
  shot, an 8 ft 30–60 lb-rated rod, a treble hook, and a Tady 4/0 (`RNRFrfepiW0`).
- **Upsized for bigger, meaner Baja fish — Alijos Rocks, "the ridge":**
  two-speed Penn Fathom 40 Narrow, 80 lb braid (filled three-quarters) to a
  60 lb monofilament top shot, a shorter, more powerful 30–80 lb rod. Stated
  triggers for stepping up beyond just fish size: big fish, windy conditions,
  or difficulty getting the jig to the bottom (`RNRFrfepiW0`).
- **A general Baja box:** a 7 ft, 30–80 lb heavy-action rod (general guidance
  40–60 lb rated), a fast reel, and a full-size Tady 4/0-class iron
  (`A70kK2niu2Q`). A year later, the same rod ran a Shimano Torium 16HG with
  50–80 yd of monofilament top shot straight to the jig, no fluorocarbon
  leader (`LTCFjqtSI8g`).
- **A heavier tier on the same rod:** a Daiwa Saltiga 35HA, 80–100 yd of 50 lb
  monofilament straight to the jig, no leader — built for schoolie fish (7–15
  lb) fired up and fought fast, or for Baja structure/kelp with bigger fish
  needing the backbone to keep a fish out of the reef (`LTCFjqtSI8g`).
  ⚠ Fact-check (contradicted-by-source): the transcript puts the Saltiga
  35HA on a separate, newly introduced rod ("this is going to be my new
  heavy jig stick... a nine foot heavy rod"), not the same rod as the
  Torium 16HG build above.
- **A recreational base build:** two-speed conventional, 80 lb braid to a
  40–60 lb monofilament top shot, no fluorocarbon, tied straight to the iron
  on a [San Diego jam knot](../rigging/san-diego-jam-knot.md), on a 7 ft rod
  stiffer than a flyline stick. Run high gear while searching for the fastest
  wind-up, low gear only once a fish is hooked and settled into the fight
  (`SAltQjih0ms`).
- **Another Searcher build, a different top-shot class on the same reel/rod
  two years apart:** Penn Fathom 40 Narrow, 7–7.5/8 ft rod rated 30–60,
  30–80, or as heavy as 40–100 lb, with 50–60 lb top shot over 80 lb braid
  (`ETw_3AFxEcM`) — kept side by side with the 80 lb mono top shot specified
  for the same rig elsewhere.
- **A 50 lb short, stout rod** rated 40, 50, or 60 lb, paired with the
  mono-buffer leader mechanism above (`hteLeDIy9Qs`).

A full one-trip build ran a Salas 6X Jr in blue-and-white on 40 lb
fluorocarbon leader tied to 65 lb braid with a uni-to-uni knot, dropping to
about 80 ft with bait marks around 60 ft (`ntQXxcH5sjI`). Further one-trip
builds and colour data points are in the
[evidence file](evidence/yo-yo-iron.md).

## Common failures

- **Iron too light for the current** — never reaches the mark, or planes off
  to the side and never fishes vertical. Match iron weight to how hard the
  water is running.
- **Winding too slow** — a lazy grind gets refused; the bite comes off speed.
- **Fishing yo-yo when the surface is alive** — if fish are boiling or the
  kelp is standing (slack current), [surface iron](surface-iron.md) reaches
  more fish.
- **Under-sizing the jig for the conditions costs working time, not just
  action.** The stated rule: light current and nice weather → small jig; wind
  or strong current → upsize. The mechanism is a time budget, not an action
  argument — a jig too light for the conditions spends most of its retrieve in
  free spool waiting to hit bottom instead of actively working the zone
  (`HTowqnwAMeA`).
- **Continuous jigging inside a marked school can spook and disperse it.**
  Working the jig with an active, continuous motion once it is down inside a
  school is the worst thing to do with it: some fish won't bite while it's
  being worked that way, the aggressive motion scares fish off, and the rest
  of the school tends to move off with them. Fix: let the jig reach bottom
  and bounce it there, or burn it up on a fast continuous retrieve instead —
  a fish may chase it down on the bottom bounce, or chase it up on the burn
  (`6Z6Dht20kTA`, `S0-BCQWA0H4`). A heavy mark held at the thermocline may not
  be a true, cohesive school at all — it can just be where fish are
  stacked/holding; casting through it and ripping the jig stirs the fish into
  chasing, which is the mechanism behind the burn/rip-it-up fix (`SH5CZQi9ATw`).
- **Physical toll, and it can spook cautious fish.** The constant
  lift-reel-drop cycle is demanding on the arms and shoulders over a long
  session, and the rapid, aggressive action can spook more cautious fish
  (`ntQXxcH5sjI`).
- **A bycatch species can own the water column, masking an interception
  problem as a jig problem.** A yellowtail day planned around yo-yo iron and
  surface iron was abandoned to bonito bycatch; switching to dead sardines —
  not a different jig — restarted the yellowtail bite, with the fish sitting
  on tuna pens at about 120 ft (`Ob45hK4HSWc`; see
  [yellowtail](../species/yellowtail.md) for the tuna-pen detail). The reflex
  when a yo-yo goes untouched is to change jig weight or speed, and neither
  fixes the wrong fish owning the column.
