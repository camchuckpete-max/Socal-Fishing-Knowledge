# Kayak Bass Fishing

Small-craft-specific execution for working [calico bass](../species/calico-bass.md)
and [sand bass](../species/sand-bass.md) structure from a kayak — covering ground,
using kelp as an anchor, and working boiler rocks safely at close range. The bass
doctrine itself (structure, current, water color, depth) lives in the species
routers and in [bass structure](../locations/bass-structure.md); this note is only
what changes when the platform is a kayak instead of a powerboat. Source: Tommy
Ponce (kayak guide, Dana Point home waters), Fred Hall Show seminar, Fisher Newb
channel (`LqW32NSkObY`, 2019-03-13); unregistered channel — medium confidence.

## Reach for this when

- You're covering bass structure from a kayak or other small, low, unpowered/
  pedal craft rather than a boat with a helm and electronics.
- You need to relocate a spot without a fish finder, or want a repeatable way
  to find it again from the water.
- You're working boiler rocks or a kelp bed at close range and need a
  small-craft-specific approach and bail-out plan.

## Finding and relocating spots without electronics

A kayak angler often has no fish finder running, or is fishing water too small
and technical to rely on one. Ponce's method: line up two shore landmarks —
a lifeguard tower, a buoy, a point on the coastline — and hold a fixed offset
from them ("100 yards south of that buoy is a rock pile"). Built ahead of time
(the night before or that morning, based on the day's wind/weather call — see
below), this lets the same spot be found repeatably on landmarks alone.

- **Weather sets the day's zone before launch.** Wind direction is used to
  pick which stretch of coast to fish — e.g., from Dana Point home waters,
  a windy day pushes the choice south to get the wind at your back rather
  than fighting a long paddle/pedal home into it. Guided trips are planned
  around the group's ability to make the crossing distance involved (Ponce
  cites an example run of roughly 8 miles).

## Kelp as an improvised anchor

Rather than carrying a physical anchor, tie a line to a piece of kelp — it is
already rooted to the seafloor and holds the kayak in place well enough to
fish the water column, top to bottom, at that spot. Untie to release; the kelp
goes back into the water with no gear left behind.

## Clearing kelp from a Mirage-Drive-style pedal drive

On Hobie-style pedal-drive kayaks, the drive unit is the first thing to foul
in heavy kelp — it buries in the fronds "like quicksand." The fix is to pull
the drive unit out of the hull rather than fight it, then either let the
current carry the kayak clear or paddle to open water, and reinstall the
drive once clear.

## Approaching boiler rocks

Turbulent water around boiler rocks holds the bigger fish (consistent with the
current-driven doctrine in [calico bass](../species/calico-bass.md) and
[current structure](../conditions/current-structure.md)), so the small-craft
approach is to get deliberately close rather than staying outside the
turbulence:

- Close to within casting range of the rocks, then push in as close as safety
  allows.
- On a pedal-drive kayak, use reverse to back straight out along the same
  line rather than turning the kayak around to return to the starting
  position.
- **Bail-out distance: about two kayak lengths.** At that range from the
  rocks, turn back to the original stand-off point; if the spot isn't
  producing, move on to the next structure rather than working it longer.
- Stay alert to the swell/surge the whole time — this is named as the
  highest-risk part of kayak bass fishing (flipping, losing gear) and is
  where "listen to your guide" is repeated as the operating rule.

## Casting to cover ground

- **Fan-casting**: work a spot by casting to a spread of points around it
  (not just straight ahead) to grade whether it's holding fish before
  moving on — used especially working the outside edge of a kelp bed.
- **Fish the outside edge, not the inside of the bed** — Ponce stays just
  outside the kelp rather than in it, both to avoid fouling and to use the
  ambush instinct: a lure cast along the edge forces fish to come out of the
  kelp to eat it, rather than presenting inside where they're already home.
  Consistent with the leading-edge doctrine in
  [calico bass](../species/calico-bass.md#finding-them-sign--sonar).
- **Working a long structure line (e.g. an island shoreline):** hold the
  kayak roughly parallel to the shore, cast ahead, retrieve, and pedal/paddle
  forward — covering ground continuously rather than sitting in one place
  unless it's actively producing. On a bite, stop and fan-cast that spot
  before moving on again.
- **Stand-off and noise discipline:** hold about two kayak lengths off the
  target and cast hard/far rather than closing the distance — the goal is to
  keep the lure's splash and the kayak's own noise from spooking fish working
  shallow, tight structure.

## Gear and tackle notes (as told)

- Minimal, spot-specific tackle: one bag of lures matched to the target
  species and area rather than a full tackle box — brought up as a common
  beginner mistake (e.g. carrying offshore trolling lures on a calico bass
  trip).
- Preference for a baitcaster over a spinning outfit for most of the day's
  fishing, favoring a stiffer-backboned rod over a rod with more tip
  sensitivity; spinning gear is kept as a second option for pitching
  accuracy in tight spots where a baitcaster's casting is more limited from
  a kayak.
- **Leader taper through the day:** starting leader length is roughly rod-tip
  to about 2 ft; as it gets nicked/re-tied over the course of a session it
  progressively shortens, commonly ending up around a foot by day's end — two
  to three leaders tied on per session as a starting allowance.
