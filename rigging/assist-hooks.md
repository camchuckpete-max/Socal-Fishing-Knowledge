# Assist Hooks — Sizing, Cord, and Tying

How to build and resize an assist hook trackside or on the water: matching hook
size to a jig, choosing cord stiffness for the jig style, and tying a single
(top) assist and a double (tail) assist. This is the tying procedure; which
hook count/placement to fish on a given jig and species is the
[knife jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md) rigging-class doctrine — this note feeds
that decision, it doesn't make it.

## When to use it

- Resizing an assist to match a new jig's length, replacing a chewed-off hook,
  or building a heavier/lighter assist cord on the rail without a full tying
  kit — see [knife/speed jigging](../techniques/knife-jigging.md) and
  [slow-pitch jigging](../techniques/slow-pitch-jigging.md) for when each jig
  style and cord stiffness applies.
- A single top (head) assist for a slender speed/knife jig.
- A double (tail) assist for a short, heavy flat-fall-style jig, built for
  jigs that keep getting bitten off or under-hooked on a single hook.

## Parameters & judgment

All from Cesar (Phoenix Rods, via Dirty Hookers). The construction/tying
parameters below are now corroborated across two videos in the same
Assist Hook series — U4zifdssSes (2024-02-23, "part one") and unARAuTgF_A
(2024-03-04, "part two") — so this note carries `high` confidence per the
registry's repeated-parameter-doctrine rule for Cesar; parameters sourced
from only one of the two videos are marked accordingly below. Owner-brand
hooks, pliers, cord, tubing, and rings used in both demos were supplied by
Owner as the video's sponsor — treat the specific product callouts as
low-confidence promotional detail; the sizing/stiffness/tying judgment below
is the transferable parameter.

- **Hook-to-jig sizing — pass-through test:** hold the hook against the jig
  body; it should be just wide enough to pass all the way through the jig's
  body — an oversized hook interferes with the jig's swimming action (the
  source doesn't address the undersized case, only warns against too big).
  On the demo speed jig this landed on a size 7 hook (Owner SJ51 —
  product-specific, low confidence; the pass-through rule is the doctrine,
  not the model number).
- **Assist position:** for a single top assist on a speed jig, the loop
  should rest roughly a third of the way down the jig's total length.
- **Cord stiffness by jig style — and why (unARAuTgF_A):** slide shrink
  tubing over the assist cord itself (not just over the hook) to stiffen it
  for a speed jig, so the loop doesn't flop around and tangle on the main
  line; slower jigs are fished with a softer cord instead. Part two gives the
  mechanism U4zifdssSes didn't spell out: a speed jig gets bit **on the
  pump/upswing** while you're actively working it, so you want the hook
  **stationary**, riding exactly where it'll be when the fish eats — a stiff
  cord (even on a stiff dual-top build, see below) holds it there for a solid
  hook set on the swing. A slow-pitch jig gets bit **on the fall**, so you
  want the hook **looser and flopping**, not locked in position. This matches
  [slow-pitch jigging](../techniques/slow-pitch-jigging.md)'s "fall is the
  trigger" doctrine and [knife/speed jigging](../techniques/knife-jigging.md)'s
  reaction-bite framing — it's the tying-side reason those two mechanics call
  for different cord stiffness. On the heavier tail double built for a short,
  heavy flat-fall-style jig (U4zifdssSes), the cord-stiffening tube step is
  skipped — the source doesn't say why, only that it isn't used there.
- **Cord weight — two different cord families, not a single number
  (asr-uncertain on the U4zifdssSes numbers — the auto-caption numbers are
  hard to pin down exactly):** U4zifdssSes's single top assist on a speed jig
  used a mono-core assist cord described as roughly "215 lb"; its tail double
  on a flat-fall-style jig stepped up to a heavier mono-core cord, roughly
  "300 lb" class. unARAuTgF_A instead uses **hollow-core** PE line rated a
  clean, clearly stated **180 lb** for both a single top assist and a dual-top
  assist on comparable speed-jig-class hooks — see the hollow-core and
  PE-strength notes below for why 180 lb hollow core is treated as plenty
  even though it reads lighter than the mono-core numbers above.
- **Double-hook orientation:** to keep the two points of a tail double riding
  outward instead of crossing or locking together, tie each hook from the
  back of the hook shank forward toward the point (one hook per leg of the
  doubled, ringed cord) — the same overhand-style wrap around the shank used
  for the single assist, just started from the opposite end. This matches the
  "two hooks must never cross or lock together" rule already logged in
  [knife jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md). **On a dual-top build tied to a
  swivel instead of a solid ring (unARAuTgF_A):** also keep each hook's eye
  perpendicular (90°) to the swivel's eye — i.e., the hook eye crosses the
  flat plane of the swivel's eye at a right angle (the source calls this out
  as something to watch carefully but doesn't state the mechanism/reason).
- **Glue:** use thick (gel), not thin, cyanoacrylate — thin glue wicks up the
  PE cord and leaves stiff, chalky marks running up the line (unARAuTgF_A);
  thick glue stays put on the knot. One drop directly on the knot, set for
  roughly 30–45 seconds (or blow on it to speed drying) before trimming the
  tag. An extra drop after the tag is trimmed and the shrink tube is on is
  optional insurance against the knot working loose.
- **Shrink tube, two jobs:** a smaller-diameter sleeve over the cord itself
  stiffens it (speed-jig builds only, see above); a larger sleeve over the
  hook's eye and knot protects that connection on every build. Shrink with a
  lighter (a hot-water dip is the presenter's usual preference, but a lighter
  is more practical rail-side) — keep the flame moving rather than holding it
  under one spot, and go easy on the cord itself, which can heat-damage
  faster than the hook.
- **Color:** hook color (black/clear/red in the Owner lineup used) doesn't
  matter to the fish — pick whatever's visible against your work surface.

### Cord type — hollow core vs mono/four core (unARAuTgF_A)

- U4zifdssSes (part one of the series) used an assist cord built around a
  mono or four-strand core. Part two introduces **hollow-core** PE assist
  cord as an alternative: the cord is hollow, so instead of doubling a solid
  cord over and knotting it, you can **splice the cord back into itself** —
  pass one end back inside the other's hollow braid to make a streamlined
  loop — before connecting a solid ring or a swivel.
- **Threading tool:** a latch needle (supplied with the Owner cord) pulls the
  cord through itself and through shrink tube alike — thread it partway in,
  give the cord a light twist so the latch pops open, hook the far end, flip
  the latch closed, and pull it back through. A short length of stiff
  monofilament, lightly wetted, serves the same pulling-tool role for
  threading shrink tube when a latch needle isn't at hand (also noted in
  U4zifdssSes).
- When looping the cord onto a ring, either poke straight through the ring
  or loop around it first — presenter preference, no stated performance
  difference between the two.
- Once doubled, a hollow-core cord is noticeably **stiffer** than it was
  single — factor that into the separate stiffening-tube step above rather
  than stacking both if the doubled cord is already stiff enough on its own.

### Connecting to the jig — solid ring vs swivel (unARAuTgF_A)

Two ways to finish the ring/swivel end of an assist, demonstrated side by
side on the same speed-jig build. Cesar frames the solid ring as the more
traditional approach and the swivel as his own, more controversial
preference.

- **Solid ring (traditional):** the doubled cord is ringed to a solid ring at
  the hook end; the hook is then joined to the leader or main-line swivel
  with a **separate split ring**.
- **Swivel (Cesar's preference), for two stated reasons:**
  1. **Room for a dual-top build.** A swivel-ended assist isn't as stiff/
     bulky as two doubled cords crowded onto one solid ring, which leaves a
     working gap between the two hook loops of a dual-top assist — enough
     room to still run a split ring off the swivel to the jig. (See the
     dual-top-on-a-swivel hook-count/placement variant logged in
     [knife jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md).)
  2. **A more solid connection under load.** When a fish is fighting the
     jig, the load runs straight onto the swivel-to-main-line connection
     instead of through a split ring. Split rings are rated strong enough to
     hold, but a direct swivel-to-line tie is framed as the more solid
     connection. A swivel also **stops line twist** from a rotating,
     fighting fish — cited as the reason more anglers are moving toward
     swivel-ended assists over a plain solid ring.
  - **Tying main line direct to the swivel:** for anglers comfortable
    fishing without a separate bite leader, the main line ties straight to
    the assist's swivel — no extra swivel-and-crimp connection needed.
    Leader-fishing anglers still gain the same anti-twist/load benefit by
    tying their leader to the swivel instead of to a solid ring.
- **Swivel sizing:** a small **#2-class swivel** (Owner brand in the demo)
  rated to **310 lb** is the smallest size Cesar will use — small in profile
  but far more than strong enough. Going smaller than a #2 risks the
  swivel's thin wire cutting into heavier line classes. A bigger swivel, up
  to roughly a **1 or 1/0**, is also fine — the wire gauge gets thicker with
  swivel size, at the cost of a bulkier connection.

### PE line strength — why the cord is rated heavier than you'll ever load

- unARAuTgF_A's 180 lb hollow-core PE cord (see cord weight, above) reads
  light next to U4zifdssSes's 215–300 lb mono-core cords, but the doubled
  loop construction means the working connection at the ring/swivel is still
  only rated to that single-strand number (180 lb), not doubled to ~360 lb —
  don't read the fold-over as adding strength at the connection point.
- The heavier-than-necessary rating on any assist cord isn't chosen for
  breaking strength — you're not going to load anywhere near even 180 lb on
  an assist cord in practice. It's chosen for **abrasion resistance**: a
  heavier cord holds up longer against a fish's teeth/scales chewing on it.
  Shrink tube over the cord (see above) is the other layer of the same
  bite-protection logic.

### Tying steps, paraphrased — single top assist (speed jig)

1. Cut the cord to length (roughly the jig's resting-point distance, about a
   third of the jig's length, plus a little extra to work with) and fold it
   in half to form the loop.
2. Thread a length of the smaller stiffening shrink tube onto the cord first
   — for a long or unwieldy cord, threading a stiff mono leader through first
   and using it to pull the tube on can make this easier.
3. Wrap the doubled cord around the hook shank with an overhand-style knot
   that cinches down onto the shank, leaving enough tag to grip.
4. Use split-ring pliers with a notched jaw to pinch the cord tight against
   the shank while the plier's jaw end pulls the tag end taut.
5. Glue the knot (see glue parameter above) and let it set.
6. Trim the tag short, then slide a larger shrink-tube sleeve over the hook
   eye and knot, butted up against the hook, and shrink it in place.

### Tying steps, paraphrased — double tail assist (flat-fall-style jig)

1. Cut a longer piece of the heavier cord, fold in half, and thread it
   through a solid ring using a uni-knot-style wrap.
2. Cinch the ring tight against the wraps with the same notched split-ring
   pliers technique as the single assist.
3. Tie each of the two hooks onto its own leg of the doubled cord, working
   from the back of the shank toward the point (see orientation parameter
   above), so the points ride outward rather than crossing.
4. Glue each knot, let it set, then trim tags short.
5. Slide a medium shrink-tube sleeve over each hook from the point down
   toward the eye, tucking the tag under the eye's lip, and shrink in place.

### Tying steps, paraphrased — dual-top assist on a swivel (speed jig, unARAuTgF_A)

1. Cut two lengths of hollow-core cord, doubling each back into itself
   (see hollow-core cord type, above) instead of tying a solid-ring knot.
2. Tie the first hook onto its cord, keeping the hook eye perpendicular
   (90°) to the swivel eye (see double-hook orientation, above), using the
   same overhand-style wrap around the shank as the single assist.
3. Tie the second hook onto the swivel's other eye the same way, again kept
   perpendicular.
4. Glue each knot, let it set, then trim tags short.
5. Shrink-tube each hook eye/knot connection the same as a single assist. A
   split ring off the swivel's remaining eye connects the finished dual-top
   assist to the jig.

## Conflict note

Cesar ties a single assist at the head of a speed jig **and**, separately in
the same series, a double assist on the tail of a flat-fall-style jig — a
top-plus-bottom, potentially three-hook combination when both are run
together. Part two of the series adds a third build: a **dual-top** assist
(two hooks, both at the head, tied to a swivel rather than a solid ring) —
still within the two-hooks-max framing on its own, but another top-heavy
variant in the same unreconciled hook-count/placement conflict. All of this
sits inside the same live conflict already tracked in
[knife jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md) (`single bottom pair, no top hook` vs.
several top+bottom / three-hook configurations already logged there) — see
that note for the full side-by-side list; not reconciled here.
