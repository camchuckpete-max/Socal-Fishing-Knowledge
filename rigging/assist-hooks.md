# Assist Hooks — Sizing, Cord, and Tying

How to build and resize an assist hook trackside or on the water: matching hook
size to a jig, choosing cord stiffness for the jig style, and tying a single
(top) assist and a double (tail) assist. This is the tying procedure; which
hook count/placement to fish on a given jig and species is the
[knife jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md) rigging-class doctrine — this note feeds
that decision, it doesn't make it.

## When to use

- Resizing an assist to match a new jig's length, replacing a chewed-off hook,
  or building a heavier/lighter assist cord on the rail without a full tying
  kit — see [knife/speed jigging](../techniques/knife-jigging.md) and
  [slow-pitch jigging](../techniques/slow-pitch-jigging.md) for when each jig
  style and cord stiffness applies.
- A single top (head) assist for a slender speed/knife jig.
- A double (tail) assist for a short, heavy flat-fall-style jig, built for
  jigs that keep getting bitten off or under-hooked on a single hook.

## Parameters

The hook-to-jig sizing, cord-stiffness, and tying parameters below are
corroborated across two videos in the same assist-hook series
(`U4zifdssSes`, `unARAuTgF_A`); parameters sourced from only one of the two
are marked accordingly. Owner-brand hooks, pliers, cord, tubing, and rings
used in both demos are sponsor-supplied — the specific product names/models
stay low-confidence promotional detail, while the sizing/stiffness/tying
judgment itself is the transferable parameter.

- **Hook-to-jig sizing — pass-through test:** hold the hook against the jig
  body; it should be just wide enough to pass all the way through the jig's
  body — an oversized hook interferes with the jig's swimming action (the
  undersized case isn't addressed) (`U4zifdssSes`). On the demo speed jig
  this landed on a size 7 hook — Owner SJ51, product name, low confidence;
  the pass-through rule is the transferable parameter, not the model number.
- **Assist position:** for a single top assist on a speed jig, the loop
  rests roughly a third of the way down the jig's total length
  (`U4zifdssSes`, `unARAuTgF_A`).
- **Cord stiffness by jig style:** slide shrink tubing over the assist cord
  itself (not just over the hook) to stiffen it for a speed jig, so the loop
  doesn't flop around and tangle on the main line; slower jigs use a softer
  cord instead (`U4zifdssSes`). A speed jig gets bit **on the pump/upswing**
  while it's being actively worked, so the hook should stay **stationary**,
  riding exactly where it'll be when the fish eats — a stiff cord holds it
  there for a solid hookset on the swing. A slow-pitch jig gets bit **on the
  fall**, so the hook should be **looser and flopping**, not locked in
  position (`unARAuTgF_A`). This matches [slow-pitch
  jigging](../techniques/slow-pitch-jigging.md)'s "fall is the trigger"
  doctrine and [knife/speed jigging](../techniques/knife-jigging.md)'s
  reaction-bite framing — the tying-side reason the two mechanics call for
  different cord stiffness. The heavier tail double built for a short, heavy
  flat-fall-style jig skips the cord-stiffening tube step; the reason isn't
  stated (`U4zifdssSes`).
- **Cord weight — two different cord families, not a single number:**
  the single top assist on a speed jig uses a mono-core assist cord rated
  roughly 215 lb; the tail double on a flat-fall-style jig steps up to a
  heavier mono-core cord rated roughly 300 lb (`U4zifdssSes`; asr-uncertain
  on both figures — captioned numbers, hard to pin down exactly). A
  hollow-core PE alternative instead runs a clean, clearly stated 180 lb for
  both a single top assist and a dual-top assist on comparable
  speed-jig-class hooks (`unARAuTgF_A`) — see [PE line
  strength](#pe-line-strength) below for why 180 lb hollow-core is treated as
  plenty even though it reads lighter than the mono-core numbers.
- **Double-hook orientation:** to keep the two points of a tail double riding
  outward instead of crossing or locking together, tie each hook from the
  back of the hook shank forward toward the point (one hook per leg of the
  doubled, ringed cord) — the same overhand-style wrap around the shank used
  for the single assist, just started from the opposite end (`U4zifdssSes`).
  This matches the "two hooks must never cross or lock together" rule
  already logged in [knife jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md). On a dual-top build
  tied to a swivel instead of a solid ring, also keep each hook's eye
  perpendicular (90°) to the swivel's eye — the hook eye crosses the flat
  plane of the swivel's eye at a right angle; worth watching carefully, the
  mechanism isn't stated (`unARAuTgF_A`).
- **Glue:** use thick (gel), not thin, cyanoacrylate — thin glue wicks up the
  PE cord and leaves stiff, chalky marks running up the line; thick glue
  stays put on the knot (`unARAuTgF_A`). One drop directly on the knot, set
  for roughly 30–45 seconds (or blow on it to speed drying) before trimming
  the tag. An extra drop after the tag is trimmed and the shrink tube is on
  is optional insurance against the knot working loose.
- **Shrink tube, two jobs:** a smaller-diameter sleeve over the cord itself
  stiffens it (speed-jig builds only, see above); a larger sleeve over the
  hook's eye and knot protects that connection on every build. Shrink with a
  lighter — a hot-water dip is an alternative, but a lighter is more
  practical rail-side — keeping the flame moving rather than holding it under
  one spot, and going easy on the cord itself, which heat-damages faster than
  the hook.
- **Color:** hook color (black/clear/red in the Owner lineup used) doesn't
  matter to the fish — pick whatever's visible against the work surface.

### Cord type — hollow core vs. mono/four core

One build uses an assist cord built around a mono or four-strand core
(`U4zifdssSes`). A hollow-core PE alternative lets the cord be **spliced back
into itself** — one end passed back inside the other's hollow braid to make
a streamlined loop — instead of doubling a solid cord over and knotting it,
before connecting a solid ring or a swivel (`unARAuTgF_A`).

- **Threading tool:** a latch needle (supplied with the cord) pulls the cord
  through itself and through shrink tube alike — thread it partway in, give
  the cord a light twist so the latch pops open, hook the far end, flip the
  latch closed, and pull it back through. A short length of stiff
  monofilament, lightly wetted, serves the same pulling-tool role for
  threading shrink tube when a latch needle isn't at hand (`U4zifdssSes`,
  `unARAuTgF_A`).
- Looping the cord onto a ring, either poked straight through the ring or
  looped around it first, shows no stated performance difference between the
  two (`unARAuTgF_A`).
- Once doubled, a hollow-core cord is noticeably **stiffer** than it was
  single — factor that into the separate stiffening-tube step above rather
  than stacking both if the doubled cord is already stiff enough on its own
  (`unARAuTgF_A`).

### Connecting the ring/swivel end

Two ways to finish the ring/swivel end of an assist, demonstrated side by
side on the same speed-jig build (`unARAuTgF_A`). A solid ring is the more
traditional approach; a swivel is a comparatively controversial pick
(`unARAuTgF_A`).

- **Solid ring (traditional):** the doubled cord rings to a solid ring at the hook end; the
  hook then joins the leader or main-line swivel with a **separate split
  ring**.
- **Swivel (the more controversial pick)**, for two reasons:
  1. **Room for a dual-top build.** A swivel-ended assist isn't as stiff/
     bulky as two doubled cords crowded onto one solid ring, leaving a
     working gap between the two hook loops of a dual-top assist — enough
     room to still run a split ring off the swivel to the jig. (See the
     dual-top-on-a-swivel hook-count/placement variant logged in
     [knife jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md).)
  2. **A more solid connection under load.** When a fish is fighting the
     jig, the load runs straight onto the swivel-to-main-line connection
     instead of through a split ring; split rings are rated strong enough to
     hold, but a direct swivel-to-line tie is framed as the more solid
     connection. A swivel also **stops line twist** from a rotating, fighting
     fish — cited as the reason more anglers are moving toward swivel-ended
     assists over a plain solid ring (`unARAuTgF_A`).
  - **Tying main line direct to the swivel:** for anglers fishing without a
    separate bite leader, the main line ties straight to the assist's
    swivel — no extra swivel-and-crimp connection needed. Leader-fishing
    anglers gain the same anti-twist/load benefit by tying their leader to
    the swivel instead of to a solid ring.
- **Swivel sizing:** a small #2-class swivel (Owner brand in the demo, low
  confidence — product name) rated to 310 lb is the smallest recommended;
  going smaller risks the swivel's thin wire cutting into heavier line
  classes. A bigger swivel, up to roughly a 1 or 1/0, is also fine — wire
  gauge thickens with swivel size, at the cost of a bulkier connection
  (`unARAuTgF_A`).

### PE line strength

- The 180 lb hollow-core PE cord (see cord weight, above) reads light next
  to the 215–300 lb mono-core cords, but the doubled loop construction means
  the working connection at the ring/swivel is still only rated to that
  single-strand number (180 lb), not doubled to ~360 lb — the fold-over
  doesn't add strength at the connection point (`unARAuTgF_A`).
- The heavier-than-necessary rating on any assist cord isn't chosen for
  breaking strength — an assist cord is never loaded anywhere near even
  180 lb in practice. It's chosen for **abrasion resistance**: a heavier cord
  holds up longer against a fish's teeth/scales chewing on it. Shrink tube
  over the cord (see above) is the other layer of the same bite-protection
  logic (`unARAuTgF_A`).

### Tying steps, paraphrased — single top assist (speed jig)

1. Cut the cord to length (roughly the jig's resting-point distance, about a
   third of the jig's length, plus a little extra to work with) and fold it
   in half to form the loop (`U4zifdssSes`).
2. Thread a length of the smaller stiffening shrink tube onto the cord first
   — for a long or unwieldy cord, threading a stiff mono leader through first
   and using it to pull the tube on can make this easier.
3. Wrap the doubled cord around the hook shank with an overhand-style knot
   that cinches down onto the shank, leaving enough tag to grip.
4. Use split-ring pliers with a notched jaw to pinch the cord tight against
   the shank while the plier's jaw end pulls the tag end taut.
5. Glue the knot (see glue parameter above) and let it set.
6. Trim the tag short, then slide a larger shrink-tube sleeve over the hook
   eye and knot, butted up against the hook, and shrink it in place.

### Tying steps, paraphrased — double tail assist (flat-fall-style jig)

1. Cut a longer piece of the heavier cord, fold in half, and thread it
   through a solid ring using a uni-knot-style wrap (`U4zifdssSes`).
2. Cinch the ring tight against the wraps with the same notched split-ring
   pliers technique as the single assist.
3. Tie each of the two hooks onto its own leg of the doubled cord, working
   from the back of the shank toward the point (see orientation parameter
   above), so the points ride outward rather than crossing.
4. Glue each knot, let it set, then trim tags short.
5. Slide a medium shrink-tube sleeve over each hook from the point down
   toward the eye, tucking the tag under the eye's lip, and shrink in place.

### Tying steps, paraphrased — dual-top assist on a swivel (speed jig)

1. Cut two lengths of hollow-core cord, doubling each back into itself (see
   hollow-core cord type, above) instead of tying a solid-ring knot
   (`unARAuTgF_A`).
2. Tie the first hook onto its cord, keeping the hook eye perpendicular
   (90°) to the swivel eye (see double-hook orientation, above), using the
   same overhand-style wrap around the shank as the single assist.
3. Tie the second hook onto the swivel's other eye the same way, again kept
   perpendicular.
4. Glue each knot, let it set, then trim tags short.
5. Shrink-tube each hook eye/knot connection the same as a single assist. A
   split ring off the swivel's remaining eye connects the finished dual-top
   assist to the jig.

## Stock flat-fall assist hooks wear out — upgrade, and size to the grade of fish

Distinct from the sizing/cord/tying mechanics above: a stock-vs-upgrade
judgment call for the small assist hooks that ship pre-rigged on
[flat-fall-style jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md), not a from-scratch tying
procedure (`fgTmUq78ofQ`).

- **Failure mode:** the small stock assist hooks that ship with most
  flat-fall jigs wear out fast — hook an estimated three or four fish and
  fish are lost after that as the hooks break off. On the boat cited here,
  the crew changes out the stock assist hooks (and, on the [Colt
  Sniper](../lures/tuna-poppers-and-stickbaits.md)-style hard baits, the
  trebles and split rings) on essentially every jig as soon as they get
  them, rather than fishing the stock hardware as-is (`fgTmUq78ofQ`).
- **The fix, as fished:** step up to a bigger hook on a bigger split ring —
  no leader material or extra hardware between hook and jig, the hook rides
  directly on the split ring, which is looped onto the jig's own ring
  (`fgTmUq78ofQ`).
- **A wire-cord assist hook** — Tady brand, product name, low confidence,
  sponsored-claim caveat; the failure-mode reasoning above is the
  transferable parameter — built with 49-strand wire rated to 65 lb test,
  coated in Kevlar, marketed as toothproof because a fish's teeth wear
  through and fray a standard cord/string assist over repeated hookups: a
  wire-core alternative to the mono-core and hollow-core PE cords documented
  above (`fgTmUq78ofQ`).
- **Size the hook to the grade of fish expected, not to the jig's stock
  hardware:** as a bluefin gets bigger, its teeth get bigger. A ~20 lb fish
  has small enough teeth that the stock assist hook is probably fine, and a
  25–40 lb fish on the stock assist hook is "probably okay" too. The real
  risk is a bigger, unspecified-grade fish taken on 80 lb line — there the
  stock assist hook has a genuine chance of losing the fish to a broken
  hook, so the advice is to "hedge your bet" and upgrade regardless of what
  size shows up. The schools fished that week ran mixed-grade, roughly
  30–150 lb, so anglers
  often don't know in advance what size fish will eat the drop — a reason to
  upgrade the hook rather than gamble on the stock hardware holding for
  whatever bites (`fgTmUq78ofQ`).
- Passengers can have the crew swap assist hooks/rings for them aboard the
  boat if they don't want to rig it themselves at the dock (`fgTmUq78ofQ`).

## Hook shape — shank, bend, and point (selection, not tying)

Selecting a hook shape for the assist, as distinct from the sizing/cord/
tying parameters above — general hook mechanics, sourced from East Coast/
Gulf slow-pitch instruction rather than SoCal-species-specific fishing,
unregistered channel, medium confidence (`-nIhadZwxAA`).

- **Shank length trades penetration speed for holding power.** A short
  shank (e.g. a Gamakatsu 510-class hook) penetrates faster with less active
  hookset needed — good for lethargic biters and smaller fish that don't hit
  hard — but leaves less metal between the point and the assist cord, so
  it's a poor pick around toothy fish (sharks, barracuda, kingfish/mackerel)
  that can bite through. A longer shank (e.g. a Gamakatsu 620-class hook)
  needs a more deliberate hookset — reel out the slack, then strike — but
  delivers more hook-setting power and holds up better against teeth. Around
  toothy fish, the fallback is shorter pitches (less fall, less slack/bow in
  the line) rather than switching to wire — see below (`-nIhadZwxAA`).
- **A deep belly (bend) improves hookup ratio and holding.** A hook with a
  deep, rounded bend seats the fish's weight where the bend can carry it once
  the point penetrates; a shallow or wide-angled bend doesn't hold as well
  under sustained load (`-nIhadZwxAA`).
- **Point orientation — straight vs. "tuna-blade" (point curved back toward
  the eye).** A straight point (wide gap) needs a more active hookset and is
  more prone to snagging bottom or fouling on the outside of a fish's face
  rather than finding the mouth. A point curved back toward the eye, on a
  narrow taper — quoted on camera as "like a hypodermic needle rather than a
  2×4" — penetrates with less active hookset, snags less, and, because the
  geometry doesn't tilt under load, drives straight back into the fish's
  face in the direction of the pull once it's set (`-nIhadZwxAA`). The
  preference stack for an assist hook: long shank, deep belly, point curved
  back toward the eye, narrow taper, slightly thicker gauge, and a spade eye
  rather than an eyelet — claimed to catch/set faster off the fish's face on
  the stated but unexplained reasoning of "the balance of the hook"
  (`-nIhadZwxAA`). ⚠ Flagged gap — no corpus source: mechanism for the
  spade-eye catch/set-speed claim.
- **Single vs. double, by jigging style — speed jigging vs. slow-pitch.**
  Speed jigging (the jig ripped through the water column in one direction)
  calls for a single hook on top only, sized to roughly a third of the jig's
  length — fish hit head-first as the jig rips through, so one hook at the
  head covers the strike. Slow-pitch jigging is different: the jig can
  pitch, slide, and fall in either direction, so which end presents as the
  "head" varies pitch to pitch. Two single hooks are run instead, one top
  and one bottom, for that reason — never crossed, the same don't-cross rule
  as [knife jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md). Separately, hookset quality
  differs between single and double hooks: a single hook tends to bury past
  the jaw, deep in the mouth, almost every time; a double/dual-hook rig is
  more likely to catch "in and around" the mouth (e.g. the cheek), which can
  pull free on a long fight (`-nIhadZwxAA`). **This is a different axis
  than, and doesn't resolve,** the hook-count/placement conflict already
  tracked in [knife jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md) (multiple sources running
  various top+bottom, dual-top, and bottom-pair configurations) — logged
  here as an added, single-source rationale for preferring fewer hook points
  where the fishery allows it, not a resolution of that conflict.
- **No wire, on the leader or the assist cord.** No wire is run anywhere in
  the system, on the stated principle that losing an occasional toothy fish
  (shark, barracuda, wahoo) to a bite-off is preferable to fighting/landing
  one on tackle scaled for it — a deliberate trade-off, not an oversight
  (`-nIhadZwxAA`).

## Conflict note

A single-top speed-jig assist and a tail double-flat-fall assist come from
the same tying series (`U4zifdssSes`) — a top-plus-bottom, potentially
three-hook combination when both are run together on the same outfit. A
dual-top assist (two hooks, both at the head, tied to a swivel rather than a
solid ring, `unARAuTgF_A`) adds a third build — still two-hooks-max on its
own, but another top-heavy variant. All of this sits inside the live
hook-count/placement conflict already tracked in [knife
jigs](../lures/knife-jigs.md) (single bottom pair, no top hook vs. several
top+bottom/three-hook configurations) — see that note for the full
side-by-side list; not reconciled here.
