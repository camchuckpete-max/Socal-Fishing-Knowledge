# Leadhead Mods & Rigging

The leadhead (jig head) is a workhorse for seabass, halibut, yellowtail, bass,
and rockfish around Channel Islands squid nests, bays, and nearshore
structure. This note covers modifying a leadhead so big soft baits hook up on
the sink, reinforcing a soft-plastic's nose so it survives more fish, rigging
a leadhead with squid for white seabass, and head selection by current,
weight, and species.

## When to use

- Squid-fished leadheads for [white seabass](../species/white-seabass.md)
  around the islands, and as a general leadhead presentation on squid nests.
- A cast-and-retrieve alternative — and companion rod — to the
  [dropper loop](../techniques/dropper-loop.md) when fish are active in
  current.
- Reinforcing a tube-style soft plastic's nose instead of replacing the bait
  after it tears from repeated [California
  halibut](../species/california-halibut.md) hits.

## Parameters

### Hook-spring mod for head-first bites

On big soft baits — an 8-inch slug, a swimbait, a Gulp grub — fished for bass
and rockfish, fish often eat the bait head-first on the sink, load the rod,
then pull free because a standard jig hook points the wrong way and slides
out backward (`RXb0HvVwqO4`). The fix: cut the hook off the leadhead leaving
a short bar, solder a spring onto that bar (pulled off a weedless swimbait
hook — Trokar, Owner, or similar), then add an assist hook on a split ring.
The soft bait screws onto the spring, which nearly eliminates it pulling off,
and the assist hook takes the fish on a head-bite (`RXb0HvVwqO4`).

Build: clip the shaft before the bend with bolt cutters, drill two small
adjacent holes in the head's base, slide the spring over the shaft, and fix
it with silver solder (epoxy also works); hold the head in vice grips while
heating (`RXb0HvVwqO4`). The mod nets close to 100% hookups on head bites
during the sink and lets one screwed-on bait survive many fish — worth the
build time in 150-200 ft for rockfish, where a torn-off bait otherwise means
a wasted wind-up (`RXb0HvVwqO4`).

### Split-ring nose reinforcement (tube-style soft plastics)

A hollow, open-backed "tube"-style soft plastic threaded onto a leadhead for
[California halibut](../species/california-halibut.md) molds up strong at
the nose, but the thin body wall there tears out after a couple of fish,
ending the bait's life early (`dDvNAZpmx-8`). Work a #2 split ring up inside
the tube's open back, snug against the head/nose — dipping the ring in hot
sauce or Smelly Jelly first helps it slide in — then thread the hook through
both the split ring and the plastic at the nose rather than the plastic
alone (`dDvNAZpmx-8`). Running the hook through the metal ring reinforces the
point that otherwise tears on a plain-hook bite, letting one bait land many
more fish before it wears out (`dDvNAZpmx-8`) — the same durability goal as
the hook-spring mod above and the Basstrix/Sudden Impact framing in
[soft-plastic swimbaits](../lures/soft-plastic-swimbaits.md), reached by a
different mechanism; no source ties the three together, so they stay
separate parameters here.

### Squid rigging for white seabass

Squid is the staple white seabass bait in early spring. Thread it onto the
leadhead like a swimbait — through the top of the mantle and up onto the
head — then pin a second squid through the top of the mantle as well; two
squid (three if small) mimics squid mating and is most effective at night
and gray light, though seabass take it in daylight too (`Xr4nURK-Z48`).

Head weight runs from 5/8 oz up to 3/4 oz on Catalina squid-nest trips;
bright orange leadheads have historically drawn a few more strikes than
white, though both catch fish, and glow-in-the-dark heads suit darker
conditions/evening (`Y2bXn44lfqo`). For a straight, non-twisting swim,
insert the hook on the squid's belly side near the middle, push through and
out the other side, then turn the point back around and push it through
again — pinned this way the squid sits flat and tracks straight instead of
spinning (`Y2bXn44lfqo`).

Fishing the rig actively — holding the rod and feeling for the tap so the
hook sets in the mouth — hooks fewer fish in the gut than a passive
dropper-loop soak strung with an Ahi twist (J-style) hook, a distinction
that matters most when white seabass will be released alive, as in a
broodstock-tagging program (`Y2bXn44lfqo`, `xFS3MW4GpDU`); see [dropper
loop](../techniques/dropper-loop.md) for that rig's own hook conventions and
[white seabass](../species/white-seabass.md#doctrine--conflicts) for the
release-survival context. On the active style, a rapid "tap-tap" is usually
a perch or other small fish chewing or stealing the bait — swinging on it
just pulls the bait off the hook — while a held, building pressure is the
cue for white seabass and yellowtail (`xFS3MW4GpDU`). One build for actively
cast squid-and-leadhead: 50 lb braid mainline to a 30 lb fluorocarbon leader
(the leader connection method is unresolved by ASR) to a 3/8 oz
leadhead, bounced lightly off bottom, then worked with either a slow
reel-back staying just off bottom or a lift of the rod without reeling —
lighter than the 5/8-3/4 oz heads above and the 1/8 oz-1 oz dockside range
below (`xFS3MW4GpDU`).

### Working it mid-column

Fishing this rig mid-column rather than on the bottom is a white seabass
program: the flick-behind-the-boat cast, the current wanted so the bait drifts
back and away from the boat, the thumb-tensioned free-spool sink, working the
column from near the surface down to the holding depth, and the wind-and-pop
that converts a follow into a bite are in [white seabass — lead head and
squid](../species/white-seabass-leadhead-squid.md) (`0PAPH1uqk4A`,
`Eo_bA8IWvyU`).

### Dockside leadhead selection

For seabass, halibut, and yellowtail on squid nests around the Channel
Islands, weight runs 1/8 oz up to 1 oz, heavier for more current and lighter
for less (`F-vOTerdulU`). Pin squid through the top of the mantle and push
it up over the head's retention ridge — one live and one dead, or two dead,
to mimic the spawn (`F-vOTerdulU`). Cast out, let it sink to bottom, then jig
it back slowly (up-down-up-down, like a Texas-rigged worm or bass jig)
through the water column above the nest; this works best with active fish
and current, especially expecting a swim-through — or fish it from the rod
holder in current with a lighter head and let it waft mid-column
(`F-vOTerdulU`).

### Banana head — light-gauge, multi-species squid presentation

A leadhead built with a smaller-gauge hook than a standard jig head, fished
with fresh dead or live squid, for Channel Islands trips out of Ventura or
Oxnard (`PnAnAS6QoG8`, `nRIAgz5G_Bc`). Weight runs 1/2 oz up to 1 oz to cover
different parts of the water column; it takes calico bass, sand bass, white
seabass, and halibut — halibut specifically when let all the way to the
bottom (`PnAnAS6QoG8`).

### Neck Breaker — compact alternative to a sliding sinker

Fishing a sliding egg sinker on braid to a fluorocarbon leader, the sinker
can ride up the mainline and get stuck above the braid-to-fluoro splice — it
can't slide down to the hook the way it would on straight mono/fluoro —
leaving an unintentionally long leader between weight and bait instead of a
compact, weight-near-the-bait presentation (`KUsFT7tN-x0`). See
[hollow-core splice & serving](hollow-splice-and-serving.md) for that
connection and [sliding sinker](../techniques/sliding-sinker.md) for the rig
this replaces. Because the Warbaits Neck Breaker builds the weight into the
head at the hook, there is no separate sinker to migrate up the line and
hang on the splice — squid and weight stay compact all the way to the hook,
and the squid rides upright instead of spinning behind a free-sliding egg
sinker (`KUsFT7tN-x0`). It is fished as the same effective presentation as a
sliding sinker, not a different technique, rigged with an angler's-choice
hook or a large Owner Reaper hook (`KUsFT7tN-x0`).

### War Baits Slayer — rigging a Pickle Kick

For a 3-in Pickle Kick swimbait on a War Baits Slayer open-hook jig head:
buckle the bait against the head's collar before inserting the hook and
sight down it to judge the exit point (by feel each time, or mark it once
with a small pinch in the plastic), push the brush guard aside, start the
hook point straight down the center of the nose, and feed it through slowly
and straight to the marked exit point; pop the hook out there and feed the
rest of the bait up the shank snug against the head's face (`g5yDzjORhho`).
A straight, unbowed, natural-tracking bait draws more bites (`g5yDzjORhho`).
