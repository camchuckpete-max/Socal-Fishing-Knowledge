# Dropper-Loop Knot & Spider Hitch — Tying the Loop

Two named loop-forming knots used to build the hook loop (or, reversed, a
sliding sinker attachment) of a classic [dropper loop](../techniques/dropper-loop.md)
rig — that note owns the rig, sinker weights, and species application; this
note holds the tying mechanics and the presenter's stated reason for reaching
for one knot over the other. Split out as its own note rather than folded
into [essential SoCal knots](essential-knots.md), which had already grown
past ~400 lines, and distinct from the surgeon's-loop family covered in
[Cut-Loop Surgeon's Loop](cut-loop-dropper.md) — a different loop-forming
knot for the same rig (Tackle Express, "Harry," `SK2M7L2vA1s`, 2024-12-06;
unregistered channel — medium confidence).

## When to use it

- Forms the loop(s) for a [dropper loop](../techniques/dropper-loop.md) rig —
  either the hook loop(s) above a fixed sinker, or, reversed, a loop the
  sinker itself slides on.
- **Dropper loop knot** — this presenter's default for **rockfish** (doubled
  up for two hooks on one leader) and for **yellowtail down deep**.
- **Spider hitch** — fished the same way, on the same rig, but the
  presenter's own preference over the plain dropper loop knot for
  **hard-pulling, structure-oriented game fish** — yellowtail, sea bass — for
  a stated mechanical reason; see Parameters below.

## Parameters & judgment

- **Dropper loop knot — tying it:** decide how far you want the sinker from
  the hook, then tie a loop in the leader sized to taste ("as large or as
  small as you'd like"). Grab the two strands just above the loop, where they
  separate, and twist them **8 times**. Open the small loop that forms at the
  twist, reach through, grab the line at the bottom of the loop, and cinch it
  down. Repeat lower on the same leader for a second loop/hook — a double
  dropper, popular for rockfish so two baits fish at once.
- **Applications named on camera:**
  - **Rockfish** — double up two loops/hooks on the same leader.
  - **Yellowtail down deep** — a **12–16 oz** sinker on the bottom, leader
    coming up **3–6 ft**, tie the loop there and drop a sardine or squid.
    Sits within the wider SoCal sinker-weight range already logged in
    [dropper loop](../techniques/dropper-loop.md#building-the-rig) — 6–10 oz
    is the general SoCal reference there, with several heavier variants up to
    16–24 oz for deeper or heavier builds; this 12–16 oz figure is a
    corroborating data point in that heavier band, not reconciled to one
    number.
  - **Halibut, rig reversed:** instead of sinker-at-bottom-with-a-loop-above,
    tie the hook at the very end of the leader, then tie the same dropper
    loop knot coming up the line and attach the sinker to that loop so it
    **slides** at the bottom, above the hook. Framed as effective for halibut
    specifically because they're a flatfish that buries in the sand — the
    sliding sinker dragging over bottom "bumps into" a buried fish. **Not
    fully resolved:** the transcript doesn't make clear whether the sinker is
    tied fixed into the loop or genuinely free-sliding on it — described only
    as going "to slide on the bottom," read here as riding loose rather than
    fixed. See [sliding sinker](../techniques/sliding-sinker.md) for the
    general fixed-vs-sliding-sinker rig family this sits beside.
- **Spider hitch — tying it:** pull out **1.5 to 2 ft** of leader (how far you
  want the sinker from the hook), form a loop sized to taste, tie a simple
  overhand knot in it, then take the loop and pass it through the overhand
  **3 times**. Lubricate, separate the small loop, hold both the leader and
  main line taut through that loop, and cinch down.
  - **Fishing it:** the presenter uses only **one loop** in this build —
    torpedo sinker at the bottom, hook above it — for halibut or yellowtail.
  - **Stated reason for preferring it over the dropper loop knot on
    hard-pulling fish:** cinching the spider hitch down pulls from the loop
    itself — the same point the hook or lure is attached to — so a
    hard-pulling fish just cinches the knot tighter under load. The plain
    dropper loop knot, by contrast, is loaded from the two legs on either
    side of the loop, which the presenter says can pull the loop apart on a
    big, hard-pulling fish. This is the stated reason he defaults to the
    spider hitch for **yellowtail and sea bass** specifically, reserving the
    dropper loop knot above for rockfish.
