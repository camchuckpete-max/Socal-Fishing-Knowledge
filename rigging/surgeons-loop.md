# Surgeon's Loop (Giant Yellowtail, Long-Range)

A large loop tied into doubled heavy mono/leader material at the end of the
main line, sized to give a long-range angler a stable connection point that
different terminal knots and hooks can be tied onto as conditions and bait
change through a multi-day trip. Presented by SearcherSportfishing (the
long-range boat **Searcher**, out of San Diego) as a "very popular" tie for
**giant yellowtail on 6-to-8-day trips down south** (`2O4Z0S78KLg`,
2024-05-11; unregistered channel, single mention — `medium` confidence).
Mechanically the same doubled-overhand family as the [Surgeon's knot
(loop)](essential-knots.md) already documented for a small single-hook
rockfish dropper loop — this entry covers the larger, heavier, giant-
yellowtail-scale build specifically, rather than folding it into that
already-long note.

**Corroborating, earlier segment in the same series (Steve Carson, Penn
Fishing Tackle, via SearcherSportfishing, "Tackle Tip Thursday Vol. 230
(Dropper Loop for Yellowtail)," `CdjT_I_PBHQ`, 2024-04-11; unregistered
channel — medium confidence):** the same loop, named on camera as **"a four
turn surgeons knot,"** framed the same way — a rig for **anglers on a
6-day-or-longer Searcher trip, much further down the Baja Peninsula**, where
the yellowtail run **30, 40, 50, 60 lb**, bigger than anywhere near home.
Fished **day or night** — big yellowtail are called out specifically as
night feeders. This is the rig this note's loop is tied into; the sections
below fill in the sinker, hook, and hook-attachment detail that video
`2O4Z0S78KLg` leaves as "chosen per conditions and bait."

## When to use it

- Giant-grade yellowtail on long-range (6–8 day) trips fishing down south —
  the presenter is explicit that standard, lighter line is "just too light"
  for these fish: "these fish are too big and mean."
- The finished loop is left as a general-purpose attachment point: "you can
  use a variety of different knots and hooks to attach here based on the day
  fishing conditions and the type of bait that you have" — the loop itself is
  not tied to one specific terminal rig.

## Parameters & judgment

- **Line class:** start with **80 lb or even 100 lb test**.
- **Loop sizing while tying:** make the loop "a little bit bigger than what
  you think you're going to need" before cinching — it draws down smaller
  than it looks mid-tie.
- **Build:** double roughly **6 in** of line, tie a simple overhand ("granny
  knot") in the doubled section, then pass the loop through itself **3 to 4
  times** (the presenter's own count wavers between three and four passes on
  camera).
- **Mid-tie check:** before cinching, give the doubled strand a light tug —
  a small **figure-eight shape** should appear in the knot. If it doesn't
  show, something went wrong; start over rather than cinch down a bad tie.
- **Cinching:** hold both legs of the doubled strand firmly (the presenter
  wraps them around his fingers) and pull hard — but not a jerk — to seat
  the knot. Heavy lubrication is required to seat it fully; the presenter
  uses spit, held in his mouth ("you need lots of spit... don't let your
  tongue get caught in the loop, it hurts a lot, ask me how I know"). If the
  knot won't tighten all the way, the stated reason is not enough
  lubrication — re-wet and continue rather than force it dry.
- **Finished size:** a loop a little over about **2 ft** long — the size
  described as what these fish "like" on the Searcher. **Corroborated**
  (`CdjT_I_PBHQ`): the Vol. 230 segment gives the same loop as **about 2 to
  2½ ft**, tied with the same "four turn surgeons knot" — a second video in
  the same channel/series, so treated as corroboration of the same house
  figure rather than an independent confirmation.
- **Reel/line pairing (`CdjT_I_PBHQ`):** a **Penn Fathom two-speed model 80**
  conventional, topshot **80 or 100 lb test — nothing less than 80, 100 is
  better** ("go with what you have"); full reel/line writeup in [rod & reel
  selection](../tackle/rod-and-reel-selection.md#long-range-yellowtail-rodlineleader--tackle-tip-thursday-darren-searchersportfishing).
- **After the loop:** terminal knots/hooks are chosen and tied onto this loop
  per that day's conditions and bait — not a fixed pairing. See [essential
  SoCal knots](essential-knots.md) for terminal-knot options and [hooks —
  style, size, and matching to bait](../tackle/hooks.md) for hook selection.
  The three ways `CdjT_I_PBHQ` gives for making that attachment are below.

## Sinker & hook (`CdjT_I_PBHQ`)

The rig is a straight bottom bait rig off this loop: sinker at the very
bottom, bait/hook riding on the loop above it.

- **Sinker:** the crew's minimum is **16 oz**; step up to **24 oz or more**
  if the bait is a little bigger or there's more wind or current — the crew
  on board advises the exact weight per the day. The same **16 oz / 24 oz**
  figures appear in [dropper loop — Baja, Sea of Cortez reference
  weights](../techniques/dropper-loop.md#building-the-rig) from a different
  source (Ray Sharifi, Dirty Hookers) and a different fishery (Cortez
  cabrilla/grouper, not long-range Pacific-side yellowtail) — kept side by
  side as a cross-fishery match on the numbers, not merged as one doctrine.
- **Hook:** explicitly **not a small live-bait hook** — a big hook the
  presenter calls **"a 60 J hook,"** sized up to an **"80 or a 90"** for
  bigger bait. Read as **6/0, up to 8/0 or 9/0** — standard SoCal/long-range
  hook-size notation (see [hooks — size, bait first, fish
  second](../tackle/hooks.md#size--bait-first-fish-second)), spoken as
  "six-oh," "eight-oh," "nine-oh" and rendered by auto-captions without the
  slash; the presenter's own hedge ("I believe is a...") shows he wasn't
  fully sure on camera either.
  **Flagged `asr-uncertain`** — preserved here as both the literal caption
  reading and the corrected reading rather than silently converted.

## Attaching the hook to the loop — three ways (`CdjT_I_PBHQ`)

Which of these three the crew uses is "really just what your favorite is" —
not ranked, except the finesse variant is called out as situational.

1. **Hook inside the loop.** If you plan ahead, thread the hook onto the
   doubled line *before* cinching the surgeon's-knot loop, so the hook rides
   captured inside the finished loop. "A lot of people like that."
2. **Standard knot to the closed loop.** If you forgot to load the hook
   before tying, tie it on afterward with a **four-turn Uni knot** — or
   "really whatever your favorite knot is."
3. **Paternoster (single-leg) variant — for a finickier bite.** Auto-captions
   render the presenter's name for this as **"pattern noer"** — read here as
   **paternoster**, the established rigging term for this exact single-branch
   construction; flagged `asr-uncertain` since the reading is inferred from
   phonetics and mechanism, not a name the corpus has spelled out elsewhere.
   Clip one leg of the finished loop short, leaving the other leg long; tie
   the hook to the end of that long single leg with your preferred knot.
   This converts the terminal presentation from a **doubled line to a
   single, longer line**, so the bait can **swim around and be more
   active** — described on camera as "a little bit more finesse" despite
   still being fished on the 80–100 lb main line. Reach for this
   specifically when **a fish is a little bit finicky** and might be easier
   to fool on the more natural presentation, while keeping the same heavy
   line class for the fight.

Once rigged, the bait is dropped to the bottom and the reel left **in
gear** — not a finesse hookset technique, the presenter is explicit ("this
is not a light tackle technique... not a finesse technique") outside of the
paternoster variant's bait presentation.
